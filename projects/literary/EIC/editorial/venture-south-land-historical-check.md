# The Venture — `The South Land` Historical Check

Status: research control for the natural-opening revision. Checked 2026-08-08.

## Documented Batavia outcomes

The historical record is unusually specific.

- `Batavia` wrecked on Morning Reef in the Houtman Abrolhos on 4 June 1629.
- Pelsaert and others reached Batavia by longboat and returned in the rescue vessel `Sardam`.
- Wiebbe Hayes's group reached Pelsaert first on the rescue ship's arrival and warned him of the mutiny.
- Pelsaert and the ship's council tried the principal mutineers on the islands.
- Jeronimus Cornelisz and six other mutineers were executed there: seven hangings in total.
- **Wouter Loos and Jan Pelgrom de Bye** were the two men formally sentenced to be put ashore on the mainland of the South Land.
- Pelsaert's surviving written instruction explicitly names those two and provides them with a small boat, equipment and goods intended for contact with local people.
- Other accused men were transported back to Batavia for further punishment.

Sources:
- Western Australian Museum, `Batavia's history, 1628–1963`.
- Australian Government heritage listing, `Batavia Shipwreck Site and Survivor Camps Area 1629`.
- Francisco Pelsaert's 1629 journal, Western Australian Museum edition, including the instruction for Wouter Loos and Jan Pelgrom.

## Conflict in the current Book One material

The current reference material gives fictional Silas Rook an additional formal marooning on the South Land coast, separate from Loos and Pelgrom.

That conflicts with a well-preserved and named historical sentencing record. Because the novel's stated contract is that the principal people are invented but the history is real, the extra formal marooning should be retired.

## Revised Rook outcome

Recommended and adopted for the editorial proposal:

1. Rook sails on `Batavia` despite Maria Mori's written warning.
2. He participates in the mutineers' coercive order after the wreck, but the surviving testimony against him is incomplete enough that he is **not substituted for any of the seven historically executed men**.
3. Maria Mori does **not** travel on `Sardam` or insert herself into Pelsaert's island tribunal. That was dramatically convenient but historically conspicuous.
4. Pelsaert's own tribunal proceeds with its documented outcomes.
5. Rook is among the remaining prisoners returned to Batavia for further judgment.
6. In Batavia, Maria's pre-sailing warning and the factor's annotations become relevant to Rook's further examination. Rook states, correctly and self-servingly, that the VOC employed him after being warned.
7. Maria's aim becomes getting that fact into the record, not selecting a punishment.
8. Rook's ultimate fate is left **unresolved in Book One** after he is remanded/removed for further disposition. This preserves room for later legend without rewriting the documented 1629 mainland sentences.

## Character consequence

This is stronger for Maria Mori.

She no longer appears to possess implausible access to the rescue expedition and tribunal. Her power is the power she has actually built over twenty-five years: languages, duplicates, professional standing, memory and the ability to make a document difficult to bury.

Her development from 1603 is therefore continuous:

- in the Pelican, she cannot make herself understood;
- in Batavia, she can make herself understood precisely;
- neither condition gives her complete control over what institutions do next.

## Rook guardrail

Do not turn Rook into the secret cause of the Batavia massacre. Cornelisz and the documented mutiny remain the historical centre. Rook is one fictional violent man inside that catastrophe, not the man history forgot to credit for it.
