# Tom's Seven Unsent Letters — Revised Chronology

## Governing rule

Tom writes seven letters to Maggie between 1616 and 1625. He sends none.

The letters are not a correspondence. Maggie receives no letter from Tom during the fourteen-year rupture. Their function is private: Tom keeps trying to turn experience into something he could place before her, then recognising that sending it would also be a demand for judgement, forgiveness or return.

The eighth letter is Daniel Vale himself, walked through the Pelican door in 1626.

Only fragments of the seven should ever appear in the finished novel. The reader should know the bundle exists more often than the reader is permitted to read it.

---

## I. Plymouth, 1616 — the first letter

Context: Tom has returned from Virginia on the 1616 voyage. He has watched the settlement, the emerging tobacco economy, the Virginia Company publicity around Rebecca Rolfe/Pocahontas, and Uttamatomakkin's attempt to count the English. He remains barred from Maggie by the 1612 rupture.

Content already established in the revised `The Same Ink`:

- fog on the Banks;
- a shark compared to Josiah Grimes;
- the immense Virginia trees;
- the smell of England before sight of land;
- questions about the Pelican roof, Hendricks and the old sign;
- nothing about the settlement's mortality, the bound settler, the two names on one line or the tally stick.

Tom reads the page and recognises that it sounds like a man trying to come through a locked door as a story.

**Disposition:** folded; not sent. Kept in his sea chest.

This is the first letter because it is the first time since 1612 that Tom gets as far as writing Maggie's name.

---

## II. Surat, 1618 — the useful letter

Context: Tom has taken the East India Company berth after Gravesend and is settling into Surat work. His Virginia proposal for two independent tallies has finally found a place where it is useful.

Core movement:

Tom writes that a warehouse count nearly placed blame on a porter for a bale that was never missing. He lays the landing tally against the store tally, finds the error and has the accusation withdrawn.

He begins the letter pleased with the result.

Halfway through, he notices the shape of what he is writing: a boy holding up repaired work for Maggie to mark.

He does not pretend the repair makes the system good. He also cannot pretend the porter would have been safer if Tom had refused the work entirely.

**Disposition:** kept face down beneath the Plymouth letter.

The important development is that Tom no longer asks whether he should work inside the system. He asks what his work does to another person.

---

## III. Surat, 1620 — the food letter

Context: newly arrived passage-boys are assessed for work before they are fed. Tom stops the assessment and has them fed first.

The phrase `Food first` has travelled east inside him without his consciously preserving it.

He writes Maggie about the boys and almost writes that she would approve.

He crosses out the sentence.

That crossing-out matters more than the anecdote. He recognises that imagined approval is another way of making her participate in his defence without consent.

**Disposition:** folded and retained.

This letter is short.

---

## IV. Surat, 1621 — the day ashore

Context: Tom is now extremely competent and increasingly uncomfortable with how well Company work fits him. Given rare shore leave, he cannot rest; he repairs an awning rope that is not his, sits where he can see both exits and returns early to work.

He writes the most personal of the early letters without mentioning Maggie herself very often.

The centre is simple: he no longer knows what a day ashore is for.

He writes that men call him one of the best boatswains east of the Cape and that the praise lands strangely because the work has become easier to understand than the man doing it.

Do not use the phrase `the machine found my shape` in the final prose. The image has been overused elsewhere. Let the behaviour carry it.

**Disposition:** retained.

---

## V. Surat, 1622 — `You were right`

This remains the burned letter.

Tom writes:

`Maggie —`

`You were right.`

He then attempts to explain what he means: Jack, recruitment, papers, the difference between helping one boy and justifying the taking of boys generally.

While reading it back he understands that the letter is constructed as an apology with a concealed request beneath it: *I now admit you were right, therefore permit me to come back.*

He burns it because the words are true and the use he is making of them is not clean.

**Disposition:** burned completely.

The bundle therefore contains six physical letters by 1625, not seven.

---

## VI. Surat, 1623 — Amboyna

Context: accounts of Amboyna reach Surat. Tom keeps names, including blank places for the Japanese men whose names the English material does not yet give him. He learns that one English factor, Daniel Vale, survived and was transferred to Batavia.

This letter changes direction.

The earlier letters look backward toward Maggie. This one begins looking toward another person.

Tom writes that there is a living man reduced to an entry and that he means to find out why the entry remains open.

He does not say that rescuing Daniel will prove anything about himself.

That omission is progress.

**Disposition:** carried separately for a time with the names; later returned to the bundle.

---

## VII. Batavia, 1625 — unsigned

Context: Tom and Maria Mori have manoeuvred Daniel Vale out of Dutch custody. Maria tells Tom that there is a house in London where accounts are read carefully. She does not know the full personal history of the door she is sending him toward.

Tom writes the final letter.

He describes Daniel only enough to make the practical point: alive, damaged, able to travel, needs food before questions.

He writes that he finally understands he cannot send Maggie an argument that he has changed. The only thing available is to bring home a consequence of what he now chooses to do and let her make no promise in return.

The final sentence should retain the existing idea:

`I remembered before I knew what remembering required.`

He does not sign it.

**Disposition:** folded once; carried home in his coat; not sent.

---

# The eighth letter — 1626

Daniel Vale walks through the Pelican door beside Tom.

No written letter is handed to Maggie.

Tom does not explain the bundle.

Maggie feeds Daniel.

She feeds Tom.

The pint comes later.

This is the only answer the fourteen-year rupture receives.

---

# Continuity controls

1. **No letter is sent.** Any prose saying Tom writes *and sends* letters to Maggie after 1612 must be revised.
2. Maggie does not secretly receive, read or retain the letters.
3. Tom may write from ports, but writing is not correspondence.
4. The first letter is now Plymouth 1616, not Surat 1615, because the Virginia chapter occupies 1614–1617.
5. The fifth letter is still `You were right` and is still burned.
6. The seventh is still unsigned and travels home with Tom.
7. Daniel remains the eighth letter.
8. The word `love` need not appear in any letter. The relationship is clearer without it.
9. Do not print all seven letters in sequence in the finished book. Their absence and concealment are part of their function.
10. Maria Mori's role in 1625 is practical and exact: she identifies the Pelican as a place that can receive Daniel. She does not become a messenger between Tom and Maggie.
