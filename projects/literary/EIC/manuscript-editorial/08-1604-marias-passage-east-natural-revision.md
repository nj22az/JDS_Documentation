# 1603–1612: Maria's Passage East

> **1612**
>
> *I write the rate.*
>
> — Maria Mori, Bantam

## I. Amsterdam

Amsterdam receives Maria in rain.

The customs clerk reads the manifest from Gravesend.

*Household goods, one lot, consigned.*

He looks at the timber.

Then at Maria inside Pieter's coat.

Then at Joos de Vries.

This is a woman.

Yes.

The clerk waits for the rest of the explanation.

Joos gives him none.

The clerk draws a line through *household goods* and writes another word above it.

Maria cannot read the word.

She dislikes that more than the clerk.

What did he write?

Joos translates.

Servant.

Whose?

Mine, for the length of this quay if it keeps him from asking a fourth question.

Maria looks at the clerk.

He has already moved to the next entry.

You could have told me.

Yes.

Why did you not?

Because I had three breaths to choose between offending you and keeping you out of a customs room.

Maria considers this.

Next time, she says, offend me with warning.

Joos inclines his head.

Agreed.

They walk through a city built against water and apparently winning by argument rather than force.

Canals hold the sky. Houses lean over them. Cargo enters upper windows by beam and hook. Numbers are chalked on casks, doors, walls and boards carried by boys moving faster than the men who own what the boards describe.

Maria watches everything.

Not because she has already discovered a philosophy of paper.

Because yesterday a clerk wrote her into a category she could not read.

She intends not to repeat that ignorance.

Joos takes her to the East India Company's rooms.

The fleet is preparing to sail.

The question becomes where to put a woman in a system built almost entirely around men.

A clerk has lists.

Sailors.

Soldiers.

Merchants.

Wives appear beneath husbands. Boys beneath officers. Servants beneath households.

Maria stands beside the desk while Joos explains.

There is one possible place.

A surgeon's widow is travelling to Bantam with her young son. Her brother is already in the Indies. The widow may take a servant if Joos guarantees the cost.

Maria listens.

What will the page say?

Household servant to Lysbeth van Aalst.

After Bantam?

The service ends.

Written?

Joos looks at her.

You learned quickly.

Written?

Yes.

Maria gives three conditions.

Her full name is entered.

No wage advance is to be taken on her behalf.

Pieter's packet remains hers.

The clerk refuses all three in under a minute.

A servant needs one name.

There is no wage, therefore no need to mark no advance.

Private papers are not the Company's concern.

Maria waits until he has finished.

Then points at Joos.

He is guaranteeing me?

Joos translates the answer.

Yes.

Then he is guaranteeing a person with a name, no debt and one packet.

The clerk looks at her although he does not understand the Portuguese.

Joos translates that too.

The argument lasts longer than the refusal.

Joos puts money behind it.

Bond, not purchase. If Maria deserts before Bantam, damages Company property or exceeds the stores allowed to the widow's household, the Chamber may recover from him.

The clerk names a sum large enough to punish principle.

Joos agrees.

That changes the clerk's mood.

Money makes unusual grammar possible.

He writes:

*Maria Mori.*

Beneath Lysbeth van Aalst.

He draws a hard line through the advance column.

Under effects he notes one packet of private papers.

Then he turns the book for Joos to witness.

Maria stops his hand.

Read it.

The clerk says something sharp in Dutch.

Maria looks at Joos.

All of it.

Joos reads every line aloud in Portuguese.

Maria listens until the spoken entry and the page agree.

Only then does she let him sign.

That night she copies the letters of her name from the clerk's page onto a scrap Joos gives her.

M A R I A.

M O R I.

Again.

Again.

Until she can make the shapes without looking.

She does not yet know enough Dutch to read the sentence around them.

That can be corrected.

* * *

## II. Lysbeth and Willem

Lysbeth van Aalst is thirty and tired of men explaining ships to her.

Maria likes her quickly and does not show it.

The widow's son Willem is seven.

He has concluded from his father's death that somebody should be in command and volunteered himself.

Have you cared for children? Lysbeth asks in slow Portuguese learned through her husband's work.

No.

Can you keep one alive?

Maria looks at Willem.

Depends what tries to kill him.

Lysbeth laughs.

Willem does not.

What will try?

Everything on a ship, his mother says.

That answer satisfies him more.

They travel north to the Texel roads with the final stores.

The fleet lies beyond the shallows, masts crossing the grey horizon.

Joos brings Maria aboard.

Before leaving he gives her a slate, chalk, waste paper and a pencil.

Dutch, he says.

Maria looks at the paper.

And numbers.

You already have numbers.

Different numbers have different words around them.

Joos considers this and gives her another pencil.

What do I owe you? she asks.

Nothing.

No.

Joos sighs.

You are very difficult to help.

Help that cannot name its price is difficult to trust.

He rests both hands on the rail.

Learn what Pieter wrote. If there is a man in the book who should answer for something, send me the name. If there is only fever, send nothing.

That is the price?

Yes.

Maria nods.

Now the account is understandable.

Joos winces at the word but accepts it.

The boat takes him ashore.

On the first page of the waste book Maria writes the Dutch words she knows.

*Hout.*

*Touw.*

*Water.*

*Thuis.*

Wood.

Rope.

Water.

Home.

Willem peers over her arm.

Your letters are ugly.

Your Portuguese is worse.

I do not speak Portuguese.

Exactly.

This begins their education.

* * *

## III. Five Measures

The voyage teaches Dutch faster than Amsterdam could.

A ship does not permit vocabulary to remain decorative.

Cup.

Bucket.

Hold.

Wait.

Move.

Sick.

Water.

No.

Again.

Willem supplies nouns. Lysbeth supplies verbs. Sailors contribute weather and obscenity in proportions Maria cannot always distinguish.

Numbers arrive first.

Every morning the widow's household receives provisions.

Three people.

Three shares.

Water morning and evening.

The rationer's assistant marks six measures each day on his slate.

Maria sees five poured.

The first day she assumes she missed one.

The second day she counts more carefully.

Five.

The third day she watches the dipper from cask to cup.

Five.

She says nothing.

On the fifth morning of contrary wind Willem wakes her before light.

He has wet the bed.

He is seven and furious with himself.

Do not tell Mother.

Lysbeth says from above them, His mother knows.

There is nowhere aboard a ship to hide wet wool.

They take the blanket on deck.

Sea water removes what can be seen and leaves salt stiff enough to scour a child's skin.

Lysbeth pours one measure of their fresh water into the rinse bucket.

The under-merchant sees it.

Drinking allowance is not washing allowance.

Lysbeth answers in Dutch.

Maria catches only half.

The man points at Maria and says Joos's name.

The bond.

Stores beyond allowance.

Charge at Bantam.

Willem goes white.

Maria holds up one finger.

One.

The under-merchant looks at her.

She takes her slate.

Three people.

Six marks.

Then five.

She points at yesterday on the assistant's slate.

Six marks.

She points at the cask.

Five pours.

The day before.

Six marks.

Five pours.

Again.

Again.

The assistant begins explaining loss in the dipper, settling, spillage.

Maria does not have the Dutch to answer the explanations.

She does not need it.

She draws four rows of six.

Then four rows of five.

Points at the difference.

Then the rinse bucket.

Ours, she says in Dutch.

The under-merchant studies the slate.

His anger changes direction.

The assistant says something about the cask.

Maria points at the cask and mimes drinking.

Willem laughs first.

Then two sailors.

The under-merchant does not.

He takes the assistant's slate.

The household is not charged for the water.

The ration issue changes the next morning.

Six pours.

Maria watches all six.

She does not celebrate.

She adds the Dutch word for *measure* to her paper.

* * *

## IV. Pieter's Book

Pieter's packet stays beneath Maria's head until she can read enough to open it without borrowing another person's meaning.

That takes months.

The early pages are ordinary sailor pages.

Wages.

Weather.

Food.

A woman in the Texel roads named Els whom Pieter intended to marry every time the ship was within three miles of her and forgot every time it sailed.

Then the Indies.

The hand changes before the words do.

Lines climb.

Dates vanish.

Names appear twice.

A fever later, but not only fever.

Pieter writes about a store dispute in which one version of a tally disappears after a factor changes his mind.

Beside it, in a shakier hand:

*Make another.*

Maria reads the phrase three times.

Not because it is profound.

Because it is practical.

If one paper can disappear, two require more work.

On the next disputed ration issue she makes a second slate before surrendering the first.

Nothing happens.

That is the point.

She begins making copies before she understands that she has begun a rule.

* * *

## V. Bantam

Bantam reaches them first by smell.

Pepper, smoke, drains, fruit, sweat and wet earth heated past anything Europe permits.

The harbour is crowded with vessels that make the Dutch fleet look less like an arrival than an addition.

Maria goes ashore beneath Lysbeth's household entry.

The paper ends at Bantam.

The people who administer it disagree.

A factor says Maria must remain in the Dutch lodge until her status is clarified.

Joos's bond names Bantam as the end of service.

The factor agrees.

The Company nevertheless remains responsible for women landed under its protection.

Maria asks how long.

Until arrangements are satisfactory.

To whom?

The factor looks at her.

What?

Satisfactory to whom?

He answers with a sentence containing the Company several times.

Maria has enough Dutch now to understand every occurrence.

She stays.

For the moment.

On her ninth day a pepper broker arrives furious over twelve sacks.

The Dutch warehouse says twelve were received.

The broker says eleven were delivered and one rejected wet.

Both have marks.

The argument has already lasted half a morning when somebody remembers the Portuguese-speaking woman in the lodge.

Maria translates.

That alone does not solve anything.

Then she asks to see the marks.

The broker's tally has eleven cuts and one cross.

The warehouse copy has twelve cuts.

Which sack was wet? she asks.

The broker points to the cross.

Where is it?

Back in his godown.

Maria asks to see it.

The Dutch clerk says the journey is unnecessary.

The broker laughs.

Maria looks at the clerk.

If the sack is there, you received eleven.

If it is not?

Then he has sold you the same wet pepper twice.

That interests both men.

They go.

The sack is there.

Still wet.

Eleven sacks are paid.

The twelfth is struck from the Dutch receipt.

The broker, Iskandar, offers Maria coin.

The factor says she may not accept private fees while housed under Company protection.

Maria asks the factor what the Company pays her for translating the dispute.

Nothing. Meals and lodging are already provided.

Maria looks at Iskandar.

Meals, she says. And a bolt for my door.

The factor frowns.

Iskandar agrees immediately.

And my name beneath the translation.

The factor begins to object.

Maria waits.

Iskandar says, Put her name. I want to know whom to find next time your numbers become twelve.

The factor looks at two merchants who both now want the same thing and yields because commerce dislikes arguments that do not alter price.

Maria signs:

*Maria Mori.*

Below the Dutch clerk's hand.

That evening she makes another copy of the corrected receipt for Iskandar.

He asks why.

In case one becomes lost.

He looks at her.

Do Dutch papers often become lost?

Only the important ones.

Iskandar laughs.

It is the beginning of work.

* * *

## VI. Day Thirty-One

The bolt arrives.

The factor dislikes it.

He dislikes more that Maria has begun earning meals outside the lodge without asking permission.

On the thirty-first day he calls her into his room.

The Company's protection cannot be indefinite.

Agreed.

A woman alone cannot simply hire rooms in Bantam and conduct trade.

Why?

The factor has several answers. None survive being spoken plainly.

Because arrangements require standing.

Whose standing?

Yours.

How do I get it?

That is not how standing works.

Maria considers this.

Then how does rent work?

The factor blinks.

Rent?

For a room.

He laughs once.

Maria does not.

By sunset she has rented a small upper room from a Gujarati merchant who cares considerably more about six months paid in advance than about Dutch ideas of female standing.

The room contains a mat, a shutter and a table that rocks.

Maria sleeps on the mat.

The next morning she works at the table.

Protection has ended.

Nothing dramatic happens.

That is the freedom of it.

* * *

## VII. What Money Buys

Work arrives in pieces.

A Portuguese captain needs a Dutch letter understood.

A Malay broker needs a Portuguese answer written.

A Dutch clerk needs to know whether a Cantonese phrase means *paid* or *promised*.

Maria knows the difference and charges extra for being asked after the contract is already signed.

She begins setting rates.

Not high at first.

High enough that the person asking must decide whether the translation matters.

That decision filters better than politeness.

A captured Portuguese dispatch comes to her one monsoon season.

The Dutch know it concerns a ship.

They want the route.

Maria translates it.

The route is clear.

The Dutch sail.

Weeks later word comes that the Portuguese ship was taken.

A Portuguese merchant who knows Maria's name finds her room.

You killed those men.

No.

You gave the Dutch the route.

Yes.

For money.

Yes.

He waits for shame.

Maria has some.

She does not give it to him because he arrived demanding ownership of it.

Why? he asks.

Because I wanted what the money would buy.

What did it buy?

Maria looks at the table.

Not this one.

The merchant does not understand.

Neither does Maria, entirely, until the teak desk arrives two months later.

* * *

## VIII. The Desk

The carpenter thinks she wants carving.

She does not.

No flowers.

No saints.

No animals.

Thick legs. Wide top. Drawers that lock. Heavy enough that two men complain when they carry it upstairs.

Maria puts both palms on the teak.

The desk does not move.

Good.

She pays the carpenter.

No debt.

No patron's mark.

No Company inventory number.

The next morning Iskandar arrives with three pepper contracts and stops in the doorway.

New fortress?

Desk.

Same thing, for you.

Maria sits.

He puts the contracts down.

What is the rate?

She tells him.

He complains.

She waits.

He pays.

* * *

## IX. Two Versions

Years teach Maria what one voyage could not.

A translation can be exact and still be used dishonestly.

A duplicate can protect a person or trap one twice.

A name on a page is better than no name and not the same thing as safety.

She learns this through mistakes expensive enough to remember.

So she makes practical rules.

Originals do not sleep where she does.

Copies go by different routes.

Families are paid before intermediaries when the money is meant for families.

A name is not sold with the information attached to it unless the person agrees or is dead and the dead person's name is the point.

She breaks one of these rules when there is no clean choice.

Then she records that too.

The work grows.

Aminah at Amboyna receives a second desk.

A Makassar bookkeeper sends ship movements folded inside spice accounts.

A widow in Patani knows which captains leave debts ashore.

Iskandar hears what merchants say when they believe brokers are furniture.

Maria buys information.

She sells some.

She withholds more than clients know exists.

Nobody who works for her is called a spy to his or her face.

The word makes men imagine romance and forget payment dates.

By 1612 Dutch factors wait outside her room instead of sending boys to summon her.

She wears dark silk because it travels well and because nobody can call it a servant's cast-off without sounding foolish.

The teak desk has acquired scratches.

That pleases her.

A young clerk brings two versions of a shipping agreement.

Dutch on one side.

Portuguese on the other.

They do not agree.

He says the Dutch should govern because the Dutch Company is paying.

Maria reads both.

Then pushes them back.

No.

The clerk looks offended.

You are employed to translate.

Yes.

Then translate the Dutch into Portuguese.

Which Dutch?

He looks down.

There is only one.

Maria points at two clauses whose numbers contradict the corresponding Portuguese provisions.

There are already two.

The clerk begins explaining which version his superior intends.

Maria lets him finish.

Then says, Bring me the intended agreement after your superior has decided what it is.

He stares at her.

The Company sets the terms.

Maria rests both hands on the desk.

The Company sets its terms.

She taps the document.

I write the rate.

The clerk leaves with both pages.

He returns the next morning.

Corrected.

Maria translates them.

Then makes another copy.
