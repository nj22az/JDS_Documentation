# The Court of the Bent Chair

The man they arrested for Henry Every was Rafe Gulley, who had been caulking hoys at a Shadwell yard for eleven years and had spent all but three of those years complaining that Wapping did not appreciate him.

He was seized at the stairs in the last hour of daylight. Two constables took his arms and a third man in a dry brown coat followed with a paper held open before him, turning his eyes from the paper to Rafe and back again as if one of them must shortly improve.

Tall, the brown-coated man said.

Five foot eight in his stockings, said Rafe.

Strong made.

Bad left shoulder, said Rafe. Ask any man at Bell's yard.

About forty years of age.

Forty-seven next June, God willing, said Rafe, and less willing by the minute.

The paper had other particulars, but rain had entered from one corner and made a river through them. What remained was apparently sufficient. The constables took him west with his wrists tied and his wife following behind, still wearing her flour apron and explaining at a volume meant for courts that Henry Every had not spent the previous Wednesday under her kitchen table being sick into a coal scuttle.

By nine that evening Rafe was back.

He came into the Pelican with the rope still on one wrist. They had cut the other loop because it had drawn tight, but this one he had refused to surrender.

Evidence, he said.

Of what? Esther Finch asked from behind the bar.

That I have been recognised by the Crown.

The room bought him a drink. It was not kindness exactly. A man mistaken for the most hunted pirate in the world was entertainment, and Rafe had returned before anybody had been obliged to feel sorry for him.

Caleb Nix lifted the wet rope from Rafe's wrist and examined the knot.

Government work, he said.

How can you tell?

Too much line and nothing held.

That earned Caleb a drink as well. Within half an hour the rope had passed through twelve hands and become, by common agreement, the property of the house.

Esther sat at the end of the bar with her account book open. Thursday was settling night, and she had four beds filled, one sheet missing, two breakfasts disputed, and a ship's cook upstairs who claimed that the blood on the bolster had already been there. She entered Rafe's first drink under charity, struck the word out, and wrote public instruction.

Adams sat two places from her with a hand of cards he was not playing. He watched Rafe tell the story for the third time. The third telling had acquired an Admiralty clerk, two loaded pistols, and a moment in which Rafe declined five hundred pounds to confess.

The corn-factor's clerk believed all of it except the five hundred pounds. He lodged in Esther's smallest room and possessed a thin neck, an exact hand, and the confidence of a man whose employer had not yet trusted him with a sum large enough to steal.

Five hundred is for the right Every, the clerk said. They'd not pay it to the wrong one.

Rafe looked wounded.

Then it is fortunate they found a distinction between us.

Your wife found it.

My wife has always maintained there is only one of me. That is not the same thing.

From his place against the wall Tanner said, Try him here.

It was the first contribution he had made. His scaled boots were stretched towards the fire, the right heel crossed over the left. When he spoke, the space around the words tended to clear itself.

Rafe lowered his pot.

For what?

For being Henry Every, said Tanner. If the first court gives the wrong answer, we can try you again.

The room was quiet for one beat, then broke.

Louie lost his chair before he understood there was a court. It was the broad one with the bowed back which he occupied every night by a species of law older than the Admiralty. Two watermen lifted him out of it, set it beneath the smoke-dark window, and placed an empty wig stand on the seat as the person of the judge.

That chair leans, Louie warned.

So does justice, said Caleb.

Only when fat men sit on it.

Esther slapped the bar once with her cloth.

Nobody stands on the chair, she said. Nobody breaks the chair. If His Majesty requires candles, His Majesty pays for candles.

The corn-factor's clerk was made clerk of the court because he could write. Rafe was put in the space between the settle and the fire and told to look guilty. Tanner claimed the prosecution without being asked. Caleb offered to defend but was refused on the ground that he liked Rafe.

Adams was chosen instead.

He looked up from his cards.

On what ground?

You don't, said Rafe. That's the qualification.

Adams considered this and laid down his hand. Three kings and a knave, Esther noticed. He had been losing carefully all night and had just abandoned the one hand that might have betrayed him by winning.

Esther poured Hollands into a chipped measure and set it before the wig stand.

For the court, she said.

The court accepted.

The clerk found a spoiled victualling account and wrote in the clear margin:

*The King against Rafe Gulley, who is said to be another man.*

Charge the prisoner, said Tanner.

With piracy?

With answering a description.

That is no offence.

It is if the reward is large enough.

Rafe was required to give his name. He gave Henry Every. The room objected that this was prejudicial. He gave Rafe Gulley. Tanner objected that a wanted man would naturally do the same.

You see the difficulty, Tanner told the judge. Innocence is exactly what the accused would claim.

The wig stand offered no guidance.

The first witness was the boot-boy from the stairs, who had seen Henry Every twice. On the first occasion the pirate was bald, immensely broad, and had a black beard to his belt. On the second he was a narrow red-haired man with one blind eye.

Same man? the clerk asked.

Same reward, said the boy.

The court admitted the identification.

The second witness was Caleb, who swore that Rafe had spoken disrespectfully of the East India Company on more than one occasion.

What did he say? Tanner asked.

He said their beer was small.

Whose beer?

Whichever one bought it.

This answer caused argument over whether the Company could be insulted by a true statement. The clerk searched the discarded account for precedent and found that fourteen dead sailors had been charged for lamp oil after the date of their deaths.

There, he said. They can be insulted after death. It follows they can be insulted by truth.

Esther looked up despite herself.

That does not follow.

It is in the book.

Being written after one thing does not make it follow.

The clerk smiled. You ought to be judge, Mrs Finch.

I ought to be paid for the beds you gentlemen promised to fill.

That was entered as a direction of the court.

Tanner produced a handwritten information sheet. The first half copied the proclamation; the second added particulars supplied by men who expected to be paid for them. Nobody saw where he had got it. The paper was too clean for the street, and Esther wondered which office had given it to him and what he had given the office in return.

The accused, Tanner read, is of middle stature, strongly built, marked by the smallpox, accustomed to command, and possessed of a persuasive manner.

Rafe scratched his cheek.

I had the pox at nine.

There, said Tanner.

So did half London.

Then half London may hang and we shall certainly catch him.

The room approved the efficiency.

Adams had said almost nothing. He stood beside Rafe with one hand resting on the bowed chair and watched Tanner exhaust the witnesses. His face carried the mild interest he gave Esther's broken stair, a thing that might be put right if first examined without temper.

At last Tanner turned to him.

Has the defence any case?

No, Adams said.

Rafe swung round.

You are a natural advocate.

The prisoner is plainly the man described. Middle height, unless stood beside a taller man. Strong, unless asked to lift with the left arm. Pox-marked in a light that favours the Crown. Persuasive when his wife is elsewhere. If those particulars do not hang him, particulars have lost their purpose.

Tanner's mouth moved by the smallest degree.

Then you concede.

I concede the paper has found what it was written to find.

The room was quieter now. The clerk's pen stopped.

Adams picked up the wet length of rope from the table.

But there is a defect in the evidence. Henry Every would not allow that knot aboard his ship.

Caleb frowned.

Nothing wrong with the knot.

It will bind under strain and must be cut, Adams said. A man saving line would have used—

He stopped.

Not dramatically. No overturned cup, no hand to a knife. A small ending, no larger than a door latch settling into place.

Esther looked at him.

The room did not. Caleb had already begun demonstrating three superior knots with the court's evidence. Rafe was demanding acquittal. Louie wanted his chair back before justice put a second crack into it. The clerk bent over the account and tried to remember how *acquitted* was spelt.

Only Tanner looked at Esther.

His right heel came down from the left boot and rested flat on the floor.

Adams recovered first.

Any competent master, he said. On any competently kept ship.

Captain Kidd would say the same, the clerk said, pleased to introduce a fact he had read. He has the King's own commission.

Adams turned the rope once between his hands and gave it back.

Then the King may tie his knots for him.

The clerk laughed because the room did. A little colour had risen above his collar.

The trial moved to sentence. Rafe was found guilty of being insufficiently unlike Henry Every and condemned to buy a gallon for the witnesses. Rafe appealed on the ground that the witnesses had contradicted one another. The judge consumed its Hollands and rejected the appeal.

At that moment a man near the door laughed.

Esther had not seen him come in.

He wore an ordinary seaman's coat, wet at the shoulders and dry below, which meant he had not walked far in the rain. His cup stood full. He was watching the clerk write.

Esther closed her account book.

Court is risen, she said.

Nobody heard.

She took the wig stand from Louie's chair and put it beneath the bar.

Court is risen.

Caleb saw her face and began collecting cups without asking why. Tanner rose. The stranger by the door smiled as if the evening had been worth his penny and went out before anyone could decide whether he had paid it.

The laughter did not stop at once. It failed in pieces. Rafe was last, still holding up the rope and offering to arrest anybody who looked more like himself than he did.

The clerk looked towards the door.

Who was that?

You wrote names? Esther asked.

Only Rafe's.

Show me.

He turned the victualling account. The joke looked smaller in ink. Rafe Gulley, Tanner, Caleb Nix, and Mr Adams appeared in the clerk's exact hand beneath fourteen dead sailors charged for oil.

The clerk had written every name.

Esther tore the sheet once through the middle and fed the halves separately into the fire. The ink blackened before it burned. For a moment the names could be read more clearly than before.

It was only sport, the clerk said.

Sport for whom?

He had no answer.

Esther gathered her account book and stood. Adams moved to take her cloak from its peg. She let him hold it while she put it on, which was not forgiveness and not trust. His fingers were steady at the collar.

On the way past Tanner she looked down at the scaled boots.

You saw him?

I saw a man leave.

That was not what I asked.

No, Tanner said. It seldom is.

Outside, the rain had thinned to something the river could lift and move sideways. Adams walked her across the Pelican's rear yard to the lodging stair although she had not asked him. At her room door he waited while she tried the latch.

Mrs Finch.

She looked at him.

There are a great many bad knots at sea, he said.

So I am told.

And a man may know one without having tied it.

She opened the door.

Mr Adams, she said, you have paid through March. I recommend you survive until then. It simplifies the account.

For the first time since he had taken the room, he looked uncertain whether she had made a joke.

Esther went inside and left him to decide.
