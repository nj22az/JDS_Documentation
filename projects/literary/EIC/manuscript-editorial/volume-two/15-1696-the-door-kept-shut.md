# Part II: The Price of a Man

## Chapter 15: The Door Kept Shut

The first thing Esther sees is the lantern moving across the ceiling.

She is alone in the washhouse behind the Pelican, sitting naked in a coopered oak tub that is too short for her legs and too narrow for comfort. The water has already gone grey with soap, hearth soot and the dirt that Wapping lays upon a person merely for breathing. Her knees rise above the surface like two small islands. Steam clings to the rafters. On the copper beside her, a tallow dip gutters in its own grease.

It is the first full bath she has taken since Michaelmas.

That is not because Esther Finch objects to cleanliness. She objects to carrying twelve pails of water, buying coal enough to heat them, barring the door against men who believe a woman alone has issued an invitation, and then sitting in a wooden barrel while an inn full of sailors discovers reasons to need the washhouse. Most weeks a basin and cloth must serve. Tonight the Pelican has closed early, the last lodger has gone above, and Anders Blom has left a tub beneath the copper after steaming the lice from a sailmaker's bedding.

Esther has bought the remaining heat for herself.

Her shift hangs from a peg beside the street passage. Her wool dress lies folded on a stool beyond it. She has put both out of reach because there was nowhere else dry to put them.

The lantern moves again.

Not the candle. Its flame is at her shoulder, and its shadow rocks behind her. This light passes from left to right across the ceiling boards, thin at first, then bright enough to catch the steam. It comes through the low iron grating by the river drain.

Esther lowers her feet into the water without a splash.

The Pelican speaks differently when danger is near. Honest men make noise before entering. They curse at the mud, miss the lower step, strike the river door with a knuckle or a pewter pot. Watermen announce themselves because they want the keeper awake and paying. Thieves test a latch. Drunk men lean upon it.

These men do none of those things.

Outside the grating, leather presses softly against stone. Iron touches iron and is lifted away. A hooded light opens for half a breath, showing the hooked end of a boarding pole, then closes.

Esther turns her head towards the house.

No alarm has come from Wapping Wall. That tells her the street door is covered too.

She rises.

The washhouse air takes the heat from her skin at once. Water runs from her shoulders and down her legs onto the flagstones. Her shift is six paces away. Adams is two flights above her. Between the two lies perhaps the time required for a man outside to lift a bar from its brackets.

She does not spend that time dressing.

She steps out of the tub, catches one foot upon the rim, rights herself against the copper and crosses the washhouse entirely naked.

At the passage she hears three soft knocks from the river door.

Not a guest's knock. A signal.

Esther leaves her clothes where they are and runs.

Her wet feet strike the back stair in quick, flat blows. The boards are winter-cold and rough enough to take skin from her heel. She catches the rail, climbs through the dark and reaches the first landing as the cooper in the front room begins to snore again after turning over. A child coughs behind another door. Somewhere above, a bed rope creaks.

Nobody opens a door.

The second stair is narrower. Esther takes it two steps at a time. She has no candle. Her right hip strikes the newel and the pain goes through her hard enough to bring water to her eyes, but she does not slow.

Adams's room is at the back, beneath the eaves.

She does not call his name. The name is a rented thing and the walls have gaps.

She lifts the wooden latch and enters.

Adams is awake before the door reaches the wall.

He comes up from the bed with a pistol in his right hand and the blanket falling from his chest. The pistol is cocked. In the darkness its barrel finds the doorway, then lifts half an inch when he knows her.

His eyes go once to her face.

They do not go lower.

That saves him a second he does not have.

Men at both doors, Esther says. River and street.

How many?

Enough.

Constables?

They brought a hook for the river bar.

Adams is out of bed. He pulls on his breeches with his left hand while keeping the pistol in his right. There is no confusion in him, no question and no plea. Fear would be easier to watch. What enters his face is calculation.

How long?

They are touching the door.

He takes a second pistol from beneath the bolster and puts it through his belt. A short hanger follows. The blanket lies between them on the floor. Esther is entirely naked, wet and beginning to shake with cold. Neither gives the fact another second.

Wake the house, Adams says.

No.

They will come through.

And if I wake thirty frightened people, half will run towards the men and half will run towards you.

Adams opens the pan of the pistol in his hand and touches the priming powder with one finger. Satisfied it is dry, he closes it.

The stair is narrow, he says. Two men can hold it.

Two men can fill it with dead lodgers.

They are coming for me.

Then you will leave.

His mouth changes by the smallest amount. Not a smile. An old refusal finding its place.

Through what?

Esther points at the sloped ceiling.

The hatch behind the linen press. Anders fitted the pin.

Adams listens. From below comes a small metallic scrape, then another. Somebody at the river door is working beneath the lower hinge.

Your clothes, he says.

If I stop to be decent, you may hang decent.

She turns for the landing.

Mrs Finch.

The pistol is still in his hand, but he is holding it down now.

Why?

You paid through March.

It is the answer she gave him before, when the stranger at the mock court wrote their names. It was not true enough then. It is not true enough now. They both know it.

Esther steps into the passage and strikes the opposite door three times with her palm.

Anders.

Nothing.

She strikes again.

Anders Blom, get up or lose the roof.

The door opens upon a knife.

Anders lowers it when he sees her. He is a long, pale shape in a linen shirt, his fair hair loose on one side and flattened on the other. Sleep leaves him slowly until he hears the sound below. Then it is gone at once.

River door? he asks.

And the street.

He looks past her at Adams buckling on his hanger.

They have him, Anders says.

Not yet.

Esther goes to the linen press. Together she and Anders drag it away from the wall. Behind it, set into the slope of the roof, is a square oak hatch blackened by a century of smoke. No lodger knows it opens. Elias found it when replacing tiles after the great wind, and Anders fitted a new pin because Elias's hands had become too swollen to work the old one.

Anders pushes up.

The hatch does not move.

Swollen, he says.

Then unswell it.

He looks at her, and for the first time seems to understand that she has reached the top of the house without a stitch upon her. He pulls off his shirt.

Not yet, she says.

Esther—

Open the roof.

He gives her the shirt anyway. She does not put it on. She catches it in one hand, holding it against herself only after he has turned to the hatch, and sees his carpenter's satchel hanging from the bedpost.

Chisel, she says.

Anders takes the narrow mortising chisel and a mallet from the satchel. The first blow into the pin sounds louder than a pistol.

Below them, a fist strikes the street door.

Esther!

Caleb Nix.

He strikes again, not with the slow authority of a constable but with the full side of his arm.

Esther, open! Open the bloody door!

Every room on the first landing wakes at once.

Feet hit boards. A woman calls for her husband. The ship's cook begins asking who is afire. A pot overturns and rolls beneath a bed.

Adams moves towards the main stair.

Esther catches his wrist.

No.

He came to warn us.

He is warning us.

Caleb pounds again.

Behind his voice, another man says, Stand away from that door.

Caleb answers with a noise like a breath driven from him. Something heavy strikes the outer wall. The pounding stops.

Adams looks at Esther's hand upon his wrist. He could break it without effort. His eyes have gone flat and distant.

Open it, he says.

And give them you?

He is outside because of me.

He is outside because he chose to knock.

That does not make him expendable.

Nor you.

From the street comes a measured blow against the oak door. Not a fist this time. A ram or mallet. The iron latch jumps in its housing.

The rooms below are opening.

Esther releases Adams and goes to the stairhead. She drops Anders's shirt over her shoulders but has no time to put her arms through it. It hangs down her back and leaves most of her uncovered. Her wet hair has begun to drip again as the warmth of running leaves her.

Stay in your rooms, she calls.

The cooper appears on the lower landing in drawers and one stocking, holding an adze.

Who is it?

Men who want one man. Do not offer them twelve.

Mrs Finch, are you—

Back inside.

There is enough command in the order that the cooper obeys before his surprise can become a question. Doors close one by one.

The man outside calls through the street door.

In the King's name, open.

The voice is educated, dry and almost courteous. Esther knows it. The stranger from the Court of the Bent Chair had laughed beside the door and left his drink untouched.

The Pelican is required to admit lawful search, he says. Henry Every is harboured within.

No Henry Every lodges here, Esther calls.

Then you have nothing to fear.

Men who say that generally bring the fear with them.

The door shakes under a second blow.

Behind Esther, Anders drives the chisel beneath the hatch pin. The wood gives a low crack.

Adams takes the back of a table and turns it onto its edge across the upper stair. He moves without hurry, placing the legs against the wall and rail so that pressure from below will drive them deeper rather than throw them aside.

Esther watches him do it.

You have done this before.

I have left rooms before.

With men at the door?

Most rooms worth leaving have men at the door.

He draws the second pistol from his belt and checks its cock.

Esther points at it.

You fire down my stair and I shall throw you through the roof myself.

If they clear the table, I fire.

You miss.

I do not.

That is what worries me.

The third blow splits something at the street entrance. Oak complains through every frame of the house.

The river door answers with a separate crash.

They are coming through both.

Anders's pin lifts.

Cold air spills around the hatch, bringing coal smoke, rain and the river's black mineral smell into the passage. He pushes with both hands. The hatch rises a finger's width and stops against the lead flashing.

It was opening yesterday, he says.

Yesterday nobody required it.

He puts his shoulder beneath it. Adams joins him. Together they force the oak upward. Lead tears from the frame with a long, shrill cry.

The street door gives way.

Men enter below in a rush that is immediately slowed by darkness, benches and one another. A lantern falls. Somebody curses the low beam and somebody else orders Caleb secured. The information-taker does not raise his voice.

Front stair. Two remain at the bar. Watch the rear passage.

He knows the house.

Esther looks down the dark well of the stair. For one instant she sees Caleb through the banisters. He is on his knees near the broken door, one constable twisting his arms behind him and another winding cord around his wrists. Blood runs from Caleb's nose into his mouth.

He sees Esther above.

His face changes when he sees what she is wearing and what she is not. Not lust. Not amusement. Grief that he has brought the night to her door.

Sorry, Ess, he says.

She puts her hand upon the stair rail.

You knocked, she says.

The constable pulls Caleb backwards. A retained waterman comes up the first three steps with a cudgel held before him.

At the river end of the house, iron tears out of wood.

Adams raises his pistol.

Esther turns.

Go.

Not without you.

This is my house.

It is surrounded.

Then someone must remain to tell them they have searched it.

Anders is half through the hatch. Rain beads upon his bare shoulders. He reaches down.

Mrs Finch.

Esther puts Anders's shirt around herself properly now. It reaches only to the middle of her thighs. She knots its open neck once and pushes Adams towards the hatch.

He resists.

She leans close enough that only he can hear her.

If you make my naked run a wasted journey, Mr Adams, I shall give them your true name myself.

That moves him.

He catches the hatch edge, pulls himself into the rain and vanishes onto the roof.

Anders follows as far as his waist, then stops.

The spar, he says.

A loose length of fir lies beneath the eaves, once part of a broken topmast and kept by Elias because no useful timber leaves the Pelican while it can still be lifted. Esther and Anders drag it across the landing and feed one end through the banister. The other catches beneath the ceiling beam. It braces the overturned table from above.

The first waterman reaches the landing and shoves.

The table moves two inches. The spar bites into the rail. A leg snaps but the barricade holds.

The man thrusts his cudgel through the gap.

Esther stamps down upon his wrist with her bare heel.

He cries out and drops the cudgel. It falls between the stairs and strikes a constable below.

Anders grins despite himself.

Göteborg justice, he says.

Wapping rent collection.

The waterman forces his arm through again, this time with a hanger. The blade cuts Anders's sleeve and opens the skin along his forearm.

Adams reappears in the hatch above them.

He fires.

The pistol's flash fills the narrow passage white. Sound strikes the walls and returns doubled. Esther feels it in her teeth.

For a second there is no house, only powder smoke, shouting and the high animal cry of a man who cannot yet tell whether he has been hit. The ball takes a fist of plaster from the wall beside the waterman's head. Lath and lime fall into his eyes. He tumbles backwards into those below.

Adams has missed.

Esther looks up.

His face tells her he has not.

He has put the ball where it will empty the stairs without making a corpse upon them.

That frightens her more than a killing might have done.

The spent pistol goes through his belt. Adams reaches down for Anders.

The information-taker calls from below.

Mr Adams. Henry Every, alias Bridgeman. Whatever name you answer to, the river stairs are held and Wapping Wall is closed. Lay down your arms.

Adams's eyes do not change at any of the names.

Esther hears the silence around him and knows one has landed.

Go, she says again.

Anders climbs through.

Esther pulls the hatch down behind him. It does not close completely now that the lead is torn, but its weight hides the opening. She drags the linen press back as far as she can. It leaves a black gap the width of her hand against the wall.

The barricade below groans.

She stands alone at the stairhead in a Swedish carpenter's shirt, naked beneath it, her feet bleeding lightly from splinters and her skin still wet in the places the cloth does not cover.

The table breaks.

Three men come through together. The first has lime in his eyes. The second holds a short hanger. The third carries a dark lantern and tries to lift it high enough to see her without appearing to look.

All three stop.

Esther folds her arms.

You have broken my front door, she says. You will pay for it.

The man with the lantern recovers first.

Where is he?

Who?

Henry Every.

She looks at the broken table.

Is he a joiner?

The information-taker comes up behind them.

He is the man from the mock court. Up close, his brown coat is better than it first appeared, the wool tightly woven and the buttons plain horn chosen to be forgotten. Rain marks only one shoulder. He has entered by the street door and let other men take the river.

His eyes rest upon Esther's face. He removes his hat.

Mrs Finch.

You have the advantage.

Jonas Cresswell.

Company?

Retained upon its information. The warrant belongs to a justice.

Then the Company may pay for the door and the justice for the table.

His gaze moves to the dark gap beside the linen press.

Search the roof.

Two men pull the press away. The hatch lifts. Rain falls through upon the folded sheets.

Cresswell looks at Esther again.

You warned him.

I heard men breaking into my house.

You ran two flights of stairs without stopping to dress.

Esther looks down at Anders's shirt as if only now considering it.

You were moving very quickly.

One of the constables laughs and turns it into a cough when Cresswell glances at him.

From the roof comes a shout.

Blood here!

Cresswell steps beneath the hatch.

Another voice farther away calls that the timber loft is open. Boots cross the tiles. A slate breaks and tumbles into the yard.

Cresswell's attention sharpens. He points two men after the sound and sends another back to the river stairs.

Downriver, he says. Do not let the tide carry him past Blackwall.

The order is right.

It is also late.

* * *

The Pelican roof is pitched to shed rain and built to kill any man who trusts it.

Anders knows this because he has repaired it.

He goes first on hands and bare feet, testing the wet slates near their pegs before laying weight upon them. Rain has made the roof black and shining. Chimneys rise like broken teeth against the low London sky. Beyond them, masts crowd the river in layers, their rigging stirring though the ships appear still.

Adams follows with his hanger between his teeth.

He is barefoot too. His boots would slide, and there was no time to lace them. Anders crosses the ridge towards the rear gable, reaches the old roof light and puts one hand upon its frame.

Wait, he says.

Adams does not.

The glass has been broken since summer and replaced with sailcloth tacked over a lead edge. Adams catches the cloth, swings across and puts his left palm upon the broken lead.

It opens him from the base of the thumb to the wrist.

He makes no sound. He pulls the hand back and blood appears black in the rain.

That hand? Anders asks.

Still mine.

Can you fire?

Once.

It is not an answer, but Anders has known captains and does not ask twice when an order has already been disguised as information.

They reach the parapet above the old ship-breaking shed. A gap of four feet separates the Pelican roof from the shed's upper loft. Below it lies a narrow passage filled with broken knees of timber, iron dogs, tar casks and an anchor fluke pointing upward.

Anders jumps.

The shed roof bows beneath him and holds.

Adams looks once behind. Lantern light spills from the opened hatch. A head rises through it.

He jumps with his bleeding hand tucked against his chest.

His right foot lands. The left slips. For an instant half his body is over the passage and the anchor fluke waits below.

Anders catches the back of his breeches and pulls.

They fall together upon the tiles.

Adams turns at once, pistol in his good hand.

If you shoot me after that, Anders says, I shall take it personally.

Adams lowers the weapon.

I was measuring the distance.

To me?

To the man behind you.

Anders rolls aside as a constable reaches the Pelican parapet. The man sees the gap, the rain and Adams's pistol. He chooses not to jump.

Adams and Anders slide down the shed roof to a dormer, kick through its rotten frame and drop into darkness.

The loft smells of old oak, tar, mice and river damp. Pelican timbers have lain here since before either man was born: futtocks black with age, deck planks with treenail holes, blocks without sheaves, ribs sawn from ships whose names have survived only in unpaid accounts.

Anders moves among them without light.

Elias taught him the path. Three steps forward, turn at the hanging chain, duck beneath the mast hoop, place the right foot upon the wall plate and the left upon the ladder. The ladder descends to the timber stage behind Darnell's yard.

Adams stops at the head of it.

Mrs Finch?

Stays.

They will take her.

They may question her. Caleb they will take.

Because he knocked.

Because he was caught.

Adams presses the cut hand beneath his opposite arm. Blood runs from his elbow.

Go back, he says.

And tell her I misplaced you?

Tell her—

He has nothing after that.

Anders waits.

Thank her? he suggests.

No.

Good. She would hate that.

Below, the ebb works around the piles with the soft, muscular pull of a large animal. Anders's skiff is tied beneath the stage where the yard watchman cannot see it from his stove. He lowers himself onto the ladder.

Adams does not move.

You know who I am, he says.

Anders looks up.

I know Esther told me to open a roof.

The reward is five hundred pounds.

Then you should not bleed on me. It lowers the value.

Adams gives one sound that might be a laugh if it had more life in it. He comes down.

They reach the skiff as lanterns enter the loft above.

Anders does not untie the painter. He cuts it. The tide takes the bow before both men are seated, swinging them stern-first between the piles. Adams reaches for an oar.

Do not, Anders says.

They will see us.

They will hear us first.

Anders lies flat and pulls Adams down by the coat. The skiff turns in black water, touches one weed-slick post and slips free. Above, men run onto the timber stage. Their lantern light reaches the river in broken gold ladders.

One waterman points upriver.

There!

A ship's boat is crossing towards Limehouse Reach, four men at the oars and a tarpaulin heaped in the stern. The searchers call to it. The boat rows harder.

The skiff passes beneath them unseen.

The ebb carries it downriver, close against the black sides of moored lighters. Anders uses one hand against the planks to keep them from striking. No oar enters the water. No pistol fires. The river that brings every hunted man to the Pelican takes this one away.

At the bend, the lights of the inn vanish behind ships.

Adams sits up.

His left hand is full of blood.

Anders tears a strip from the rag wound around the forward rowlock and binds it.

Where? he asks.

Adams looks towards the invisible shore.

Anywhere the paper arrives after I do.

* * *

By dawn the Pelican has been searched three times.

The constables open mattresses, empty sea-chests and thrust iron rods into the ashes. They pull boards from Adams's room and find a silver coin beneath one, broad and unfamiliar, with Persian letters across its face. Cresswell wraps it in cloth without touching it directly, as if piracy might pass through the skin.

They find the spent pistol wadding at the stair and blood upon the roof light. They find Adams's boots beneath his bed.

They do not find Adams.

Caleb sits tied to a post in the public room while the search continues. His nose has stopped bleeding. One eye is closing. Each time Esther passes him he tries to speak, and each time she shakes her head.

There is nothing he can say that will make the door open sooner.

At five, Cresswell comes down from the upper floor with rain on his coat and lime dust upon one sleeve.

He lays a folded paper on the bar.

Receipt, he says.

For what?

Property removed in lawful search.

Esther opens it. The list includes one foreign silver coin, a length of bloodied linen, two boots, one discharged pistol wad and three letters addressed to Mr Adams.

It does not include the broken door, the table, the roof hatch, six slates, the torn lead, the emptied mattresses or Caleb Nix.

Your account is incomplete, she says.

You may petition.

Whom?

The justice who issued the warrant.

And if he dislikes the price?

Cresswell puts on his gloves.

Then the King's peace has been expensive to you.

He turns towards Caleb.

Esther places both hands upon the bar.

He warned a householder of men in the lane. That is not harbouring.

He obstructed officers.

He knocked upon a door.

At the wrong moment.

There it is, Esther says. The whole English law in four words.

Cresswell studies her. In daylight he looks tired rather than sinister, which she finds worse. A cruel man may be opposed. A tired man finishing necessary work will carry anyone to prison and sleep afterwards.

You could have opened the door, he says.

And you could have knocked before bringing the ram.

We did knock.

At both doors together.

He gives her the smallest bow.

Mrs Finch, a dangerous man escaped tonight.

Yes.

You assisted him.

I kept an inn. Men entered. One left.

Five hundred pounds might have purchased a better answer.

Five hundred pounds has purchased every bad answer in Wapping.

Cresswell's eyes move towards the stair where the wet marks of Esther's feet have dried into pale soap-edged prints. They begin at the washhouse passage and climb without pause.

You knew what he was, Cresswell says.

Esther looks at Caleb.

He sits with rope biting both wrists because she kept the door shut.

I knew what you were, she says.

Cresswell takes Caleb by the arm. The constables form around them and lead him through the broken entrance into the colourless morning.

Caleb looks back once.

Esther does not tell him she is sorry. Sorry is a coin offered after the market has closed.

She stands in the doorway wearing Anders Blom's shirt beneath her winter cloak, her bare feet among oak splinters, and watches until the lane takes him.

Then she closes the Pelican.

The broken door will not meet its frame. She lifts it by the lower hinge, puts her shoulder against the inner face and forces the bolt across one finger's width at a time. The iron enters its socket with a dull, final knock.

In the washhouse, the bathwater is cold.

Her shift still hangs upon its peg. Her dress remains folded on the stool. The candle has drowned in its own tallow, and river mud lies beneath the grating where a boot pressed through.

Esther lowers herself beside the tub.

For the first time since the light crossed the ceiling, nobody requires her to move.

She looks at the water, at the small grey surface in which the house rests upside down, and understands that the night will be told incorrectly by everyone who survives it.

The constables will say they nearly caught Henry Every. The watermen will say he fired upon them. Adams will remember the roof and the tide. Anders will remember the broken lead and the skiff. Men in taverns will remember that Esther Finch ran naked through the Pelican, because a naked woman is easier to repeat than the choice that sent her upstairs.

Caleb will remember the door.

So will she.

Esther takes the folded shift from its peg and dresses in the cold.
