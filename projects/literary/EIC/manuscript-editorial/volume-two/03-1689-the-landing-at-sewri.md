# Part I: The Company’s War

## Chapter 3: The Landing at Sewri

> **Bombay, night of 14–15 February 1689**
>
> *“An island is only a fortress while the water agrees.”*
>
> — Master Gunner Jonathan Hobbs

### I. Low Water

The tide makes a road at Sewri.

For six hours it has covered the eastern flats, lifting fishing stakes, filling mangrove channels and separating Parel from Mazagaon and Mazagaon from Bombay. Then the moonless water begins to withdraw. Black ridges emerge between the islands. Creeks narrow. Mud becomes ground.

Men who understand the tide have been waiting for it.

Tanner stands on the northern work with one foot upon the cheek of a dismounted gun carriage. The wall beneath him is unfinished. Fresh lime shows pale between older blocks, and three embrasures remain filled with woven palm, earth and timber because the stone promised from the mainland has not arrived.

Nothing has arrived from the mainland except rumours.

Beyond the wall the island is dark. The distant fires noted before Miller’s burial have gone out. No village lamp shows towards Parel. No boat challenges the roadstead. Even the frogs among the flooded palms have fallen quiet.

Hobbs leans over the parapet.

Hear that?

Tanner hears rigging against a mast in the harbour. A sentry coughing below. The faint scrape of Hawkins shifting his matchlock from one shoulder to the other.

Then the sound comes again.

Not one stroke. Many. Wood entering water, lifting, entering again. The rhythm is too broad to belong to a single craft and too disciplined for fishermen running a tide. It reaches the wall without revealing direction, carried through creeks and around low islands until the whole north-east seems to be rowing.

Hobbs removes his cap.

The skin beneath is white.

How many? Hawkins asks.

Hobbs looks at him.

Enough not to count from here.

The gate wicket opens below. Padre Miguel enters without waiting for the guard to recognise him. Mud reaches above his knees. One sleeve has been torn from wrist to elbow. Behind him comes Rama Koli, barefoot and breathing through his mouth, with a rolled fishing net over one shoulder.

The guard lowers his weapon.

Where is your pass?

Rama drops the net.

A dead heron falls out.

Wrapped inside it is a strip of wet cotton marked with charcoal: an inlet, two high banks, a line of boats and arrows pointing west.

Miguel looks up at Tanner.

They are coming ashore below Sewri.

How many?

Rama answers before the priest can translate. He opens both hands, closes them, opens them again, then gestures towards the dark water.

Miguel shakes his head.

He stopped counting boats when the boats concealed the shore.

When did you leave?

At high water. They held him until the first men were landed. He came through a mangrove channel too narrow for their gallivats and walked the last part.

Who held him?

Men of Janjira. Others with them. He heard Marathi, Hindustani and a tongue he did not know.

That is more useful than a flag.

Tanner descends the stair.

Rama catches his wrist and speaks rapidly. Miguel listens.

They have pilots, the priest says. Men who know every cut between Sewri and Mazagaon. They have taken the watchmen at the little fort. No alarm was fired.

Why let Rama go?

They did not. He went under a boat.

Hobbs has followed them down.

Where are the Castle scouts?

Miguel looks towards the council house.

Still waiting for authority to discover what has already happened.

The first alarm gun sounds before Tanner reaches the gallery.

It is fired from the northern outwork without proper warning. The charge is too heavy. Flame opens red above the parapet, followed by a report that strikes every shutter in the Castle at once. A flock of roosting birds tears itself from the palms.

The shot goes nowhere useful.

The rowing continues.

### II. Defensible

Ephraim Vane has dressed for council.

His coat is buttoned, his stockings are clean and the green ledger lies open beneath two candles. Sir John Child stands at the end of the table with one hand upon a map. Captain St John, commander of the landward companies, is attempting to buckle on a sword belt over a nightshirt and breeches.

The room has received three reports in ten minutes.

The first says Maratha raiders have entered Parel.

The second says fishermen are burning huts to conceal a theft of Company timber.

The third says Sidi Yakut Khan has landed an army at Mahim, Sewri and every place between.

Vane arranges the papers by the rank of the man who delivered them.

Rama’s charcoal map is not among them.

Tanner places it over Child’s printed chart.

The governor looks at the wet cloth.

What is this?

The report that arrived on its own feet.

Vane lifts one corner without touching the mud.

From whom?

Rama Koli.

The unlicensed boatman.

The pilot who drew the creek for us in eighty-six.

The man has an interest in frightening us away from the northern fisheries.

Miguel steps forward.

He has no fishery left, Mr Vane. Your patrols took his boat.

Vane ignores him.

Child studies the map. Fatigue has hollowed the flesh beneath his eyes, but his attention is exact. He is not a madman surprised by consequence. He is a governor who has spent years describing authority so completely that the physical world now arrives as an insult.

What strength at Sewri? he asks.

Unknown.

Artillery?

Unknown.

Horse?

Unknown.

Then what precisely do you know?

That the beach is full of boats, the watch has failed and the tide is falling in their favour.

Captain St John finds the buckle.

I require the militia at Dongri and the Mazagaon work reinforced. We can hold both until daylight.

Hobbs looks at the map.

With which guns?

The six-pounder at Mazagaon. Two sakers at the timber yard.

One saker has no vent cover. The other has a split axle. The six-pounder can fire if no one asks it to move.

Then make it move.

Vane draws a paper from beneath the ledger.

The Mazagaon warehouse contains timber, cordage and grain entered as stores under military protection. Abandonment will void the valuation.

St John turns.

I am discussing ground, Mr Vane.

The warehouse is upon the ground.

There are perhaps thousands coming over Parel.

Then the stores are precisely what they mean to take.

For one useful instant the room sees Vane’s war entire. Men may retreat. Grain entered as secure may not.

Child points to Tanner.

Take thirty men to Mazagaon. Remove the powder, preserve the grain and support the militia. Hobbs will bring the six-pounder south if the position cannot be maintained.

St John objects.

I need Tanner at the Dongri road.

Vane speaks over him.

The cordage must be inventoried before removal. Otherwise responsibility—

Child strikes the table once.

The sound stops them.

Sergeant Tanner will secure Mazagaon and fall back upon the Castle when necessary. Captain St John will hold the road. Mr Vane will issue every cart and draught animal required.

Vane’s face alters.

The bullocks are entered against harbour works.

Then alter the entry.

Tanner takes Rama’s cloth from the map.

How will I know when St John falls back?

The captain answers.

You will see me.

And if I see the enemy first?

Then you will have mistaken me for him.

Hobbs pushes his cap onto his head.

That is the clearest order of the night.

### III. The Six-Pounder

Mazagaon is burning when they reach it.

Not the whole settlement. Three roofs near the English timber yard have caught, sending sparks over coconut trees and store sheds. Nobody knows who began it. A Company watchman blames the landing force. A Portuguese householder blames the watchmen. A Marathi carpenter says the first flame came from a clerk burning prize papers behind the warehouse.

The distinction no longer matters to the roofs.

The six-pounder stands upon the rise below the small fort. Its bronze barrel is green around the mouldings and hot from three rounds fired into darkness. Hobbs puts his palm near the chase without touching.

Who laid this?

An Indo-Portuguese gun captain named Fernandes points towards the creek.

At boats.

Which boats?

The dark ones.

Hobbs closes his eyes.

All boats are dark at night.

The gun captain has twelve men. Four carry Company coats, three wear militia sashes, two are dock labourers, one is a cook, and the remaining pair are boys old enough to lift a handspike together. Their powder lies in open tubs behind the carriage.

Hobbs begins by saving it from its defenders.

Lids. Wet hides. Slow match to leeward. Sponge after every round. If any man loads before the worm is clear, I shall kill him before the gun manages it.

The familiar certainty steadies them.

Tanner goes down to the timber yard.

Vane’s promised carts have not come. One bullock lies dead in its traces beside the gate. The other has been taken by the deputy storekeeper to carry his wife, two children and a chest towards the Castle. Forty men are attempting to move grain by hand while bales of cordage remain stacked beneath a roof already smoking.

Hawkins stands among them with a tally.

Powder first, Tanner says.

Mr Vane’s order says grain.

Powder burns faster.

The boy looks at the warehouse.

So does cordage.

Then Vane may choose which fire to enter.

They form a line. Powder kegs move out first, then bags of rice small enough for two men to carry. The larger sacks are cut and divided into sailcloth. Grain falls beneath their feet and becomes mud. A clerk objects until Tanner gives him one corner of a hundredweight bag.

From the rise comes the six-pounder’s report.

This charge is measured. The sound is hard rather than wild. It travels across the flats and returns from Parel.

Then come the matchlocks.

Not a volley. Individual cracks, widely spaced, each followed by the small red falling star of a match being lowered. The shots advance through the groves from north to south. The landing force is not rushing. It is feeling the ground, securing each track and high place before the mud behind it hardens.

Tanner climbs the rise.

Dawn has begun without light. The eastern sky is a diluted grey over the harbour. Below Sewri, boats cover the remaining water in broken rows. Men have already crossed the first mudflat. More emerge from the creek, carrying matchlocks above their heads, leading small guns, bearing coils of rope and baskets on poles. Their order is more frightening than shouting would be.

No single theatrical mass comes into view.

There are only men arriving faster than the island can oppose them.

Rama points.

A second body is moving along the Parel side, using a bank concealed from the English works. Tanner sees heads, spear points and the occasional pale flash of cloth. The bank leads towards the low crossing behind Mazagaon.

They will be behind us before the grain reaches the road.

Hobbs peers along the six-pounder.

Then the grain has become theirs.

He fires again.

The ball strikes the mud ahead of the advancing line, throws a black fan into the air and passes on. Men drop. Whether from iron or instinct, Tanner cannot tell. The line opens around the mark and closes after it.

Hobbs watches.

Too low.

You hit something.

Mud. The island is full of it.

Fernandes begins to reload.

Hobbs stops him.

We move.

The gun captain looks at the carriage.

The road is soft.

It was soft when they gave us a gun.

They run drag ropes through the carriage and put twenty men upon them. The gun moves three feet, sinks to the nave and stops. More men join. The left wheel rises with a wet sucking sound. The right remains buried.

Hawkins arrives from the store.

Powder is away. Six bags of rice. No carts.

Where is St John?

Nobody has seen him.

The firing to the west grows louder.

Hobbs kneels beside the trapped wheel and pushes his knife into the mud. The blade disappears to the haft.

Leave the carriage, Tanner says.

Hobbs looks up.

Bronze barrel. Iron fittings. Sound wheels.

Leave it.

We can sling the piece.

With what animal?

Hobbs looks towards the dead bullock, though it is half a field away and hidden by smoke.

Tanner takes an axe from a timber man and cuts the cap square from the left trunnion. Hobbs makes a sound as though struck.

What are you doing?

Saving the men who would save the gun.

They lift the freed barrel with handspikes and roll it from the carriage onto two timber skids. The carriage remains upright in the mud, an English machine awaiting a new owner.

Hobbs takes the leather pouch containing the vent tools.

He does not look back.

### IV. The Road South

The retreat begins as an order and becomes a population.

Soldiers come first from the Dongri road in groups too small to be companies. Some retain their matchlocks. Others carry only belts and powder horns. Captain St John is not among them.

Behind the soldiers come factors, warehouse men, fish sellers, grooms, widows, children, servants, priests and people who belong to no Company list. Bundles grow larger as weapons disappear. A man carries a locked writing desk while his daughter walks barefoot beside him. A woman has brought a brass cooking pot and nothing to put into it. Two boys drag a door between them because their grandmother cannot walk.

The six-pounder barrel lies across a timber handcart. Hobbs walks beside it with one hand upon the bronze as if guiding an invalid.

Tanner keeps the retreat on the raised track. To either side the flats are too soft for carts and too exposed for frightened people to scatter. Smoke from Mazagaon passes over them. In its intervals he sees the landing force advancing along two lines, one towards Dongri and one behind the abandoned fort.

They are not slaughtering everyone they meet.

That would be easier to understand.

Men who drop Company weapons are pulled aside and searched. Labourers and families are directed off the military road. A group of armed militia who try to hold a garden wall receive a close matchlock volley and disappear in smoke. The Sidi’s officers distinguish between bodies more efficiently than the Company has done for years.

Miguel moves along the column calling in Portuguese and Marathi.

Leave the boxes. Keep the water. Children between adults. Do not enter the flats.

Rama speaks to families from the fishing settlements and points them west, away from the Castle road.

Tanner catches his arm.

The gate is south.

The priest translates Rama’s answer.

The gate will close.

Not yet.

It always closes before the last poor man reaches it.

A shot cuts leaves from the palm above them.

Hawkins raises his matchlock towards the smoke.

Tanner pushes the barrel down.

Can you see a man?

No.

Then you are firing at an island.

They move.

Near Dongri they find Captain St John.

He has collected perhaps eighty militia behind a low bank and ordered them to cover the road. The position commands nothing. The enemy can pass on either side through palms and dry channels. St John’s face is grey beneath powder smoke, but his sword is drawn and his voice remains formal.

You were to support me.

You were to be visible.

Where is the gun?

Hobbs points to the barrel.

Mostly there.

St John stares at the missing carriage.

I ordered the position held.

Which position? Tanner asks.

The captain looks north. For the first time he seems to notice that every landmark behind him is either occupied or burning.

The Castle signal flags appear above the southern wall.

Three red squares.

Retire within the works.

St John reads them twice.

Then he orders his men to fire a volley at an enemy still beyond useful range.

Half the matches fail. The rest produce a ragged line of smoke.

The answer comes from both flanks.

Two militia men fall. A third spins against the bank with his hand over his mouth. The column breaks around them.

Order has finished.

Tanner strikes St John’s sword down before it takes the ear from a fleeing soldier.

South, he says. Or remain and command the mud.

They go south.

### V. The Gate

The Castle gate is wide enough for a cart and too narrow for fear.

By the time Tanner reaches it, the approach is blocked from wall to ditch. People press towards the arch while guards inside try to separate soldiers from families, English from Portuguese, Company servants from everyone whose service has never been written down.

Vane stands upon a wagon beside the wicket with three lists.

Military to the left. Covenanted servants and dependants to the centre. Island inhabitants must remain clear until arms have passed.

Nobody remains clear.

A gun captain pushes his way through with neither gun nor crew because his name is on the military list. An Indo-Portuguese widow is turned back because her husband’s company cannot be identified. Two bales of private cloth pass beneath a factor’s seal while a carpenter who built the north wall is told there is no entry for him.

Tanner reaches the guards.

Open the full leaf.

Vane looks down.

The inner yard is already beyond capacity.

Open it.

The enemy is less than half a mile behind you.

So are half the island.

St John arrives, sees the crush and climbs the wagon.

Close after the soldiers.

Tanner turns.

There are no soldiers separate from it.

Then make a line.

With whom?

St John looks for his company. Fewer than twenty remain near him.

Hobbs’s gun barrel reaches the gate. The handcart wedges between a wheel and the stone jamb. Men push from behind. People scream as the cart tilts.

Hobbs climbs onto it.

Back, you blind bastards. Give the wheel a foot or I shall load the piece loose and fire it through all of you.

There is no carriage, no powder and no intention behind the threat.

It works.

The crowd yields one foot.

They straighten the cart and drag it through. Tanner sends Hawkins and Fernandes with it. Then he takes six guards beyond the arch and forms them across the road with matchlocks held sideways.

Walk, he says. If you run, the child in front of you dies under your feet.

The first ranks slow.

Families enter five abreast. A clerk calls names until the names become useless. Miguel brings the grandmother on the door. Hamid Rafiq emerges from the salt-house detail carrying a water jar and helps lift her clear. A cooper rolls two casks through and returns for his wife. The factor’s cloth remains inside.

Rama is not in the column.

Tanner searches the faces.

He finds the woman from the Back Bay route—the one who carried the other end of Rama’s basket—holding a boy against her hip. She reaches the arch and catches Tanner’s sleeve.

Miguel listens to her.

Rama went back for his sister’s children.

How many?

Three.

Where?

Beyond Dongri.

The firing has changed. Matchlocks crack from the first houses north of the approach. Stone chips jump from the outer wall. A guard drops without a sound, struck beneath the cheek.

St John shouts from inside.

Close the gate.

Tanner keeps the line.

Another fifty.

Close it.

The heavy leaves begin to move.

Tanner puts his shoulder against one and stops it.

People pour through the narrowing opening. A basket bursts. Rice runs beneath boots. A child falls, vanishes and reappears in Miguel’s arms. A sailor throws away his musket to pull an old man by both wrists.

From the north comes a measured drum.

Not the frantic alarm of the Castle. A steady field beat marking men who are already where they mean to be.

Tanner sees the leading Sidi troops between the houses.

They advance behind mantlets and garden walls, not charging, not wasting themselves. A matchlock rests upon every available stone. The road is within their fire.

If the gate remains open, the crowd will carry armed men through whether the Sidi sends them or not. If it closes, the people still upon the road become no concern of the wall.

The choice has no clean side.

That is why it belongs to Tanner.

He looks once more for Rama.

There is no sign of him.

Close, Tanner says.

The guards pull.

Hands strike the wood before the gap is gone. A woman forces a bundle through and loses it inside. A man gets one arm between the leaves and Tanner frees it by striking his wrist with the flat of his hanger. The arm disappears.

The gates meet.

The crossbar falls into its sockets.

Outside, palms, fists and weapon stocks beat upon the timber. Voices call names in Portuguese, Marathi, Gujarati and English. Some demand entry. Some beg. Some merely try to tell the people inside where to find their children.

The guards look at Tanner.

He has obeyed.

Nobody thanks him.

### VI. The Account Within

The enemy does not assault the gate.

By sunset Sidi Yakut’s men hold Sewri, Parel, Mazagaon, the northern roads and most of the inhabited ground outside the Castle. They occupy stone houses for cover, establish positions around Dongri and begin measuring the English walls.

The people shut outside do not all die.

Some return north. Some cross the flats towards Portuguese ground. Some enter the Sidi’s lines and are searched, questioned or put aside. Some disappear into settlements whose inhabitants have survived Maratha, Mughal, Portuguese and English demands by knowing when not to be visible.

This does not absolve the gate.

Inside, the Castle contains more people than anyone can count.

The parade is covered with families, wounded soldiers, chests, baskets, animals and goods rescued by men who left neighbours behind. The six-pounder barrel lies beside the council stair without a carriage. Hobbs sits upon it and cleans mud from the vent with the corner of his shirt.

Vane moves through the crowd with Sillitoe, a narrow young writer whose ears seem too large for his fever-thinned face.

Count by category, Vane says.

There are no categories, Sillitoe answers.

Then make them.

The writer begins with soldiers.

Tanner goes to the gate.

The beating has stopped. Through the wood comes the lower sound of people speaking to one another. A baby cries. Farther away, an officer gives an order in a language Tanner cannot follow. Feet move north along the road.

Miguel stands beside him.

Rama’s woman and the boy are inside, Tanner says.

The priest waits.

The rest are not.

No.

Did I close too soon?

Miguel puts one hand against the timber.

If you had waited, more would be inside.

That is not an answer.

It is the only one the door permits.

From Dongri comes the first deliberate gun.

The ball strikes low on the northern wall. Lime dust falls from the arch. The people in the yard cry out together and then become silent.

Hobbs looks up from his vent tool.

There, he says. Now we know where they are.

Vane asks Sillitoe for the time.

The writer records the opening of the siege.

He does not record the names still outside.
