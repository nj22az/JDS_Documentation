# Part I: The Company’s War

## Chapter 5: The Price of Peace

> **Bombay, 1690**
>
> *“Restored is what a Company calls a thing after everyone who remembers it has died.”*
>
> — Tanner

### I. Twenty Demands

The Company prepares for surrender by increasing its demands.

On 27 November 1689, George Weldon and Abraham Navarro stand inside the landward gate beside five small horses, three iron-bound chests, two interpreters, one imperial intermediary and a guard of men whose uniforms no longer agree.

The chests contain money, gifts, letters and twenty proposed articles for future trade.

The Castle contains no sound horse fodder, little clean water and fewer men each week. The articles nevertheless ask for fixed customs, protected warehouses, free movement of bullion, prompt discharge of goods, control over English sailors, payment of debts, freedom to sell imported horses and privileges from Surat to Madras and Bengal.

Defeat has made the Company no less ambitious.

It has made the handwriting smaller.

Vane reads the inventory aloud while Sillitoe checks each object against a second copy.

Two horses for presentation at the imperial camp.

Three horses for travel and later disposal.

Coin for necessary charges.

Plate for gifts.

Cloth suitable for persons of rank.

The word bribe appears nowhere.

Tanner stands at the gate with twelve men. His scaled boots are dark from the damp salt-house floor. Hobbs’s quadrant rests inside his coat, wrapped in the dead gunner’s remaining shirt.

Weldon watches Vane count the coin for a third time.

If we miss the tide, we do not reach Kalyan before the escort changes.

The account must be exact.

The account will remain exact in the dark.

Navarro says nothing. He is a small, careful man who has spent enough years interpreting English certainty to know when speech becomes another expense. He checks the leather packet containing Child’s petition, then checks the knot.

Sir John Child comes down from the council house.

The governor walks with a stick. Illness and siege have reduced him, but not into the fevered tyrant gossip has begun constructing. He knows the articles. He knows which may be abandoned, which must appear to have been resisted and which concessions London will later pretend were never authorised.

He draws Weldon aside.

Tanner hears only the end.

Do not return with promises. Return with paper.

Weldon looks towards the wall where Sidi guns have opened two pale scars in the stone.

Paper is lighter.

That is why it travels.

The gate opens.

Nobody cheers.

The envoys pass between English guards, through the strip of ground still held by the Castle and into a war that has already become a negotiation everywhere except the gun batteries.

The horses pick their way around broken masonry. One carries a rolled tent. One carries the twenty demands. The two intended for presentation are in better condition than the animals remaining to haul Company guns.

The gate closes behind them.

Vane enters the exact time of departure.

Tanner watches the road until the party disappears beyond the first Sidi post.

How long? Hawkins asks.

Until London says we were never waiting.

### II. Progress

The envoys move. Their papers return faster.

A letter reaches Bombay four days after they enter Kalyan. It describes a town damaged by long war, arrangements with Mir Nazim and the currency accepted along the road. Vane reads the commercial observations twice and the ruined houses once.

Another letter reports their departure from Kalyan on 10 December.

Another reports mountain roads, fortified passes and delay.

On 19 December they reach the moving imperial camp without even a sound tent of their own. On the following day they are received standing by the chief minister, Asad Khan, in a small space made at the entrance to his tent. They deliver Child’s petition and the Surat governor’s letter. Gifts change custody. Business begins.

Every sentence must cross the Sidi’s lines, the mainland, the harbour and the Castle wall.

By the time it reaches Vane, it has acquired four endorsements and lost every admission of fear.

He pins copies to a board in the council room.

Hawkins reads them when assigned to the door.

It says they are well entertained.

Miguel looks over his shoulder.

It also says they have no tent.

Perhaps that is how ministers entertain merchants.

It says the King was angry but the minister has undertaken the business.

Then the minister wishes to be paid for carrying anger away.

Vane enters behind them.

You are not authorised to interpret diplomatic correspondence.

Miguel steps aside.

That has never prevented the correspondence from requiring interpretation.

The factor removes the letter from the board.

Inside the Castle, nothing changes.

The batteries continue. Rice falls below half issue. Men go over the wall. A fever moves from the salt-house to the barracks and returns under another name. The garrison debates whether the envoys have saved them, sold them or died upon the road.

Tanner guards the northern work.

He has learnt to recognise a successful negotiation by the absence of any immediate improvement.

Child remains in the council room for shorter periods. His hand shakes when he seals papers. Some mornings he cannot climb the stair. He still reads every dispatch and corrects figures in the margin.

Vane brings him a draft in January.

It describes the siege as the enemy’s temporary occupation of outlying ground.

Child strikes out temporary.

Not because it is false.

Because it has lasted too long to be useful.

### III. Four February

Sir John Child dies on 4 February 1690.

There is no final council, no collapse beneath the Great Seal and no speech that explains the war.

At the evening bell he is alive in a shuttered chamber overlooking the harbour. Before dawn the surgeon comes out carrying a basin and tells the two guards that the President no longer requires them.

Tanner is called because St John is ill and seniority has become a list of absent men.

He enters with Vane and Sillitoe.

Child lies beneath a cotton sheet. His wig is on its stand. His seal, keys and spectacle case have been arranged upon the table. The room looks more orderly than any place occupied by a living person inside the Castle.

Vane checks the pulse after the surgeon has already done so.

Time?

The surgeon gives it.

Cause?

The surgeon looks at the body, the closed shutters and the man asking.

Fever. Flux. Exhaustion. Choose the one London pays.

Vane does not react.

Sillitoe waits with his quill.

The death is entered upon three papers.

The first is for the council.

The second is for the Company.

The third is a private memorandum recording seals, keys, plate and papers transferred from the room.

None says that the man died while negotiators attempted to save a war authorised in London and enlarged in Bombay. None needs to. The date lies beside the dispatches.

The council meets before sunrise.

Its first decision is not the succession.

It is whether the envoys should be told.

If word reaches the imperial officers before the farman is secured, Vane says, they may treat the petition as void.

Miguel has not been invited but stands beside the door because the council requires his Portuguese and occasionally his conscience.

The petition belongs to the Company, he says.

It bears Child’s authority.

His authority ended upstairs.

The Company’s did not.

There it is again: an institution surviving by refusing the ordinary boundaries of a body.

The council sends no immediate notice to the imperial camp.

It does not conceal Child’s death inside the Castle. That would be impossible. The bell sounds. The guard is changed. The body is carried to the English burial ground beyond the southern work under an escort too small for ceremony and too public for secrecy.

People line the path.

Some remove their caps.

Some do not.

Anandi watches from the salt-house door with an empty water jar. Fernandes stands with his arm in a sling. Hawkins leans upon the matchlock Child’s war placed in his hands.

Tanner walks behind the body carrying no weapon.

He does not forgive Child.

He does not make him innocent merely because London will find it convenient to make him singular.

The governor chose, signed, threatened and persisted. He also received instructions, expectations and praise from men who will not be buried beneath the same guns.

Both accounts are true.

That is why neither will fit Vane’s page.

### IV. A Dead Man’s Removal

The terms arrive in pieces.

First comes word that the English submission has been accepted in principle.

Then comes the amount.

One hundred and fifty thousand rupees must be presented to the imperial treasury. Seized ships, money and goods must be restored or compensated. The English must return to quiet trade under established custom. Their factories may reopen when the obligations are met.

Sir John Child must be dismissed and expelled from India.

Vane reads that line aloud in the council room.

No one interrupts him.

Outside, a gun from Dongri fires. The windows move in their frames. Lime falls from a crack above Child’s empty chair.

Sillitoe looks towards the covered furniture.

How do we comply?

Vane lowers the paper.

The condition has been overtaken by fact.

Tanner stands at the door.

Then enter him as removed.

The factor’s eyes settle upon him.

The order was issued without knowledge of the President’s death.

The Emperor’s clerk and yours have agreed at last.

Vane returns to the text.

The joke does not survive copying.

Payment does.

Silver is gathered from the Company treasury, private advances, merchants’ balances and plate that has avoided earlier inventories. Bags are weighed, sealed, opened and weighed again. Men who have received no wages for months guard coin intended to restore the employer withholding them.

Tanner takes the first watch.

Hawkins takes the second.

During the third, Hawkins asks how many years of Miller’s pay lie in one bag.

Tanner does not know.

More than he received.

John Vaux is sent towards Surat to receive the farman and remains there as security until English obligations are performed. The Castle receives orders to restore property its ledgers have already consumed through storage, damage and charges.

Vane spends six days reversing profit.

The captured snow once valued at six hundred and eighty pounds is now a hull without cargo, tackle or sound planking. Three merchants claim it. Two produce papers. Hamid Rafiq identifies the marks beneath the Company numbers and names the interests Vane combined.

You cannot restore forty-one shares separately, Vane says.

Hamid looks at him.

You seized them separately.

The factor searches for an answer in law.

The young clerk waits with the patience of a man who has survived the salt-house.

The cargo cannot be restored, Vane says at last.

Then restore its price.

Its value deteriorated in custody.

Whose custody?

Vane closes the file.

Peace has not improved the question.

### V. Twenty-Two June

The siege ends after the peace.

The imperial orders are dated. The farmans are copied. The money moves. Goods are returned, substituted or argued into balances. The Sidi’s troops remain upon the islands while each obligation acquires proof.

Bombardment becomes less frequent but does not cease.

No one inside trusts quiet.

On 22 June, lookouts report movement at Mazagaon.

Tanner climbs the northern work in the scaled boots and raises Hobbs’s old glass. Men are leaving the stone houses. Boats gather at the eastern creeks. Loads move towards Parel. Smoke rises from damaged works and timbers that will not be carried away.

The English wait for a trick.

By noon the nearest trench is empty.

By evening the road beyond the gate belongs to nobody.

The crossbar is lifted.

It takes twelve men because damp and disuse have swollen the timber. When the leaves open, the air outside smells of ash, wet earth and vegetation reclaiming houses.

Tanner walks out first with Hawkins and six soldiers.

They pass the place where the crowd beat upon the gate. No mark identifies whose hands reached it. Farther north they find abandoned gabions, broken tools, cooking pits and a pair of English gun wheels taken from Mazagaon and used beneath another carriage.

The island has exchanged property more honestly than the prize court.

At the edge of the flats, Tanner asks after Rama.

Three people offer three answers.

He crossed to Portuguese ground.

He piloted boats for the Sidi.

He died near Parel.

Anandi hears all three and accepts none.

She walks north with the empty jar.

The Company begins its survey.

It counts walls, guns, houses, stores and men capable of standing guard. Barely seventy English soldiers remain fit to defend the fort. Around them are Indo-Portuguese militia, labourers, sailors and families whose survival will be reduced to supporting numbers.

Vane writes restoration at the top of the page.

### VI. Two Lists

Trade returns cautiously.

The first country boat enters under a white cloth and demands payment before unloading rice. The first merchant asks who now guarantees Company paper. The first carpenter presented with a ruined warehouse quotes twice his former rate.

Every one of them has understood the settlement.

Vane prepares the London account.

Hostilities concluded.

Privileges renewed.

Island restored.

Trade recommenced.

Garrison preserved under extraordinary difficulty.

He does not write victory.

He has found a word that performs the same work without inviting proof.

Tanner receives his account at the sea gate.

Four years of wages appear upon the left. Against them stand clothing, provisions, arms, medical issue, lost equipment and advances Tanner does not remember receiving. The balance is subject to confirmation in London.

The boots are not charged.

Sillitoe’s unserviceable entry has protected them from value.

And the dead men’s pay? Tanner asks.

Claims may be presented by lawful representatives.

Represented by whom?

Widows, executors or nominated kin.

Miller’s wife is in Ratcliff.

Address?

He gave it to the pay clerk.

The clerk died.

Then the supporting record may be incomplete.

Hobbs left the Pelican.

A tavern is not a lawful representative.

It was more lawful than his wages.

Tanner folds his account.

Miguel waits outside with a second list.

It contains names from the salt-house board: soldiers, sailors, prisoners, militia, boatmen, servants, women and children. Beside some, he has written a street, parish, vessel or person in England. Beside others there is only a remembered sentence.

Fernandes spoke of a brother at Goa.

Miller asked whether the fleet had come.

Hobbs left the quadrant to the Pelican.

Rama remains without a final mark.

What am I to do with this? Tanner asks.

Tell somebody who cannot alter it.

There is no such person.

Then tell a room.

Later in 1690, Tanner secures passage aboard a homeward-bound Indiaman carrying repaired cargo, invalids, letters and explanations. His chest contains three shirts, a hanger, a cracked compass, Hobbs’s quadrant and both lists.

The official list is sealed.

Miguel’s is not.

The carpenter’s mate who helps lower the chest into the berth deck is a broad, fair man named Anders Blom. He comes from Göteborg, has served beneath Dutch and English masters and speaks each language as though it has cheated him differently.

He notices the quadrant.

Gunner?

Dead.

Family?

The Pelican in Wapping.

Anders nods.

I know it.

Good house?

No.

He sets the chest upon the deck.

Honest house?

No house full of sailors is honest.

Then why go?

Because a lie told there must survive witnesses.

Above them the capstan turns. Bombay’s cargo enters the hold by tackle, number and mark. The Company has called the island restored before the ship has cleared the roadstead.

Tanner looks down at the scaled foreign hide around his ankles.

He carries no imperial speech, no grand lesson from a throne and no belief that defeat has made the Company modest.

He carries a dead gunner’s instrument and the names the ledger could not use.

The ship turns west.

The year turns while the ship is at sea.

### VII. The Pelican

Months of ordinary danger carry them home.

Nothing upon the passage deserves a dispatch. A topmast springs off the Cape and is fished with timber from a spare yard. A cask breaks loose in the lower hold and kills a rat rather than a man. Fever takes three invalids already counted as unlikely to survive. Anders finds a working in the forward ceiling where one of the futtocks has begun to move and drives a wedge while the ship complains around him.

He does not call it Swedish work, Dutch work or English work.

He calls it work that holds.

Tanner serves his watch, keeps Miguel’s paper dry and learns enough of Anders to understand why the man knows Wapping.

His father cut timber above the Göta River. Anders went to sea in a Dutch flute because Amsterdam paid before Göteborg did. He learnt English in a Portsmouth gaol after choosing the wrong employer during the last war and improved it aboard ships whose captains preferred foreign hands at foreign wages.

The sea has made his nationality an item negotiated at each pay table.

When the Indiaman reaches the Thames, she does not sail grandly to the City.

She takes her berth in Blackwall Reach among yards, cranes, repair docks, victualling boats and craft that gather around a homeward ship before her anchor is settled. Company officers arrive first. Families wait behind them. Searchers come aboard to find private trade concealed beneath bedding, inside tool handles and under false chest bottoms.

Tanner’s chest is opened.

Three shirts.

One hanger.

One cracked compass.

One brass quadrant.

Two lists.

The searcher lifts the unsealed paper.

What account?

Dead men.

The man lets it fall as though death itself pays no duty.

The official list interests him more because it bears a Company seal. He turns it over, confirms that it is addressed to Leadenhall Street and returns it unopened.

Tanner is permitted to carry the Company’s dead through London because another Company office expects to lose them differently.

His wages are not paid at the ship.

They are subject to examination.

His chest is lowered into a lighter with passengers’ baggage, two sick men, four cages of fowl and a clerk who objects each time spray touches his shoes. Anders comes with him. His carpenter’s tools have been retained for inspection, leaving him poorer by the exact instruments through which he earns money.

The lighter works upriver on the flood.

Blackwall passes first, then Poplar, Limehouse and the long working edge of Ratcliff. Stairs descend between houses. Cranes lean over the water. Shipwrights’ blows travel across the river with the clean delay of sound over distance. Every reach smells different: pitch, beer, hides, coal smoke, bilge, wet timber and the mud exposed beneath all of it.

Tanner has imagined Wapping during four years of heat.

He has imagined cold rain, dark beer and English voices saying nothing important. He has not imagined how narrow it will seem after Bombay, nor how many windows can overlook one piece of water.

At Wapping Stairs, Anders helps him lift the chest onto the landing.

The tide remains beneath their boots.

Where now? Tanner asks.

Anders points with his chin towards a painted bird hanging above a door in the riverside row.

There.

The Pelican has survived by alteration.

Its front room is older than its fittings and darker than the afternoon outside. A long oak counter divides drink from debt. The hearth smokes when the wind comes east. Beneath the river windows the boards rise and settle with the damp. Men occupy stools as though each has inherited the exact distance between himself and the next man.

Behind the bar stands Esther Finch.

She is not yet the woman the Every reward will attempt to purchase. She is a keeper with rooms to fill, creditors to delay and an injured husband who still believes yard work will become safer if the right man complains.

Elias Finch sits at the end of the counter sharpening a carpenter’s pencil with a rigging knife. He looks first at Anders’s empty tool belt, then at Tanner’s boots, then at the chest. A working man’s order of inquiry.

Homeward? Esther asks.

Bombay.

The room hears the word.

Not loudly. A head lifts near the fire. A dice cup stops against the table. Somebody at the river window turns enough to look without admitting curiosity.

Bombay has already arrived in London as letters, prices and a restored settlement.

Tanner is the first part of it in the room that has a face.

Esther sets down two mugs.

Tanner places no money beside them.

Pay table? she asks.

Examination.

Then the Company drinks water until it has examined you.

She pushes the beer towards him.

Anders raises his mug.

Good house.

You said it was not.

I said it was not honest.

Esther looks between them.

He is learning the distinction, Anders says.

Tanner opens the chest.

The room does not gather. That restraint is why he continues.

He takes out Hobbs’s shirt, unwraps the brass quadrant and sets the instrument upon the bar. The metal has darkened during the voyage. One edge bears three shallow cuts made by the gunner’s missing fingers whenever he folded it closed.

Master Gunner Jonathan Hobbs, Tanner says. He asked for this house.

Elias puts down the knife. He tests the hinge, sights along the arc and moves the index arm.

Sound.

He was.

Esther touches neither instrument nor sentiment.

Who receives it?

The house.

That answer changes something in her face. Not surprise. Recognition of an obligation that has arrived without asking whether she can afford it.

She bends, finds the narrow dry fault beneath the old oak bar and places the quadrant inside. The fit is not exact. Houses, like ships, make room by losing something else.

Tanner gives her Miguel’s list.

Not the sealed return?

That goes to men who know the totals.

And this?

For somebody who can keep the names.

Esther unfolds the first part.

She reads Miller aloud.

Then Hobbs.

At António Fernandes she stops.

Dead?

Alive when I left.

Then why is he here?

Because it is not a list of the dead.

What is it?

Tanner looks around the Pelican: the bar worn by forearms, the smoke-dark beams, the river door, the rooms above and the strangers who have heard Bombay without demanding a victory.

The bill.

Esther refolds the paper along Miguel’s creases and puts it beside the quadrant.

The official list continues towards Leadenhall Street in the morning.

The other account has reached its destination.

The Pelican has heard of Tanner now.

It gives him a room before the Company gives him his wages.
