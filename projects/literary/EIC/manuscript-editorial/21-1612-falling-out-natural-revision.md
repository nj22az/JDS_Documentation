## XV. With or Without Me

Carter comes in while Jack is practising a rolling hitch on the leg of the centre table.

He sees the boy.

Then Tom.

Then Maggie.

The chandler removes his gloves slowly.

The boy is not mine, he says.

Maggie looks at him.

Only the debt.

His father spoiled twelve lengths of Riga pine.

His father was under them when they fell.

And the yard paid him through the quarter while he healed.

Jack's hands stop on the rope.

Carter notices.

The debt is four pounds, he says, more quietly.

Tom has already arranged it.

Jack ships on the Company's next vessel under Tom. Half his wage goes against the yard debt. Tom's own instruction allowance—two shillings a month—is to go against it as well until the four pounds are cleared.

Carter looks at Tom.

Your allowance?

Yes.

Why?

Because I am the one saying the boy should go.

Jack says, I said I would go.

Tom looks at him.

I know.

Maggie has not moved.

How long? she asks Carter.

At the two deductions? A year if the voyage pays clean.

If it does not?

Longer.

Jack tightens the hitch until the rope complains against the table leg.

I can do a year.

Maggie looks at him.

Do you want the sea?

Jack glances at Tom.

Maggie says, Not him. Me.

The boy looks back at her.

I want the wage.

That was not my question.

Jack's jaw sets.

I want to get out of the rooms before Carter's steward takes them.

Still not my question.

A little colour reaches his face.

Then yes. I want the sea.

Maggie nods once.

Tom relaxes by an amount only she notices.

He thinks the hard part is over.

He takes Jack's articles from inside his coat.

Already drawn.

Wage entered.

Debt deduction entered.

Instruction allowance entered in Tom's hand where the purser told him it belonged.

Only Jack's mark is missing.

Tom smooths the paper on the bar.

Have you a pen?

Maggie looks down.

Tom follows her eyes.

The paper lies on the old oak above the fault.

Above Bell's page.

Above the thimble he left nine years earlier.

Tom's hand remains on the corner.

Maggie says, Take it off.

What?

Take their paper off my bar.

Tom lifts it at once.

Not defensively.

Simply because she asked.

He puts it on the table instead.

Jack looks between them.

I can mark it at the hiring room.

Yes, Maggie says.

Tom says, It changes nothing where he marks it.

Maggie turns to him.

No?

It is the same paper.

I know what it is.

Then what are we arguing about?

Maggie wipes the place where the articles lay.

Nothing was spilled.

Her rag passes over it anyway.

Tom watches.

He will sign with or without me, Maggie.

There it is.

What?

The sentence you use when you want a choice to disappear.

Tom frowns.

It is true.

Maybe.

It is.

Maggie puts the rag down.

There is always a *with or without me*.

Tom says nothing.

She points at Jack.

With you, he has a boatswain watching him, two shillings more against the debt and somebody who knows his name.

Yes.

Without you, he may still go.

Yes.

Those are not the same thing.

That is what I am saying.

No. You are using the worse one to make the first one clean.

Tom's face changes.

Only slightly.

Maggie sees it.

He is not angry yet. He is hurt that she has mistaken what he thought he was showing her.

I am trying to keep him alive.

I know.

Then what would you have me do?

That was not my question.

Tom lets out a breath.

Maggie—

No. Answer mine first.

He waits.

She asks, When did you ask him what he wanted?

Tom looks at Jack.

Before I spoke to the purser.

Jack says, He did.

Maggie's eyes stay on Tom.

Before Carter's debt?

No.

Before you knew there was a berth?

No.

Tom understands where she is going and dislikes it because the facts do not help him.

He needed a way out.

So you found one.

Yes.

A way you know.

Yes.

A way that puts him under you.

Tom's voice hardens.

Under me is better than under a man who beats him because he cannot splice.

Maybe.

Not maybe.

Maggie folds her arms.

There. Better.

Tom looks at her.

What does that mean?

Say better. Say safer. Say you can teach him. Say you will spend your own wage on the debt.

I have said all of that.

Then stop saying you had no choice.

Tom looks away.

Carter has become extremely interested in his gloves.

Jack pulls the hitch loose and begins again.

Tom says, If I walk away, the debt remains.

Yes.

The ship remains.

Yes.

The boy may sign anyway.

Yes.

So what changes?

You.

The word lands harder than it should.

Tom looks back at her.

Maggie does not soften it.

You change, every time you tell yourself the next thing would happen anyway.

He laughs once without humour.

You think I should leave him to it so I can stay pure in your bar?

No.

What then?

Know what you are doing while you do it.

I know exactly what I am doing.

Maggie looks at the articles on the table.

That is what frightens me.

Silence.

Tom's mouth tightens.

He has been shouted at by captains and factors, cursed by men twice his age and once shot at by Portuguese sailors who at least had the courtesy to mean exactly what the shot meant.

This is worse because Maggie is speaking quietly.

Jack says, I asked him to take me.

Maggie looks at the boy.

I know.

Then do not blame him for it.

I am not.

Sounds like it.

Maggie accepts that.

Perhaps it does.

Jack looks at Tom.

I can sign at the hiring room.

Tom nods.

Tomorrow.

Jack gathers the articles.

Maggie says, Leave them.

The boy stops.

Why?

Because you have not finished your hitch.

Jack looks at the rope.

Then at the paper.

For one second he is sixteen again rather than a debt with hands.

He puts the articles on the stool and returns to the table leg.

Maggie goes behind the bar.

Tom says, You have made your point.

No.

No?

I have made several. You only liked the first one.

His temper arrives at last.

I brought him here because this is the one place I thought would not look at him and see cargo.

Maggie's face goes still.

Then why did you bring the articles already written?

Tom has no quick answer.

Because the berth will go.

There are other berths.

Not good ones.

There are no good ones, Tom.

Then what would you have me do?

I do not know.

The admission stops him.

Maggie looks tired now.

Not righteous.

Not certain.

Tired.

I do not know how to fix Carter's four pounds. I do not know how to keep Jack's rooms. I do not know how to make a ship safe. I know how to feed him before he signs and ask him twice whether he wants what is being sold to him.

Tom says, That does not clear the debt.

No.

Does not get him a wage.

No.

Does not keep a bad boatswain off his back.

No.

He spreads his hands.

Then it is not enough.

Maggie looks at him for a long time.

No, she says. It is not.

That should end the argument.

Instead it breaks something in it.

Tom had come prepared to defend his arrangement against someone who thought she possessed a cleaner answer.

Maggie has none.

She only refuses to call his answer clean.

Tom turns away first.

His eyes find the counter.

The place where the paper lay has already dried beneath Maggie's rag.

He says, I thought you would understand.

Maggie reaches beneath the bar.

Tom hears the small scrape of metal against oak before he sees what is in her hand.

The brass thimble.

She puts it on the counter between them.

Not thrown.

Not offered.

Set down.

Tom looks at it.

Maggie says, I understood you better when you left this here.

That is unfair.

Yes.

He looks up sharply.

Maggie does not withdraw it.

Yes, she says again. It is.

That honesty is worse than denial would have been.

Tom's face closes.

Take it, Maggie says.

No.

It is yours.

It was my mother's.

I know.

Then why—

Because I do not want to keep a promise you have decided already came true.

Tom stares at her.

I came back.

Maggie's eyes fill and do not spill.

Yes.

The word leaves no room to stand in.

Jack has stopped tying the hitch.

Carter is no longer pretending not to listen.

Tom reaches for the thimble.

His fingers stop an inch above it.

Then close into a fist.

He leaves it where it is.

Maggie says, Go.

Tom looks at her.

For tonight?

Maggie's hand tightens around the edge of the counter.

Go, Tom.

He waits one second too long.

Not because he expects her to change her mind.

Because he has never before had to understand the Pelican door from this side.

Then he takes his coat.

Jack says, Tom—

Tomorrow, Tom says. Hiring room. Bring the papers.

He opens the door.

Cold river air moves through the room.

Maggie does not say his name.

Tom does not look back.

The door closes.

For a while nobody speaks.

Jack is first.

He looks at Maggie.

You were hard on him.

Yes.

You were wrong about some of it.

Yes.

He was trying to help me.

I know.

Jack waits for her to add something.

She does not.

Carter picks up his gloves.

What about the debt?

Maggie looks at him.

What about it?

If Jack signs, I will take the half wage as agreed.

And Tom's allowance?

If Fletcher still directs it there, I will take it.

Maggie looks at Jack.

Do you want that?

Jack thinks.

Yes.

Then it is yours to agree to.

Carter nods.

He leaves.

Jack collects the articles from the stool.

At the door he turns.

Will he come back tomorrow?

Maggie looks at the thimble.

He said he would.

Jack leaves.

The room empties slowly after that.

When Maggie is alone she takes the thimble from the counter.

Holds it in her palm.

Then puts it back into the fault beneath the bar.

She wipes the counter once.

Stops.

Puts the rag down.
