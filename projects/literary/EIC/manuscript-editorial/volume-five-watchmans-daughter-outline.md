# Volume Five — *The Watchman's Daughter*: full-novel outline (target ~100,000 words)

Expansion of the deployed 13,220-word compact edition
(`12-1888-the-watchmans-daughter`) into a full novel, per the author decision
that every volume be as extensive as *The Venture*.

**RESTRUCTURED.** Drafting began under a 4-part, ~26-chapter outline and
produced 13 short chapters (~22,000 words, averaging ~1,700 words each) — a
rhythm that would have landed the book around 45–50k, not 100k. The author was
interviewed on four forced choices and the answers restructured the whole
architecture. This document reflects the **current, active structure**.

## The organizing principle (author, this session)

**Su is the whole world of this book.** Unlike Book One, which follows an
ensemble (Tom, Maggie, Maria, Daniel, Arthur, Jack) with several independently
dramatized arcs, Volume Five stays single-gravity throughout:

- **The East India Company returns to the page only through Dr. Cray.** No
  broader Company canvas, no second Company-adjacent protagonist. His Bengal
  service, his calm, his case — that is the whole of how the machine re-enters
  this book. (The dockworker's Mutiny testimony in Ch.5§IV is in-bounds because
  it exists *to illuminate Cray*, heard by Su, in service of her investigation
  — not a digression into the dockworker's own independent life.)
- **The Prospect is vicinity, not a second cast.** Flo, the dockworker, the
  potboy, the regulars — they are texture and support around Su, rendered
  through her attention and her decisions. None of them gets an independent
  flashback chapter or section built around *their* history for its own sake
  (the kind of thing Book One does for Maria or Daniel). If a secondary
  character appears at length, it must be in service of something Su is
  doing, deciding, or learning — never a chapter that leaves her to follow
  somebody else.
- This **supersedes** the earlier "Flo vignette, second vignette" plan from the
  restructuring interview (a standalone Flo-backstory section). Chapter 8
  keeps Flo present and warm but does not dramatize her own life apart from
  Su's.

## The four locked decisions

1. **Time span: full-life, like Book One.** Not a single autumn plus a short
   coda — Su's story runs from the family's crossing (Hong Kong, 1868) through
   her old age as an established master, mirroring Book One's multi-decade,
   multi-arc structure.
2. **Chapter shape: 12 chapters, plus an epilogue** — longer, sectioned
   chapters (Book One's own convention: numbered `## I.` / `## II.` sections
   within a chapter, e.g. "Tom at Surat" §I–§XII), not many short ones.
   Averages ~7,000–9,500 words/chapter, matching Book One's own average.
3. **Investigation: Cray remains the sole real candidate.** No added suspects
   or red herrings — the claustrophobia stays tight and focused.
4. **Secondary cast: a few vignette/flashback sections**, Book-One-style,
   folded in as sections **within** the relevant chapters (not standalone
   chapters), so the 12-chapter shape holds.

## Premise (unchanged from the compact edition)

Su Zhang, daughter of a Chinese chandler-and-laundry family on the Limehouse
Causeway, trained since seven in her family's real Southern kung fu — **Hung Ga
and Wing Chun** — and its four rules — **never first, never for show, never for
payment, never in anger** — lives through the Autumn of Terror of 1888. Two of
the laundry's own customers become the third and fourth names on the killer's
list. A beloved Prospect fixture, Dr. Reginald Cray — a retired East India
Company surgeon — is either the season's monster or only a monster, and the
novel, like Su, never finds out which. The Company that washed her family out
of Canton and taught Cray to be calm is thirty years dead; its consequences
arrive at the bar. The book now also carries her life onward from that autumn:
marriage, teaching, the lineage completing.

## The art is real — Hung Ga and Wing Chun

The family style is authentic Southern Chinese kung fu carried out of Canton:
**Hung Ga (Hung Kuen, 洪拳)** and **Wing Chun (詠春)** — both Cantonese arts,
exactly right for a mid-century Canton master displaced by the Opium War.

- **Hung Ga** — the horse stance held until the legs shake and then stop
  shaking; forearms conditioned until bone learns what it can bear; the plain
  wooden **fan** as both correction tool and bonesetter's instrument (*dit da*
  / 跌打). The family's *hard* foundation.
- **Wing Chun** — the "**listening**" through two crossed wrists is **chi sao
  (sticking hands, 黐手)**: reading force through touch, ending a fight in the
  fewest moves. The family's *close* work, and what Su uses in the alley.
- **"Kung fu" = the practice.** *Gong fu* (功夫) literally means skill won
  through time and effort — Wei's flat word "**the practice**" is simply *kung
  fu* in English, his register, not a euphemism. He uses the true names in
  Cantonese and makes Su give them back in English.

**Su becomes a master.** Grandfather (Canton) → Wei (Hong Kong, then London) →
**Su**. She begins the book her father's student and ends it a **kung fu
master in her own right**, the lineage passed to her — now dramatized across
real years (Ch.11–12), not summarized in a short coda.

## The method: dilation and life-span, not invention

No events are invented that weren't already implied by the compact edition or
the character bible — the year (and now the life) is lived at full length:

1. **The ordinary world first** (done, Ch.1–4) — summer 1888, before the
   killings, so the reader loves Elisabeth and Catherine before the list takes
   them.
2. **The generational strand** (done, Ch.3) — Canton → the Opium War → Hong
   Kong → the crossing → the white bowl → the boy who said *Run*.
3. **Lee's death** (done, Ch.4) — recontextualizes Cray before the investigation
   needs him to be ambiguous.
4. **The terror closing in** (done, Ch.5–6) — the watch committees, the
   foreigner-hunts, the double killing, the yard's "stops things, does not
   avenge them."
5. **The investigation-that-isn't** (Ch.7–8, to draft) — the case, the shirt,
   nobody to tell, never first, dilated, with the Flo vignette folded in.
6. **The alley and its aftermath** (Ch.9–10, to draft) — the set-piece and its
   resolution; Cray kept ambiguous throughout (rumour and record only).
7. **The full-life turn** (Ch.11–12 + epilogue, to draft) — Erik, the decades,
   the lineage completing, old age. This is the new material that fulfils the
   "full-life, like Book One" decision.

## Hard guards (this book is delicate)

- **The ambiguity is sacred.** The novel never confirms Cray was the Ripper.
  *It fits and it does not prove* is the spine. **We never enter Cray's head to
  settle it.** The case goes into the river unopened and stays there.
- **The real victims are people, not spectacle.** Elizabeth Stride and Catherine
  Eddowes are customers, a song, an uncollected bodice — humanised, never
  murder-porn. The murders themselves stay off-page or at the edge of report.
- **POV rule (deployed, live):** Su knows the two women only as **Elisabeth**
  and **Catherine** — never the street names (*Long Liz*, *Kate*, *Eddowes*,
  which belong strictly to the world/the papers), never their trade until the
  murder column tells her, too late.
- **Fiction laid against history, not a claim to solve a real case.** The
  drowning is invented and the story knows it; no real person is accused.
- **Cray remains the sole real candidate** — no red herrings, per the author's
  restructuring decision.
- **The four rules govern the prose** as they govern Su: under-say everything;
  let objects carry weight (the fan, the white bowl, the flower, the bodice,
  the river).
- **Vol V's permitted drift** (six-volume plan): free to follow Su through
  Whitechapel, Limehouse, Shadwell and the docks; the Prospect remains her home
  world but is no longer a compulsory container. The Company is **legacy
  only** — the engine that shaped Wei and Cray, dead thirty years, its
  consequences arriving now.
- **Two languages, kept in separate rooms** — Cantonese and English never
  blur; a rule said in only one language is only half known.
- **The kung fu is authentic, never chop-socky.** Hung Ga and Wing Chun are
  real, named, and depicted with respect: correct technique (chi sao, the
  horse stance, bridges, *dit da* bonesetting), real lineage transmission, no
  wire-fu, no exoticising. The one earnest use in the main timeline (the alley)
  reads as mastery under discipline, not spectacle.
- **Su is the whole world of this book** (see principle above). The Company
  returns only through Cray; the Prospect is vicinity, not a second cast. The
  dockworker's Mutiny testimony (Ch.5§IV, done) stays in-bounds because it
  exists to illuminate Cray, heard by Su, in service of her investigation.
  No secondary character gets an independent backstory chapter or section
  built around their own life for its own sake.

## Principal cast

- **Su Zhang** — the watchman's daughter; POV; ends the book a master.
- **Wei Zhang** — her father; chandler, laundryman, Hung Ga/Wing Chun master
  and *dit da* bonesetter; aging hands; the lineage-holder who passes it to
  Su. Dies in the full-life expansion (Ch.11).
- **Sau-Ling Zhang** — her mother; sent for from Hong Kong; the mission-church
  creed of the open ledger; boils the sleeve.
- **Lee Zhang** — the brother, dead at seven (the Cray entry).
- **The grandfather** — Canton kung fu master (Hung Kuen and Wing Chun), the
  fan's first owner, the white-bowl world (flashback); his school broken by
  the Opium War.
- **Dr. Reginald Cray** — retired Company surgeon; the beloved fixture; the
  ambiguity; the sole candidate.
- **Elisabeth (Long Liz — Elizabeth Stride)**, **Catherine (Kate — Catherine
  Eddowes)** — customers, then the third and fourth names. POV rule above.
- **Flo** — Prospect barmaid; the flower cutting; her own vignette (Ch.8).
- **The dockworker** — Mutiny veteran; his own vignette (Ch.5§IV) plants
  Cray's shadow two months early.
- **Erik Johansson** — the Swede off the timber ships; the full-life
  expansion's courtship and marriage (Ch.11); the cycle tie (Trinidad/Calcutta
  indenture line).
