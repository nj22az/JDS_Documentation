# Volume Five — *The Watchman's Daughter*: full-novel outline (target ~100,000 words)

Expansion of the deployed 13,220-word compact edition
(`12-1888-the-watchmans-daughter`) into a full novel, per the author decision
that every volume be as extensive as *The Venture*. This is the architecture;
prose pilots follow.

## Premise (unchanged from the compact edition)

Su Zhang, nineteen, daughter of a Chinese chandler-and-laundry family on the
Limehouse Causeway, trained since seven in her family's real Southern kung fu —
**Hung Ga and Wing Chun** — and its four rules — **never first, never for show,
never for payment, never in anger** — lives through the Autumn of Terror of 1888. Two of the laundry's own
customers become the third and fourth names on the killer's list. A beloved
Prospect fixture, Dr. Reginald Cray — a retired East India Company surgeon — is
either the season's monster or only a monster, and the novel, like Su, never
finds out which. The Company that washed her family out of Canton and taught
Cray to be calm is thirty years dead; its consequences arrive at the bar.

## The art is real — Hung Ga and Wing Chun

The compact edition kept the martial art unnamed ("*the practice*"). The novel
makes it **real and named**. The family style is authentic Southern Chinese kung
fu carried out of Canton: **Hung Ga (Hung Kuen, 洪拳)** and **Wing Chun (詠春)** —
both Cantonese arts, exactly right for a mid-century Canton master displaced by
the Opium War. The deployed text already describes real technique; the novel
simply names it and gets it right:

- **Hung Ga** — the horse stance held until the legs shake and then stop shaking;
  forearms conditioned on the wooden rail until bone learns what it can bear;
  tiger-crane bridges; iron-body work; the plain wooden **fan** as both correction
  tool and bonesetter's instrument (Hung Ga masters were often the lane's
  bonesetter — *dit da* / 跌打). This is the family's *hard* foundation.
- **Wing Chun** — the "**listening**" through two crossed wrists is **chi sao
  (sticking hands, 黐手)**: reading force through touch, the centreline, close-range
  trapping, ending a fight in the fewest moves. This is the family's *close* work,
  and it is what Su uses in the alley — she steps inside a long man's reach where
  he has no leverage and lets his own arm tell her where it is going.
- **"Kung fu" = the practice.** *Gong fu* (功夫) literally means skill won through
  time and effort. So Wei's flat, humble word "**the practice**" is simply *kung
  fu* in English — his register, not a euphemism. He refuses the sailors'
  exoticising "Chinese boxing"; he uses the true names (*Hung Kuen*, *Wing Chun*)
  in Cantonese and makes Su give them back in English, because a name known in
  only one language is only half known.

**Su becomes a master.** The novel's spine is a lineage: grandfather (Canton) →
Wei (Hong Kong, then London) → **Su**. She begins the book her father's student
and ends it, unmistakably, a **kung fu master in her own right** — the art has
become hers, tested in earnest, and the lineage passes to her. The **fan** is the
lineage token; by the coda it is Su's to carry and Su's to pass on. The
watchman's daughter becomes the watchman — and the master.

## The method: dilation, not invention

The compact edition is the **map**; the novel is the **territory**. Its 13
sections are the skeleton. Almost every paragraph currently *summarises* scenes
the novel *dramatises*. No new events are invented — the year is simply lived at
full length. Where the ~87,000 new words come from:

1. **Open the ordinary world first** (~18k) — the novel begins in **summer
   1888**, before the killings, so the reader loves Liz and Kate before the list
   takes them. Dramatic irony carries Part One.
2. **The generational strand** (~18k) — Canton → the Opium War → Hong Kong →
   the crossing → the white bowl → the boy who said *Run* → the rules.
3. **Lee's death** (~6k) — the brother, the fever, Cray's counted fee.
4. **The Prospect + Limehouse cast** (~12k) — Flo, the Mutiny-veteran dockworker,
   the potboy, the Causeway's Chinese community, the sailors' home, the watch
   committees, the foreigner-hunts.
5. **The investigation-that-isn't** (~15k) — the case, the shirt, nobody to
   tell, never first, dilated.
6. **Cray, kept ambiguous** (~8k) — his Bengal/Company past through **testimony
   and record, never his own head**.
7. **The autumn + the after-years** (~10k) — the 1888 chronology as pressure;
   then the fuller coda (Erik Johansson, the Trinidad/Calcutta line, the river).

## Structure — four parts, ~26 chapters

Word budgets are targets; each chapter names the compact-edition section (§) it
draws from, and flags **[new]** material.

### PART ONE — The Ordinary World (summer 1888) — ~25k

1. **The Dawn Yard** (~3.5k) — §IV. Su and Wei at the Practice before the counter
   opens; the horse stance, the fan, the crossed wrists, *listening*; the white
   bowl on the high shelf; the shop waking. Establishes Su's watchfulness and the
   family's two systems of debt.
2. **The Causeway** (~3.5k) — **[new]**. The Limehouse Chinese world: a few
   hundred souls, the sailors' home, the Cantonese kept in a separate room from
   the English; Sau-Ling's mission-church faith and her creed-in-waiting; the
   walk from Causeway to the Prospect's custom.
3. **The Prospect** (~4k) — §I, §V. The tavern; Flo, the dockworker, the potboy;
   the short-legged chair left for last; **Dr. Cray introduced as the beloved
   fixture** — the docker's-shoulder scene and the *level nothing* on his face.
4. **Long Liz** (~3.5k) — §II. Elizabeth Stride on a Monday; two ocean-crossers;
   grief with floors and walls; the *Princess Alice* story that isn't true and is
   load-bearing; the quarter-hour of being understood.
5. **Kate** (~3.5k) — §II. Catherine Eddowes; *duchess*; the songs; the quay song
   with no back half; the hopping plans; "mind yourself down there."
6. **The White Bowl** (~3.5k) — §IV. Wei's crossing begins in the telling: the
   tea-stall woman, the bowl, *bring it back yourself*. The generational strand
   opens.
7. **Lee** (~3.5k) — §V. The dead brother; the fever; Cray at his supper; the
   counted fee her mother cannot forgive; the family's private entry, eight
   winters old. The first shadow.

### PART TWO — The Terror Comes (Aug–Sept 1888) — ~25k

8. **The Watch Committees** (~3.5k) — §I. Su walked home by the tradesmen;
   deciding, calmly, which to put down first; "that is what the autumn has done."
9. **Leather Apron** (~3.5k) — §I, §VIII. The first killings (31 Aug, 8 Sept); the
   East End learns the new gait; the papers name the fear.
10. **The Hands in the Doorway** (~4k) — **[new]** + §VIII. The foreigner-hunts;
    windows boarded; Wei standing at closing with his hands folded where the
    street can see them; the community under suspicion.
11. **Run** (~4k) — §IV. The full crossing: the harbourmaster, the tide lost, the
    ruin, the boy who read him boots-to-bundle and said *Run*, the sampan, the
    steerage meal, the frightened cabin boy, the bowl found in the bundle. Rule
    three, dramatised whole.
12. **The Thirtieth of September** (~4k) — §III. The double event; Long Liz and
    Kate; Su hears *Eddowes* read aloud in the Prospect; her mother over Kate's
    uncollected parcel and the creed (the open ledger); the father and the stiff
    high bolt. **Part Two climax.**
13. **The Rules for This** (~3k) — §IV close. The yard, the morning after; the
    banked heat; "the rules don't change because you are bleeding"; stopping vs
    avenging; the knife's-width difference.

### PART THREE — The Banked Heat (Oct–Nov 1888) — ~30k

14. **A Terrible Autumn** (~3.5k) — §V close. Cray's condolence; the two long
    Company echoes met in one room; the aging-hands motif deepened.
15. **The Case** (~3.5k) — §VI. The clasp comes loose; the oilcloth shape; the
    coin warm from his pocket; memory returning the razor cut and *a man my age
    should keep a steadier hand*.
16. **What the Doctor Saw** (~4k) — §VI + **[new, ambiguous]**. The asking-around;
    the Mutiny-veteran dockworker on Company surgeons who certified flogging-deaths
    as fever; Cray's Bengal past as rumour and record — never his own head.
17. **The Shirt** (~3.5k) — §VII. The one shirt in fifteen years, sent the week
    after the clasp; the question put as linen; Su starving it of an answer.
18. **Nobody to Tell** (~4k) — §VIII. The station steps; the Polish Jew walked in
    bleeding; the arithmetic of a Chinese girl accusing an English gentleman;
    telling no one — not Flo, not her mother, and deliberately not her father,
    *because he would believe her*.
19. **Never First** (~3.5k) — §IX. The discipline of doing nothing correctly;
    carrying the grandfather's fan in her sleeve; the twice-felt pressure on the
    neck; scratching out Lee's name every time.
20. **The Second Half of the Song** (~3.5k) — **[new]** + §XIII seeds. Grief
    settling into the household; Kate's bodice wrapped in the back room; Liz's
    absent Mondays; the Causeway's mourning; the small mercies.
21. **The Fog Comes Down** (~3.5k) — §IX close, §X open. The city thickening; the
    boarding-house errand at the far end of Wapping; the shortcut behind the
    sufferance wharf; the sentence of her life narrowing to one night.

### PART FOUR — The Alley and the River (late Nov 1888 + after) — ~20k

22. **The Alley** (~4.5k) — §X. The scuffle; the woman freed by a dropped basket;
    the first wrist "like green wood"; the waiting that unmakes him; the lancet;
    the third rush; the river taking him with a question on his face. **The
    set-piece.**
23. **The Clasp** (~3.5k) — §XI. The case in the mud; the hand on the clasp for a
    long time; taking the hand off — *why* is weightless, *what* has the weight;
    the case into the Thames, unopened, weighted with the shop's chain.
24. **The Boiled Sleeve** (~3k) — §XI close. Home before first light; the mother
    already at the copper; boiling the blood past testimony; the largest sum of
    both their lives entered wordless in a book Su was never taught to read.
25. **No Sixth** (~3.5k) — §XII. The winter of theories; Cray reported missing;
    the river giving back its drowned on its own terms; *it fits and it does not
    prove*, forever.
26. **What the River Kept** (~4k) — §XIII + **[mastery]**. The coda: the yard
    ("your weight is back on your heels — fix it"); Flo's flower cutting; the song
    with no back; Long Liz understood at last; **Erik Johansson**, the marriage
    into a language with floors and walls, the Trinidad/Calcutta indenture line,
    the river Sundays, Kate's bodice kept in its paper forever. And the lineage
    completes: Wei's hands fail by degrees, and the dawn yard becomes **Su's** —
    the fan passes to her, she is the master now, and in the last movement she is
    teaching the form to the next pair of small hands (her own child, or a
    Causeway child left at the shop), before dawn, the way it has always been
    taught. The watchman's daughter is the watchman. The account left open on
    purpose.

## Hard guards (this book is delicate)

- **The ambiguity is sacred.** The novel never confirms Cray was the Ripper.
  *It fits and it does not prove* is the spine. **We never enter Cray's head to
  settle it.** The case goes into the river unopened and stays there.
- **The real victims are people, not spectacle.** Elizabeth Stride and Catherine
  Eddowes are customers, a song, an uncollected bodice — humanised, never
  murder-porn. The murders themselves stay off-page or at the edge of report, as
  in the compact edition. The book refuses what the newspapers did.
- **Fiction laid against history, not a claim to solve a real case.** The
  drowning is invented and the story knows it; no real person is accused.
- **The four rules govern the prose** as they govern Su: under-say everything;
  let objects carry weight (the fan, the white bowl, the flower, the bodice, the
  river).
- **Vol V's permitted drift** (six-volume plan): free to follow Su through
  Whitechapel, Limehouse, Shadwell and the docks; the Prospect remains her home
  world but is no longer a compulsory container. The Company is **legacy only** —
  the engine that shaped Wei and Cray, dead thirty years, its consequences
  arriving now.
- **Two languages, kept in separate rooms** — Cantonese and English never blur;
  a rule said in only one language is only half known.
- **The kung fu is authentic, never chop-socky.** Hung Ga and Wing Chun are real,
  named, and depicted with respect: correct technique (chi sao, the horse stance,
  bridges, *dit da* bonesetting), real lineage transmission, no wire-fu, no
  exoticising. The violence is brief, decisive and costly, exactly as the four
  rules require — she ends fights, she does not perform them. The one earnest use
  (the alley) reads as mastery under discipline, not spectacle.

## Principal cast

- **Su Zhang**, 19 — the watchman's daughter; POV.
- **Wei Zhang** — her father; chandler, laundryman, Hung Ga/Wing Chun master and
  *dit da* bonesetter; aging hands; the lineage-holder who passes it to Su.
- **Sau-Ling Zhang** — her mother; sent for from Hong Kong; the mission-church
  creed of the open ledger; boils the sleeve.
- **Lee Zhang** — the brother, dead at seven (the Cray entry).
- **The grandfather** — Canton kung fu master (Hung Kuen and Wing Chun), the
  fan's first owner, the white-bowl world (flashback); his school broken by the
  Opium War.
- **Dr. Reginald Cray** — retired Company surgeon; the beloved fixture; the
  ambiguity.
- **Long Liz (Elizabeth Stride)**, **Kate (Catherine Eddowes)** — customers, then
  the third and fourth names.
- **Flo** — Prospect barmaid; the flower cutting.
- **The dockworker** — Mutiny veteran; Cray's Bengal testimony.
- **Erik Johansson** — the Swede off the timber ships (the coda; the cycle tie).
