# Never First (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"A whisper against a foreigner is a torch. A whisper against a gentleman is a shrug."*
>
> — Su Zhang, on the steps of a police station she does not enter

***

## I. Nobody to Tell

Telling someone is the correct thing to do, and Su thinks it through properly, all the way, because the rules require exactly that kind of thinking — never first means a person exhausts the world's other answers before she becomes one herself.

She gets as far as the steps of the police station on a Thursday in the last week of October, a sentence prepared and rehearsed in her mouth the whole walk over, and stands across the road in the raw cold long enough to watch two constables walk a man in for questioning ahead of her — a Polish Jew, by the shouts of the crowd that has boiled up out of nowhere behind him, bleeding above one eye for the crime, as far as Su can tell from the street, of resembling somebody's idea of somebody. It has been like this since the papers printed *Leather Apron* in September: the East End hunting foreigners, any foreigners, shopkeepers who talk wrong finding their windows boarded overnight, her own father taking to his doorway at closing with his hands folded where the street can see them doing nothing at all.

She reads the whole knot before she pulls it, standing there on the far pavement.

A Chinese laundry girl, nineteen years old, walks into a police station and accuses an English gentleman — a doctor, a Company pensioner, a man with fifteen years of goodwill banked at every counter on the reach — of being the thing the whole city is currently praying is not real. In the best telling of it, she is laughed down the steps by a desk sergeant who has heard six theories that week already, each more confident than the last, and sends her home to her mother with a kind word about overwork. In the ordinary telling, the story escapes the station by the following supper, as stories do on a river this small, and reaches the Whitechapel Road by dark with her name already worn off it and only the shape left — *the Chinaman's girl said* — and by the following morning it is not Cray's windows being boarded up against a mob. It is her father's, which are boarded already, twice as thoroughly, because a mob does not trouble to distinguish between the story it heard and the story that would have been more convenient to hear, once it has decided a face will do for either.

Thirty years ago and half the world away, a clerk kept her father standing at a harbourmaster's counter while the tide went out with everything he owned still aboard it, and an official answered his one honest question in English aimed just over his head, at some more important middle distance, because there had been nothing at all wrong with his papers and it had not mattered in the slightest. Su has known that story her whole life, since before she could properly read either of her languages, and she had always understood it, until this exact moment on this exact pavement, as a story about the boy who came running afterward, the boy with the ledger who said *Run* and meant it kindly.

Standing across from the station, she hears the first half of the story properly, for what feels like the first time in nineteen years: that before anyone came running to help him, a man stood in front of an empire holding his own papers in his own two hands, and the empire looked straight through him, and there had been nothing wrong with the papers at all. The papers were never the difficulty. The looking-through was the difficulty, and no amount of correct paperwork had ever once been proof against it.

A whisper against a foreigner is a torch, she thinks, walking home with the rehearsed sentence gone cold and unused in her mouth. A whisper against a gentleman is a shrug. The law of this city, she thinks, has a current in it the same way the river does, and it does not, on any night she can presently imagine, run in her direction.

She tells no one. Not Flo. Not her mother. Not — and this decision costs her more than any of the others, and she makes it deliberately, with both eyes open, standing at the shop door with her hand already on the latch — her father.

Because her father would believe her. That is exactly the trouble with telling him. He would believe her at once and without a single qualifying question, the way he has believed every true thing she has ever brought him since she was seven years old standing in a horse stance not understanding a word of what was being asked of her — and having believed her, the rules that hold the whole of him together would demand something of him in answer, one way or another, because a man who has spent forty years teaching four rules to his daughter cannot simply set them down himself the one time they become inconvenient. And he is beloved on this street, and he is foreign on this street, and this is currently a city burning foreigners for the smallest kindling it can find. Whatever is going to be carried out of this particular autumn, Su decides, standing at her own front door with her hand not yet turning the latch, is going to be carried by her, alone, because she is the one person in the household who can set the weight down again afterward without the street noticing she ever picked it up.

## II. What Flo Almost Asked

There is one evening, in the first week of November, when Su comes closer to telling somebody than she ever comes again.

She is settling the week's chit at the Prospect, later than her usual hour, the room thinned out to the last few determined drinkers and Flo wiping down a stretch of counter that has already been wiped, the way Flo does when her hands want occupying and her mind has gone somewhere her hands are not required to follow. Cray is not on his stool. It is, Su realises only afterward, the first evening in a fortnight she has settled a chit without his eye somewhere in the room, and the absence sits oddly in her, a held breath she had not noticed she was holding until it was, briefly, allowed out.

You've gone quiet on us, duchess, Flo says — Catherine's word, landed now in a mouth that has no idea it was ever anyone else's, and Su feels the small unbearable coincidence of it and says nothing about that either. Quieter than the autumn's got any right to make anybody, and I've watched this autumn make everybody quiet.

Su says she is only tired.

Flo goes on wiping the counter a moment, not looking up, in the particular way of a woman who has spent twenty years learning that the surest way to be told something is to stop appearing to want telling.

You can say, if there's a thing, Flo says. Whatever it wants saying about. I've heard worse in this room than most rooms hear in a lifetime, and none of it's ever once left it that I sent out myself.

And for the length of exactly one breath, Su very nearly does.

She can feel the shape of it rising in her, the whole of it, the clasp and the oilcloth and the shirt with nothing in it and the dockworker's surgeon who never once let a flogging cost him anything — the whole private ledger she has been keeping since the last week of October, offered up whole to the one person in this room who has spent her whole life being the first safe place a hard thing lands. It would be such a small thing, she thinks, to simply say it. Flo would believe her, or she would not, and either way the weight would be, for one evening, somebody else's to help carry.

Then she thinks of the station steps. She thinks of a whisper against a foreigner and a whisper against a gentleman, and how little either of those whispers actually depends on who first speaks it, once it has left the room it started in — because Flo, however kindly she meant it, however carefully she kept it, is still one more mouth between Su's own and the street, and a truth this size, passed even once, is a truth that no longer entirely belongs to the person who first thought it.

Only tired, she says again.

Flo looks at her a moment longer, and Su watches her decide, the way her father decides things, to let a closed door stay closed rather than force a hinge that plainly does not want moving tonight. Flo does not ask again. She reaches instead for the small unreasonable pot on the shelf behind her — the rooted cutting of the crimson flower that has hung in the Prospect's window longer than either of them has been alive, split and re-split by a hundred years of the house's keepers — and turns it a half-inch toward the last of the evening light, a thing her hands do most nights without either of them remarking on it, the way some people cannot help tending a small live thing whenever their hands find themselves briefly empty.

You'd tell me, though, Flo says. If it got to be more than tired.

I'd tell you, Su says, and it is, she understands even as she says it, the first outright lie she has told in this room in three years of settling chits across this counter, and she carries the weight of the lie home with her that night folded in beside the other weight, because a person can only ever set down one account at a time, and this particular week she has not the room in her for two.

## III. Never First

She does not let herself decide he is the author of all five. That would be the newspapers' arithmetic, and she has no way of doing it honestly, and she knows the plain difference between what a person can count and what a person only wants very badly to count — her whole life has been kept in ledgers that had to balance to the farthing or not balance at all, and she will not now let this one close on a want dressed up as a sum.

And she does not let herself do the other thing either — the thing the banked heat under her ribs has wanted, low and steady, since the thirtieth of September: to go and find him alone in the dark, deliberately, and be sure, one way or the other, by whatever means the finding out required.

Because those are the rules, and she keeps them now the way her mother keeps a fast day — resentfully, and completely, and without once pretending the keeping is easy. Never first: he must be moving before she is; it is not her place, whatever she suspects, to make the first move herself. Never in anger: and here she does the hardest work of the whole autumn, harder than any stance her father ever held her in for the length of a lesson, because the heat under her ribs wants this. Wants it to be him. Wants it for Elisabeth and for Catherine, and, lower down and considerably older, for Lee — which is the entry she scratches out first, every single time, because Lee's debt is the one that is real, and owed, and exactly the kind the rules forbid her, above every other kind, to go collecting on her own authority. The rules say plainly that whoever goes hunting a debt in a dark lane will collect it whether or not it was ever truly owed to them, and a want this clean and this large is, her father has told her, precisely the want that needs the most watching, not the least.

So she does not go looking. She takes the heat and banks it the way the copper banks its coals overnight, and goes back to folding shirts, which is the whole of what the practice is asking of her now — to do nothing, correctly, for exactly as long as nothing is what the rules require of her. It is, she thinks some evenings, the single hardest thing she has ever not done in her life, harder by a wide margin than either of the two times she has done something.

She keeps back only one small thing for herself, and tells herself, doing it, that it means nothing at all.

Since the killings began she has taken to carrying her grandfather's fan in her sleeve — the plain folded wooden one that has corrected her own elbow every dawn of her life, its wood gone warm against the inside of her wrist within a minute of leaving the drawer, the way a thing carried close to the skin always warms faster than a person expects. Not as a plan. Not as a hunt. For the same reason, she thinks, that her father has taken to standing in his own doorway at closing with his hands folded where the whole street can see them doing nothing threatening at all: a private steadiness, the one small living piece of the dawn yard small enough to carry with her into a city that has, this particular season, gone thoroughly and collectively frightening. She tells herself it means nothing. She is nineteen years old, and it is exactly that kind of autumn, and every woman working the dock roads between here and Whitechapel is carrying something of her own by now, tucked where a stranger's eye will not find it first.

Twice in the weeks that follow the shirt she feels it, on the ordinary walk between the shop and the Prospect — not footsteps, nothing so generous to her as an actual footstep behind her in the dark. Only the particular pressure at the back of the neck that a dawn yard has taught her, over twelve years, never once to argue with, however unreasonable the hour or the empty street around her makes the feeling seem. Both times she stops at a lit window as though minding her own reflection in the glass, and reads the darker street behind her in it the way her father reads a knot he cannot see the far end of, and both times the street behind her proves, on inspection, to be only a street, empty, ordinary, entirely innocent of whatever her own neck believed it had felt.

It proves nothing either way. That is the particular cruelty of this kind of watching: a stair that is a half-inch off does not stop being a hazard merely because nobody happens to fall on it tonight. By the first week of November she has understood that this — the pressure, the checked reflection, the fan gone warm against her wrist, the empty street proving nothing — is simply the shape her whole life has taken now, and will go on being its shape until one of them, herself or the thing she cannot yet properly name, makes the single mistake that finally ends the waiting one way or the other.

## IV. The Fog Comes Down

The city thickens toward the end of November the way a copper thickens toward the boil: slowly at first, in ways a person notices only afterward, and then, past some point nobody could ever quite mark in advance, all at once and entirely.

It has been building for a fortnight by the last week of it — the true article, the London Particular, coal-smoke off ten thousand grates married to whatever the tanneries and the gasworks along the river breathe out between them, gone the colour of old brass and thick enough, on the worst evenings, to soften every gas lamp on the Highway to a bruised smear and swallow the far end of any lane before a person has properly crossed the near end of it. It gets into the mouth on the bad nights. It tastes of sulphur and wet ash and something underneath both that is faintly, unaccountably sweet, and it lays a fine grit on the lips that does not wash off with the evening's washing, and long before a person reaches the wharf on such a night she can taste the river coming up through it, mud and tar and a cold that has nothing to do with the season's proper cold at all.

It is the kind of fog, Su thinks, walking home through the thickest of it on the last Thursday of November, that unmakes a city rather than merely hiding it. It takes away the plain straight lines a person has walked her whole life without once needing to think about them, and hands her, in their place, a doubt she has to feel her way through one step at a time — a street walked a thousand times becoming, in a fog like this one, a thing felt for rather than known, and anything at all, she thinks, could be standing at the near edge of the next yard of it, and a person would not know until she was already close enough to be in its arms.

She has a delivery that particular evening at the far end of Wapping, a sailors' boarding house that settles its account on the last Monday of the month and only then, and her arms ache with the weight of it by the time the ordinary lit world of the Highway falls away behind her and the fog stands up in its place — the street stalls still flaring under their naphtha lamps somewhere behind her in the murk, the costermongers still crying the last of the evening's herring into a dusk gone the colour of old brass, the whole warm animal noise of poor people not yet finished with their day — and then, within the width of a single crossed street, all of it falls away, and there is only the fog, and the particular silence a fog this thick presses down over even the loudest of cities.

She takes the shortcut behind the sufferance wharf anyway, because it is late, and cold, and the shortcut is exactly where the shortcut has always been, every night of her life, without once giving her cause to doubt it.

The banked heat under her ribs, which has not gone out once since the thirtieth of September, sits very still and very warm as she walks, the way a coal sits at the bottom of a grate long after the flame above it has been let die down for the night — not spent, only waiting, patient in the particular way her father's own kind of patience has taught her to recognise, because she has watched him hold exactly this stillness in the cold yard a hundred mornings of her life, and knows now, walking into the fog with her grandfather's fan warm against the inside of her wrist, that a fire banked this carefully for this long does not, in the end, go out on its own.

It waits for the one night the rules will finally let it be needed.
