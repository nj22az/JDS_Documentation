# Volume Two scene design: The Pelican night raid

**Status:** Adopted architecture for Part II; prose not yet drafted.  
**Primary location:** The Pelican, Wapping, 1696.  
**Recommended chapter:** Chapter 15, *The Door Kept Shut*.  
**Inspiration rule:** Preserve the dramatic mechanism of the 1866 Teradaya escape without importing its people, weapons, imagery or cultural setting.

## Editorial decision

The complete inn-raid mechanism belongs in Volume Two rather than Su Zhang’s 1888 chapter.

Su already has a complete domestic and martial structure: the laundry and chandlery train her perception, she intervenes in an attack already under way, and her confrontation with Dr Cray resolves in the Pelican Stairs alley. Adding a second surrounded-building escape would enlarge her story sideways and weaken the severe intimacy of the alley.

Volume Two already contains the elements the mechanism needs:

- a genuinely hunted man in Adams;
- a river inn whose upper rooms can conceal him;
- Esther, whose knowledge of the house matters more than weapon skill;
- an official reward capable of turning neighbours into searchers;
- Company interests that benefit from capture without needing to command every constable;
- Anders Blom, a Swedish carpenter’s mate who understands roofs, fastenings, small craft and the tide;
- Caleb Nix, whose arrest can make the successful escape morally expensive.

One small principle may remain common to Su and Esther: domestic labour teaches each woman to notice danger before armed men do. The scenes must otherwise remain separate.

## Period translation

| Teradaya function | Wapping equivalent |
|---|---|
| A politically hunted guest hides at an inn | Adams, understood by the author to be Henry Every, rents an upstairs room at the Pelican |
| Government agents quietly surround the building | A justice’s constables and hired watermen approach from Wapping Wall and the river stairs; a retained Company information-taker identifies the house |
| A woman below notices the threat first | Esther, closing the washhouse or scullery, sees lantern light and iron moving past the low river grating |
| The warning reaches the upper room seconds before entry | Esther uses the service stair and wakes Adams without alarming the sleeping lodgers |
| Firearm and spear hold a narrow stair | Adams has period pistols; Anders uses a spar and the stair door as obstacles. The scene is an escape, not a heroic gun battle |
| The fugitive’s hand is injured | Adams cuts his palm on a broken roof light or takes a shallow hanger cut at the hatch, making every subsequent grip uncertain |
| Escape crosses roofs to a lumber store | The route runs over the Pelican’s rear roof, through the old ship-breaking loft and into a timber yard |
| Allies send a rescue boat | Anders reads the ebb and brings a skiff beneath the timber stage; the river, not superior firepower, completes the escape |
| Later assassination supplies tragic sequel | No copied assassination follows. The English consequence is Caleb’s arrest and death, the loss of the escape route, Tanner’s later search and Esther’s refusal of the reward |

## Scene sequence

### 1. The sign below the window

The Pelican is settling after late service. Esther is at the copper or counting damp linen when a moving lantern throws the shadow of a boat-hook across the river grating. A second light appears on Wapping Wall. Men are approaching both doors.

She does not know whether Adams has been identified. She knows the house is being closed.

### 2. The warning

Esther climbs the narrow service stair without calling his assumed name. Adams wakes with one pistol in hand and reaches for another. His first instinct is to make the stair cost lives.

Esther refuses. The rooms below contain sailors, widows and children. If he fires through the house, he turns every guest into his shield.

This gives her command of the scene.

### 3. Caleb at the front door

Caleb Nix pounds upon the street door. He may have seen the searchers gathering or may have unknowingly led them from the lane. Esther cannot establish which before the constables reach him.

Opening the door may save Caleb and expose Adams. Keeping it bolted gives Adams the seconds he needs.

Esther keeps it bolted.

The choice, rather than the escape, is the chapter’s emotional centre.

### 4. The stair and hatch

Anders removes the roof-hatch pin with a chisel while Adams and Esther delay the first men entering below. A table, stair door and loose spar turn the passage into a mechanical problem. One pistol discharges at most; its target and result should remain confused in smoke.

Adams is not made innocent or glamorous. His competence is frightening. Esther sees how readily he measures bodies against time.

### 5. The roofs

Rain has made the tiles dangerous. Anders tests each span as he would a deck beam. Adams cuts his hand crossing the broken roof light and can no longer trust the wet pistol in that hand.

The three reach the old ship-breaking loft. Esther does not continue to the boat. The Pelican is hers to answer for.

### 6. The ebb

Anders lowers Adams through the timber stage and takes him into the moored craft. He uses shadow, pilings and the outward current rather than speed. Searchers at the stairs look upriver while the skiff falls downriver beneath the black sides of anchored vessels.

Adams leaves without confession or farewell.

### 7. The account left ashore

Caleb is taken. The front latch is broken. A wet handprint remains upon the inner stair. One of Adams’s unfamiliar coins is found beneath a board and Tanner later searches the emptied room.

The escape therefore solves only the physical problem. It creates the personal betrayal that drives Chapters 16 and 17.

## Character ownership

- **Esther owns the warning and the moral choice.**
- **Anders owns the physical escape route and tide.**
- **Adams owns the dangerous competence.**
- **Caleb pays the immediate human cost.**
- **Tanner owns the later institutional betrayal.**

No one becomes a direct counterpart of Sakamoto Ryōma, Narasaki Ryō or Miyoshi Shinzō.

## Historical and material checks before prose

- Verify which English warrant, justice, Admiralty or local officers could lawfully search a Wapping house for a named piracy suspect in 1696.
- Use constables, deputies, hired searchers or watermen as supported; do not use the anachronistic term *police*.
- Fix the date against the English proclamation and the £500 reward; keep any later Company increase distinct.
- Establish the Pelican’s 1696 doors, service stair, roof hatch, ship-breaking loft, adjoining timber yard and river access before choreographing movement.
- Confirm the exact pistol lock and loading method. A flintlock or late snaphaunce must behave as a single-shot weapon, not as a revolver.
- Let darkness, smoke, wet timber, tide and uncertain identification constrain the scene.
