# Leather Apron (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"The dead are confirmed enough to redirect a paper's front page and not confirmed enough to be spoken of plainly in the room where their neighbours drink."*

***

The first one comes at the very end of August, and for the first week it is only a name in a stop-press column, a woman found in Bucks Row with her throat opened, and the East End reads it the way it reads any bad thing that has happened to somebody it does not personally know: with the particular blend of horror and distance that lets a person finish their tea before finishing the paragraph.

It does not stay distant.

By the second week of September there is a second woman, found off Hanbury Street this time, and this one the papers cannot manage with distance at all, because whatever was done to her was done with a thoroughness that makes the first killing read, in hindsight, almost gentle by comparison — and the papers, who have discovered by now that horror sells better dressed in restraint than horror dressed in plain description, begin printing the sort of sentence that says everything by refusing to say anything: *injuries of a character it is unnecessary to describe.* Su reads that sentence over a carman's shoulder in the Prospect and understands, the way you understand a wrongly tied knot before you can say which strand is at fault, that the restraint is not mercy. It is appetite wearing mercy's coat, because a reader's own imagination, given that much room to work in, will always supply something worse than the plain fact could have managed on its own, and the papers know it, and print the gap on purpose.

A name starts moving through the streets before the second week is out: Leather Apron. Nobody Su asks can say for certain where it began or whether there is a real man behind it — a local character, a bootmaker, somebody's half-remembered lodger — but the name has the particular stickiness of a thing that answers a fear people would otherwise have to carry shapeless, and once a fear has a name, however invented, it is very much harder to talk anyone out of hunting the name down.

The shop feels it before the Prospect does, because the shop is further from the money and closer to the fear.

A woman who has brought her washing to the Zhangs for six years stops on the step one Tuesday, parcel still under her arm, and asks Su's mother, not unkindly, whether it mightn't be safer for a body to take her custom somewhere else for a while, until things have settled. Sau-Ling takes the question the way she takes everything, without heat, and says only that the shop's water boils the same for everyone's linen regardless of who is doing the murdering three streets off, and the woman, embarrassed now that it has been said back to her so plainly, leaves her washing after all, and comes the following Tuesday too, and the one after that, because embarrassment, in the end, is a weaker tide than habit.

Su hears the second killing discussed at the Prospect the same evening it becomes known, in the particular tone a room takes on when everyone in it wants to talk about a thing and is frightened, underneath the wanting, of what talking about it might summon.

A stevedore says he heard the woman was cut nearly in two. Another corrects him — no, that's the next one, wait and see, this is only the second — with the terrible matter-of-fact certainty of a man already numbering an event that has not yet happened, as though the numbering itself were a kind of grim comfort, a way of getting ahead of a thing too large to meet unprepared. A third says his sister-in-law has stopped taking in washing after dark on principle now, whatever the water costs to heat twice. Dr. Cray, on his stool, asks no questions and offers no theories, which Su notices only because it is unlike him — he is a man who likes a good puzzle, has said as much more than once over a chess problem some regular or other brings in — and yet on this one subject, this autumn, he sits with his brandy and lets the room construct its terrors around him without once adding a brick of his own.

She thinks nothing of it. There is nothing yet to think.

By the middle of September the shape of the fear has settled into a shape the whole reach recognises without anyone needing to draw it: two women dead, both of a kind the papers call unfortunates without quite explaining to a genteel reader what the word means, both killed at night, both in streets no better lit than any other street in Whitechapel, which is to say not lit at all past the second lamp from any corner. The watch committees have begun in earnest. Men who have never in their lives carried anything heavier than a ledger are carrying cudgels now, out in twos, patrolling roads they would ordinarily have crossed the river to avoid at that hour, because fear, it turns out, makes brave men of the merely frightened just as readily as it makes cowards of them, and nobody yet knows which kind of man the autumn is going to need.

Su walks home each of those evenings with the basket on her hip and the arithmetic running underneath everything else she is doing, and twice already the watch has stopped her, kindly, and seen her the last stretch of the way, and both times she has thanked them in her careful English and said nothing about what she had already decided to do with either of them had the kindness turned out, at the last second, to be something else.

Elisabeth still comes on her Mondays. Catherine still sings her fronts of songs and promises the backs of them from Kent. Neither of them has yet been touched by any of it, not directly, not by name — they are, to Su, still only the two customers she is fondest of, moving through a city that has grown strange and watchful around them the way weather grows strange before a storm nobody has yet agreed to call a storm.

The thirtieth of September is two weeks off, and nothing in the shop, or the yard, or the long easy evenings at the Prospect, gives any sign at all of what that particular night is going to take.
