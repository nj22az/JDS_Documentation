# 1614–1617: The Same Ink

> *“I went west to be out from under it. The paper was there before me.”*
>
> — Tom Fletcher, 1617

## I. The Second Paper

After Jack walks toward Deptford, Tom goes back to the Company.

The pay office is still open. Pritchard's petition occupies one clerk, two sailors and a corner of the table where coin should be counted. The paymaster has changed his collar. A red line remains above it where Tom's fist pressed the cloth against his throat.

Nobody mentions the wall.

The Company is skilled at continuing after an interruption. Ledgers reopen. Men receive balances. Pepper leaves the warehouse under guard.

A clerk gives Tom three things.

His balance.

An offer of a boatswain's berth.

A letter seven months old.

The letter travelled from an Essex parish to Leadenhall Street, from Leadenhall Street to a ship preparing to sail, then back to the office when Tom's vessel had already cleared the Downs. It crossed London twice and no ocean. That is enough to make it arrive after the death it carries.

His mother was ill for nine days. A parish widow sat with her. The parish buried her beside Tom's father because the older grave had room along one edge and because Tom's father, dead these six years, still held the better claim on what remained of the family.

The letter contains one question from the parish clerk: whether the son wishes the rent on the rooms to continue until he can examine the effects.

There are no effects worth the rent.

Tom knows each one. A pot with one sound handle. Two stools. His father's sea chest, emptied after the burial. A wool coverlet patched in three colours. Needles. Thread. No thimble.

The thimble lies beneath Maggie's bar.

Tom sees it on the flagstones again, bright in firelight after she drops it at his feet. He did not pick it up. Maggie did. Somewhere west along the river, the last object his mother held daily is in the keeping of the woman who told him not to come back.

The pay clerk waits for a decision about the rooms.

Close them, Tom says.

Signature here.

Tom reads the line. Disposal of effects at parish discretion. Rent discharged from the date of death. No further claim against the son.

He signs.

It is the cleanest family paper he has ever seen.

The clerk slides the berth offer closer. Surat. Better wage. Departure in six weeks. The Company needs experienced men after Swally, men who know the western coast and can train crews while factors turn victory into trade.

Tom does not touch it.

He has spent the morning learning what the eastern paper is. He has held a paymaster against a wall to make eleven months of a boy's wage come back out of a book that had swallowed them, and the book gave them up in seven minutes, and nothing about the book has changed. The berth is the same instrument with a better wage written on it. He would be signing the thing he has just had his hands round the throat of.

What else, Tom says.

The clerk is surprised, which he conceals, and pleased, which he does not. Men who ask what else are rare and are remembered.

There is a westward berth, he says.

He puts it on the table. Different paper. Different seal. A different company altogether, which is the whole of what Tom takes in and the whole of what matters to him at that table: the word on it is not *east*.

Virginia. A plantation five years old and hungry for men who can keep a ship's gear alive across an ocean. The ship is the *Treasurer*, armed, victualling now. She is not a spice ship and she is not going to Bantam and she is not going to Surat and no part of her business is anything Tom has spent eleven years inside.

Wage?

Less, the clerk says.

Tom thinks of Jack's answer at the road.

No.

The word belonged to a boy with somewhere else to go.

Tom signs the berth.

He does not read the foot of the page, where the treasurer's name is entered in a clerk's fair hand. He has read eleven years of Company paper and he has learned exactly which parts of a page carry the harm, and he is entirely certain, that afternoon in that office, that the harm is in the clauses.

* * *

## II. What the Subscription Bought

The Virginia Company is not the East India Company and says so at every opportunity.

Tom learns this in the six weeks before sailing, from the men who come down to the ship and from the paper that comes down with them. There are pamphlets. The East India Company has never printed a pamphlet in its life; the East India Company has pepper, and pepper argues for itself. Virginia has printed sermons.

They are handsome. God has set aside a country and given the English the key to it. The savages are gentle and will be brought to Christ. The soil is deep. The rivers are full. There is silk to be had, and glass, and iron, and wine, and men of quality are subscribing.

There is also a lottery.

That is the part Tom understands. Not the sermons. The lottery: the plantation has run out of the sort of money that comes from merchants who expect a return, so it has been licensed to take the other sort, in small amounts, from people who expect a prize. Wheels are drawn in Paul's churchyard. A serving-man puts in his shilling. Somebody in Hertfordshire wins a suit of plate and the plantation gets his neighbours' shillings, and the plantation eats them, and next year there is another wheel.

Tom stands in the churchyard one evening in his last week and watches a wheel drawn.

There is a great deal of noise. The names come out slowly enough to be enjoyed. A boy beside him has a ticket bought jointly with three others and holds it in both hands the way a man holds a paper he cannot read and has been told is worth something.

The boy asks Tom whether he is subscribed.

I am shipped, Tom says.

The boy thinks this is the same thing and better, and says so, and Tom does not correct him.

He is thinking about a room at the Pelican eleven years ago and a Dutchman with a squint saying that a room can beat a bully and no room has ever stood up to a paper. He is thinking that this is a new sort of paper, a paper that does not even need a man's hand at the bottom, only his shilling. He does not have the words to take it further than that. What he has is the sensation of having walked out from under a machine and found, in the open air, a wheel.

The *Treasurer* drops down the river in the autumn.

She goes past Wapping in the early morning with the tide under her, close enough to the north bank that a man at the rail can count windows.

Tom is at the rail. The house is there. The sign is there and wants painting. There is a light on in the low room because Maggie has been up since four for the bread, as she has been up since four for the bread every day of his life and every day of it before that.

He is a hundred and forty yards from her door and travelling at four knots and he does not lift his hand, because there is nobody at the window to see it lifted, and because lifting it would be for himself.

The river takes him east to go west. That is the shape of it. Everything leaving London goes the wrong way first.

* * *

## III. Southing

The passage is the longest thing Tom has ever done in a straight line without a factory at the end of it.

They do not run down the Channel and stand west across the open Atlantic, which is a thing being tried and will one day be ordinary. They go the old road, southing until the weather changes, then west with the trade behind them. Two months, near enough, with an island in the middle of it.

He finds out what he is aboard within a fortnight.

The *Treasurer* carries more guns than a plantation needs. Her crew is half seamen and half something else, men who have signed for a colony and cannot splice, and men who can splice and did not sign for a colony. The master is Argall, who is thirty-odd and has already been across four times, and who is the most competent man Tom has served under since his father, and the coldest.

Tom watches him for six weeks and cannot get a purchase on him.

Argall is not cruel. He does not flog for pleasure or short the water or take the first pick of anything. He runs a clean ship and an exact one and he answers a question with the whole answer, which in Tom's experience is worth more than kindness at sea. And he has the same face going into a squall as coming out of one, and the same face reading a letter as reading a chart, and Tom, who has spent eleven years learning to read a man's shoulders across a deck, gets nothing off him at all.

Somewhere south of the Canaries Tom works out what it reminds him of.

It reminds him of a clerk.

Not the man; the surface. The unvarying attention. Argall handles a ship the way Arthur Higgins handles a column — completely, correctly, and without any part of himself in it that the work does not require. Tom has met one and served under the other and he did not expect to find the two of them the same animal.

He does not decide whether this is admirable. He files it, which is what he does now instead of deciding, and the filing is itself a thing Maggie would have something to say about, and he knows exactly what she would say, and he goes forward to see about the fore topmast stay.

* * *

## IV. The Islands

They water in the West Indies, because the *Treasurer* is armed and the islands are where her other business is.

Nobody explains this to Tom and nobody needs to. A ship that carries a colony's stores does not carry that weight of ordnance, and a colony's stores do not require the master to be ashore three days at a Spanish anchorage that is not on any list Tom has been shown. Prizes are taken on that coast. Everyone forward knows it. The Company that prints sermons about bringing the savages to Christ has a ship in the Indies looking for Spaniards to rob, and both of those things are true at once, and Tom has been holding two true things at once since he was twenty-two and finds it no harder here than anywhere.

What he does not expect is the cane.

They fill water at a place with a low white town and a church and, behind the town, running back from the shore further than a man can see from a masthead, fields.

The fields are worked. It is the middle of the day and it is a heat Tom has no measure for, worse than Bantam because it does not move, and the fields are worked. He counts, because counting is what he does with a thing he cannot otherwise hold: perhaps ninety in the near ground. Africans, most of them. Some not. A man on a horse at the headland with nothing in his hands, which tells Tom the whole arrangement inside four seconds, because a man who does not need to hold anything is a man whose holding is done for him somewhere else, on paper, in a town, by somebody who has never been in that field.

The boatswain's mate beside him says it is a shame the English have not got the trick of it yet.

Tom says nothing.

He stands at the rail for a while and puts it where he puts things. This is not his ocean and these are not his Company's fields and there is nothing here he has signed. He tells himself that, exactly, in those words, and hears underneath it — quite clearly, in a voice he has been carrying for eleven years — the beginning of the sentence Maggie would use to take it apart.

He does not finish the sentence for her. He goes below.

Sixty-odd years later, in a different century, a different book will be kept about that coast and those fields and what the English learn to do there, and none of it will be Tom Fletcher's business and all of it will be the same instrument. He is not given to see that. What he is given is one afternoon, one anchorage, ninety people in a field and a man on a horse with nothing in his hands, and the recognition — flat, unwelcome, filed — that he has seen this before in a warehouse on the Thames with the light coming in differently.

* * *

## V. The Fort

Virginia in the spring is the most beautiful country Tom has ever seen and the plantation sitting in it is a debtor's shed.

Both of those stand. He never afterwards manages to say the one without the other and he stops trying.

The river is a mile wide and full. The trees go up like a cathedral and go back, he is told, for four hundred miles. There is game in it a man could not eat in a lifetime and fish that come to the net in numbers that would empty an English pond, and Tom stands in it in his first week and thinks that a person could live here.

Then he goes up to the fort.

The arithmetic is the first thing that reaches him, because arithmetic always is. He asks, in the ordinary way of a boatswain asking after a place, how many are here. He is given a number. He asks how many have been sent since the beginning. He is given a much larger number, and it is given easily, and that is what stops him: the man reciting it is not ashamed of it and does not appear to have set the two figures beside one another before.

Tom sets them beside one another.

Eight years. Something upward of a thousand sent. What is standing in front of him would not fill a Wapping parish on a wet Sunday.

He does not say so. He has learned what happens when a boatswain says so. He goes back down to the ship and sits with a tally board and does it properly, because doing it properly is the only religion he has left, and the answer he gets is a number he has met before in a different form. The Company enters dead boys as cargo. The plantation enters them as attrition, or as unseasoned, which is the word they use here and which Tom hears eleven times in his first month. A man is unseasoned. Then he is dead. The word does the work of the entry.

The colony is under martial law and has been for years, which nobody calls martial law; they call it the laws, and they are read out, and they include hanging for stealing an ear of corn from a store that does not have enough corn in it. Tom watches a man put in irons for going to live among the Powhatan and hears the offence described as running to the savages, and hears, the same week, from a cooper who has been in the country five years, that men run because over there they are fed.

Both of those are true. He holds them.

And in the middle of all this — the shed, the arithmetic, the corn, the irons — one thing has started to work.

Tobacco.

They have found, in the last year or two, that the leaf will grow, and that London will pay, and that it will pay so extravagantly that nothing else the plantation was chartered for signifies any longer. Not the silk. Not the glass. Not the iron or the wine or the passage to the South Sea or any of the sermons in the churchyard. Tom sees the streets of the fort planted with it. He sees the market square planted with it. He sees men who were sent to build a country growing a weed in the space where the country was supposed to go, because the weed is the only entry on the whole page that has ever come out black.

He thinks: it will be this, now. Whatever else they say. It will be this, and it will want ground, and it will want hands, and I have seen the ground and I have seen where they get hands.

He thinks it in Virginia in 1615 and he is entirely right and there is nobody in the world he can say it to.

He does one useful thing in Virginia and it is refused.

The stores are short and everyone knows the stores are short, and the argument about why has been running for years and has two sides, and both sides are men who are certain. Tom listens to it twice and then does what he does, which is ask to see how the counting is done.

It is done once. That is the whole of it. A thing comes off a ship and a man writes it down, and later a thing goes out of the store and a man writes that down, and the two men are not the same man and neither of their books is ever laid against the other because there is nobody whose office it is to lay them.

Tom proposes a second tally.

He puts it plainly, at the right time, to the right man, without any suggestion that anybody has been dishonest, because his whole experience is that the harm in a store is almost never dishonesty; it is that a number nobody checks becomes, within about four months, a number nobody can check. Two boards. One at the ship, one at the store. Read together on the same day, by two men who each keep their own, and neither of whom is required to trust the other.

He is heard out courteously.

He is told that it is a good notion for a Company factory and that this is a plantation under the laws, and that the difficulty in Virginia is not the counting.

Which is true. The difficulty in Virginia is that men are dying and the ground is not planted and the country is enormous and nobody in London believes any account that comes out of it. The counting is the eleventh difficulty on a list of ten.

Tom does not press it. He goes back to his own gear.

He carries the idea in his head for three more years and puts it into a warehouse at Surat, where the difficulty *is* the counting, and where it works so well that within a decade it is simply how the thing is done — and where, in the way of every good thing he ever builds, it makes the machine that required it a little harder to catch out.

He writes Maggie about the trees.

* * *

## VI. The Passenger List

In the spring of 1616 the *Treasurer* is to go home, and she is to carry the plantation's argument with her.

Tom gets it as a working problem before he gets it as anything else. Passengers. A dozen and more, above the usual, and one of them is a woman the Company means London to see, and the arrangements come down the hatch to the boatswain as arrangements always do: where they are to be berthed, what is to be built for them, what is to come out of the hold to make room for what is going into it.

He builds partitions in the great cabin. He shifts water casks. He rigs a canvas screen and takes it down again because it is wanted six feet further aft, and rigs it there, and this is the whole of his part in it and he never afterwards claims otherwise.

The list is made out on deck at a table because there is better light there.

Tom is at the table because the master's clerk cannot count barrels and Tom can. So he is standing four feet away, waiting to be asked about the water, when the woman's entry is made, and he sees it made, and he reads it upside down, which he has been able to do since he was twenty-three and which has never once done him any good.

She is entered under the name the plantation gave her at her christening.

Beside it, in the same hand, smaller, in the way a clerk notes a thing he has been told he must not leave out, is the name she had before.

Two names on one line. One of them is the entry. The other is a note.

Tom stands there with a figure for the water in his mouth and does not give it.

Because he has seen this page before. He has seen it in a coroner's finding read out in a room he was not in, eleven years and four thousand miles ago, where a woman who had been dragged into a tavern with another man's blood on her was written down as a foreign female, name uncertain — and Tom has carried what that sentence did, and how little effort it took, and how correct every word of it was.

This is not that. Nobody here is uncertain. Both names are on the paper and both are spelled and the clerk takes trouble over both.

That is somehow worse, and Tom cannot get to the bottom of why, standing at a table on a spring morning with a figure for the water going cold in his mouth. It takes him the whole crossing and part of the year after to arrive at it, and when he does it is not a thought so much as a shape:

They have written her true name down in order to have written it down.

The clerk asks him for the water.

Tom gives him the water.

## VII. The Crossing

Nine weeks, near enough, and Tom is crew.

That is the honest account of what he is and it does not improve on inspection. He is the boatswain of a ship carrying thirteen or so people east across an ocean, and his business with them is rope, canvas, headroom and the smell of the bilge, and his business is not their business. He is up before them and turned in after them. He speaks to their party perhaps nine times in nine weeks, all of it about buckets, ladders, the danger of the after hatch in a seaway, and once about a child.

He sees her the way a boatswain sees a passenger.

On deck in the mornings when it is fine, which is not often, because it is the North Atlantic in the spring and it is fine perhaps one day in five. Standing at the lee rail with her son when the boy is well enough. Going below, quickly, when it comes on to blow, with her husband's hand under her arm and her own hand on the coaming in the manner of a person who has learned in eight weeks the one thing everybody learns in eight weeks, which is where to put a hand.

She is small. She is much younger than he had assumed from the way the arrangements had been described to him. She is often ill and hides it, and hides it in a way Tom recognises, because it is the way his own men hide it — by continuing to do the next ordinary thing at the ordinary speed, which is the only concealment available at sea and does not work on anybody who has ever done it themselves.

That is the whole of what Tom Fletcher knows about her, and he never claims one word more, for the rest of his life, to anybody.

He carries a chest.

That is his one contact. Somewhere south of the Grand Banks, in a lull, the party's gear is shifted forward and there is a small chest that is to go up and he takes it up, and at the head of the ladder she is standing there, and she says something to him.

He does not have the language. He has forty words of Portuguese, some Gujarati coming, a boatswain's Malay and enough Dutch to be rude in, and he has not one word of hers.

She sees that he does not have it. And she does not do what an Englishman does in that situation, which is repeat himself louder; she takes it back to English, which is not hers either, and says thank you.

Thank you, Tom says.

Then he goes back down the ladder, because the fore topsail wants attention and because there is nothing else in the encounter, and there never is, and the whole of it takes four seconds.

He thinks about it for thirty years.

Not because anything happened. Because nothing did — because for four seconds at the head of a ladder in mid-ocean two people with no language between them managed the exact transaction the entire enterprise on both sides of that water is failing at, which is to hand a thing over and say thank you.

* * *

## VIII. The Counting Stick

They make Plymouth in the first week of June and the whole ship is on deck for it.

Land after nine weeks does that. Even the men who have crossed a dozen times come up for the first sight of the Sound, and the passengers come up, and for an hour the *Treasurer* is not a ship with a working part and a carried part; she is fifty-odd people at a rail looking at the same green thing.

Tom is at the fore rigging with a coil in his hand when he notices the man with the stick.

He is one of the party. Tom has had him pointed out — a grave man, older than most of them, sent by the woman's father, and the men aft say he is a priest of theirs, and the men forward say other things. He has been on deck more than any of the rest across the crossing and has watched the working of the ship with an attention Tom has been quietly flattered by.

Now he is standing at the rail with a stick about the length of his forearm and a knife, and he is cutting notches in it, and he is looking at Plymouth.

Tom watches him do it for some minutes before he understands.

He is counting the English.

Tom knows, immediately and without being told, exactly what he is looking at, because Tom has spent his whole life in a house where a woman cuts a notch in the underside of a bar for every boy the sea does not give back. He knows what a notch costs to make and what it is for. It is for the ones the number would otherwise take. It is the last thing a person does when they have understood that the sum is going to be too large to hold and they have decided to hold it anyway.

The man is trying to bring a number home.

Plymouth is a middling English port on a Tuesday in June. There are perhaps four thousand people in it. There are boats on the water and carts on the Barbican and a crowd on the quay that has heard something is coming in, and the knife goes and goes and the notches crowd each other and get shallower and start running into one another along the stick.

At some point he stops.

He does not throw the stick over the side. Tom notes that, and has never stopped noting it. He looks at what he has cut, and looks at Plymouth, and stands there with the stick in one hand and the knife in the other, and then he puts the knife away and holds on to the stick and goes on looking.

He is going to have to go home and say the number cannot be got.

And Tom — thirty-five years old, eleven weeks unwashed, with a coil of new-tarred line over one shoulder and no business in the matter whatsoever — has to turn round and face the rigging for a moment, because it has gone through him without any warning at all.

He is not moved by the man's grief. He would not presume. He does not know the man and will never speak to him and cannot say one true sentence about what is in him.

What has gone through him is the stick.

Because Maggie's notches are on the underside of the bar where a hand finds them and an eye does not, and there are not very many of them, because Wapping is one street and a boy is one boy and a keeper can hold that. And what Tom has just watched is what happens when somebody tries the same instrument against a number that is not one street. The stick is the right idea. The stick is the only idea. The stick is what any decent person would do.

And it does not scale, and a man has just found that out in public, at a rail, at the mouth of the wrong country.

Tom goes and sees about the boats.

That night, in Plymouth Sound, with the ship quiet and the land smell coming off the water like something being cooked, he sits on the fore hatch for a long time and does not write anything.

* * *

## IX. The Road Up

The party goes up to London by road and Tom stays with the ship.

He is not asked and would not be. A boatswain does not accompany passengers into the country; a boatswain gets a ship's gear ashore, surveys what nine weeks of the Atlantic has done to it, and argues with a chandler about the price of what has to be replaced. He is in Plymouth eleven days and he works them.

What comes down to the ship from the road is talk, and the talk is very good, and it improves.

They have gone up in state. They are received. There is a house taken for them; there is a bishop; there are coaches. The Company has printed her likeness. She is to be shown at court at the turn of the year and the King's own masque, and every man on the quay at Plymouth has an opinion about what it means and every one of the opinions is about money.

Tom listens to it the way he listens to any dock rumour, which is with about a third of himself, and hears in all of it one thing that nobody else on the quay appears to hear at all.

They are showing her to the subscribers.

That is what it is. He has watched a wheel drawn in Paul's churchyard for a plantation that could not pay its way, and now the plantation has something to put in front of the shillings that a printed sermon cannot do: a living argument, brought four thousand miles, that the country is real and the people in it can be brought over and the whole enterprise is what the pamphlets said. The bishop is not an accident. The coaches are not an accident. The Company is not being kind and it is not being cruel; it is doing what a company with an empty book does with an asset that has arrived, which is show it to the room.

Tom knows this because he has been an asset shown to a room. He was twenty-two, and the room was the hiring hall, and the argument he was brought in to make was that a boy of good family with a boatswain father would sign.

The scale is not the same. The instrument is.

He does not go up to London on the road. He has no cause to and would not be given leave, and he understands perfectly well that the second half of that sentence is doing all the work for him, and he is grateful for it, and he despises the gratitude, and he goes and argues with the chandler about the price of six fathom of three-inch.

* * *

## X. What He Wrote from Plymouth

He writes to Maggie from Plymouth and sends it, as he has written and sent from every port he has fetched in three years and will go on writing and sending for eleven more.

It is a good letter. It is one of the best he ever writes, and every word in it is true, and it is a lie the size of an ocean.

He gives her the crossing. He gives her the fog on the Banks so thick a man at the wheel could not see the mainmast and they went four days by the lead and the smell of the water. He gives her a shark that followed them for two days and looked, he says, like the elder Grimes, and had the same opinions. He gives her the first sight of the Sound and the whole ship's company at the rail like boys, and the fact that a country you have been away from smells of cooking before you can see it.

He tells her the trees. He has been saving the trees for two years and he spends them here — that there are trees over there that four men cannot get their arms round, and that they go back further than anyone has walked, and that a person could stand under them and forget the price of anything.

He asks whether the roof still takes water at the north corner in a south-westerly. He asks whether she had the sweep to the flue before the cold came in. He asks whether Hendricks is still holding down the short end of the bar. He asks whether the sign has been repainted yet and whether she kept the old one when it came down. He asks whether her chest is easier this winter than last.

He does not ask her to write back.

And he does not write one word about the fields at the anchorage, or the arithmetic at the fort, or the tobacco in the market square, or a name written twice on one line, or a man at a rail with a stick and a knife who ran out of stick.

He can hear exactly what any of it would sound like in her hands. A man reporting. A man laying out what he has seen, correctly, in order, with the moral placed at the end where she cannot miss it — which is a boy holding up a copybook and wanting the mark, and she told him at the door what she thinks of a man who uses paper to plead his own case.

So he sends her a fog, a shark with opinions, and some very large trees.

He is a hundred and ninety miles from Wapping when he seals it, which is the nearest he has been to her in two years, and he puts it on the London road in the hands of a carrier who will have it at her bar inside the week, and he goes back aboard, and he does not follow it.

* * *

## XI. Gravesend

In March of 1617 the *George* is to go out to Virginia, and the party is to go with her, and Tom is shipped for the passage under the same master.

He has not been paid off. That is the plain reason and he gives it to himself several times: a man does not walk away from a berth in March with a summer's wage in front of him and nothing else arranged. It is a good reason. It is even true. Underneath it is the other thing, which is that a berth is a set of orders and orders are the one form of paper that will not let a man arrive at a door.

They drop down the river from Blackwall.

Tom is on the forecastle head as they go, and this time he does not count windows, because they pass Wapping in the dark on the ebb and there is nothing to count. There is the shape of the north bank he could draw with his eyes shut. There is one light where the low room is. That is all, and it goes astern in about ninety seconds.

She is ill before they reach Gravesend.

Tom does not know how ill and does not learn it in any way that would let him claim to have known. What reaches him is what reaches the fore part of a ship, which is movement: the master sending for the surgeon, and then the master going below himself, which Argall does not do; and then, at Gravesend, the order to bring the ship up and get a boat away.

He is in the boat's crew.

It is a raw grey afternoon and the river at Gravesend is the colour of a knife. They put her ashore at the stairs, and she is carried, and there is a small confusion at the top of the steps about which house, which is settled by somebody who knows the town, and the whole business takes perhaps a quarter of an hour and is done quietly and well by people who are doing their best.

Tom is at the oar. He holds the boat off the stairs with a boathook while it is done, which is his entire part in it, and it is the same part he had at the head of a ladder in mid-ocean, and it is the same part he had in a tavern in Wapping in 1603 when he stood in a doorway and took a backhand and a man died anyway on the centre table nine feet behind him.

He is very good at holding the boat off the stairs.

She dies in the town within a few days. Tom is not there; the ship lies at Gravesend and he is aboard her. The word comes down with the boat in the morning along with the bread.

The ship's business does not stop. That is the fact he takes away and keeps. There is a burial in the parish church and there are people at it who cared for her, and there is also a master who must get a ship to Virginia, and stores that will spoil, and a spring tide, and a Company in London that will require an account of the delay and will receive one. The passage is put back and then made. Everything continues. Everybody behaves correctly.

Paper does not observe a death unless somebody pays to have the entry made. He has known that sentence for fourteen years. Here the entry is made — parish register, a name, a date — and the entry is correct, and it costs the Company nothing at all, and none of that is the same as being observed.

And Tom Fletcher stands at the rail of a ship at Gravesend on the twenty-second of March, 1617, and he is twenty-six miles from the Pelican's door.

Twenty-six miles. One tide and a bit of a walk. He has been four thousand miles from it for three years and it did not feel like this, because four thousand miles is a condition and twenty-six is a decision.

He has been closer than this. That is the thing that will not leave him alone at the rail. In the spring of 1614 he stood at a road fork at Blackwall with the Pelican lying west and Deptford east and looked toward neither, and Jack asked him straight out whether he would come to Wapping, and he said no, and gave a reason that was true — that he would only be going to show her the collar he had pulled straight.

That was a good reason and he has lived on it for three years.

It does not reach as far as this afternoon. He can feel that it does not, standing on a wet deck at Gravesend with his hands cold on the rail, and he cannot yet say why, and it will take him until Surat to find the words: that in 1614 he withheld himself from her because he had nothing to bring but himself, and that is one kind of refusal; and that today he has something to bring, and that is precisely why he must not.

He works it out properly, because he is that sort of man and because it is the only way he can stand there at all. Leave in the morning boat. Up the river with the flood. He could be on the Wall by the afternoon and through the door before the fire is banked, and she would look up, and he would be a man who had come to her with this in his hands.

And that is the end of the arithmetic.

Because he can see it. He can see himself in the low room with the smell of the pie and the bar between them, telling her — quietly, well, in order, the way he tells things now — about a girl carried up some stairs at Gravesend and a company that had shown her to its subscribers and a man on a quay who ran out of stick. He can see himself watching her face while he does it. He can see, with total clarity, the thing he would be doing, which is bringing a dead woman up the river as evidence of his own improvement.

She would know inside a sentence. She has known him since he was twenty-two.

Tom does not take the morning boat.

He goes below and turns in and the ship sails for Virginia within the month, and he does not sail in her. He takes his discharge at Gravesend the week after the burial, walks the twenty-six miles to London in a day and a half along the Kent road, arrives filthy at Leadenhall, and never once, in all of it, goes east along the Wall as far as the sign.

## XII. The Seal

He goes to be paid off, and to sign whatever is next, and both of those happen in one room in an hour.

The clerk is not the clerk of three years ago. The office is the same office. There is a rail, and men behind it, and the particular smell of a counting house, which is ink and sealing wax and wet wool and no air.

Tom's westward balance is paid. He signs the discharge and reads it first, all of it, which the clerk does not expect and does not comment on.

Then he asks about the East.

Surat, the clerk says, without needing to look. There is always Surat. The berth is better than it was in fourteen and the Company is short of men who have kept gear alive in a hot country, and there is a ship in six weeks.

He gets the paper out.

And Tom, who has been three years at sea and has learned in that time to read a page from the bottom because the bottom is where the harm is kept, reads it from the bottom.

The treasurer's name is at the foot of the sheet.

He knows it. He has been looking at it for three years. It is at the foot of the Virginia articles in his chest at that moment, in a clerk's fair hand, exactly as it is at the foot of this one — the same man, governing the one company and holding the purse of the other, in the same square mile, out of what is very probably the same house.

Tom stands there at the rail with the sheet in his hand.

The clerk asks whether there is a difficulty.

No, Tom says.

There is no difficulty. That is precisely the trouble with it. Nothing here has been concealed from him. The name was on the paper he signed in the spring of 1614 in this room, six inches under his own hand, and he did not read it, because he was busy being certain the harm was in the clauses.

He went west. He crossed an ocean the wrong way to get out from under one company and he arrived, three years and four thousand miles later, at the same desk, and the desk had not moved and had not needed to.

And a woman in a tavern told him this in 1603, standing on the other side of her own bar with his contract in her hand, and he was twenty-two and hearing something else. The companies do not even have to be the same. The man is the same. The ink is the same.

He had thought, at the time, that she was talking about Harcourt.

Tom folds the sheet and reads the top of it, and the middle, and the clauses, out of habit and because he does not know what else to do with his hands for a moment.

Then he signs it.

Not because the machine has beaten him. He would not put it that way and neither will anybody else in this account. He signs it because he is thirty-six years old and a boatswain and there is nothing else on the table; because a man who has learned exactly how a thing is made is more use inside it than shouting at it from the Kent road; because he is good at this and being good at something is the last unmixed thing he has; and because — this is the one he does not look at directly, then or for years — the East is a very long way away, and he has just proved to himself that being twenty-six miles from her door is worse.

Departure in six weeks. He is aboard in five.

On the passage out, somewhere south of the Cape Verdes with the trade steady and nothing to do in the last hour of the watch, he writes Maggie a foolish page about a fish that came aboard in the night with a face like a magistrate, and asks, at the foot of it, whether the roof has been seen to.

He does not tell her he was at Gravesend.

He never tells her he was at Gravesend. Not in eleven more years of letters, not on the night in 1626 when she finally slides the pint across the bar, not in the nine years after that. It is the first thing he keeps back from her and it will not be the last; and unlike the one that comes five years later — three words, folded into his chest under his mother's parish notice — this one was never written down at all, and so there is nothing anywhere in the world to find.

Both accounts remain open. He would have said so himself, if anybody had asked him, in exactly those words, because by 1617 that is how Tom Fletcher has learned to hold what he cannot put down.

Nobody asks him.

The ship runs south. The trade holds. Ahead of him is Surat and eleven years and a machine that will fit him exactly.
