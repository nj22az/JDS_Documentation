# 1614–1617: The Same Ink

> *I thought west was another direction.*
>
> — Tom, 1617

## I. The Second Paper

After Jack walks toward Deptford, Tom goes back to the pay office.

The room is still open.

A clerk is arguing over Pritchard's petition. Two sailors wait for balances. The paymaster has changed his collar, though a red line still shows above it where Tom's fist held him against the wall that morning.

Nobody mentions the wall.

Coin is counted. Names are called. A warehouse boy comes in for a signature and leaves with one. The day has continued without asking whether Tom is ready for it.

At the rail a clerk gives him his balance, an offer of a boatswain's berth to Surat and a letter seven months old.

The letter is from Essex.

His mother is dead.

Nine days ill. A parish widow with her at the end. Buried beside his father because the older grave had room along one edge.

The clerk waits while Tom reads it twice.

There is a question at the bottom. Does Thomas Fletcher wish the rent on his mother's rooms continued until he can examine the effects?

Tom can examine them from memory.

One pot with a sound handle. Two stools. A patched coverlet. Needles. Thread. His father's sea chest, empty except for things nobody else wanted after the burial.

No thimble.

The thimble is beneath Maggie's bar.

He sees it on the Pelican floor again, bright in the firelight after she dropped it at his feet and told him what she thought of the man he had become. He left it there. Maggie picked it up.

Close the rooms, Tom says.

The clerk turns another sheet.

Sign here.

Tom reads it.

Disposal of effects at parish discretion. Rent discharged from the date of death. No further claim against the son.

He signs.

The clerk pushes the Surat berth forward.

Better wage than the last voyage. Six weeks to sailing. The Company wants men who have seen the western coast of India and can keep a deck working while the factors make trade out of Swally.

Tom does not touch it.

What else?

The clerk looks up.

Else?

West.

The clerk searches another pile.

Virginia wants seamen. Stores, settlers, gear. Less money.

How much less?

Enough that I would take Surat.

Tom takes the Virginia paper.

Different seal. Different heading. No East Indies anywhere on the page.

That is enough for him that afternoon.

He reads the wage, the term, the food allowance, the penalties for desertion and the charge for kit. He reads the parts he has learned to fear.

He does not read the names printed above them.

He signs.

The clerk sands the page.

When?

Spring sailing, if the ship finishes victualling. You will be told.

Tom folds his copy into his coat beside the notice of his mother's death.

One paper closes a room.

The other opens an ocean.

He leaves by the door nearest the river and does not go to Wapping.

* * *

## II. A Shilling in Paul's Churchyard

Virginia is sold differently.

Tom learns that before he sees a ship.

There are sermons. Broadsheets. Men who can make a swamp sound like Eden without once appearing to lie. Silk, iron, vines, timber, a passage through the continent if God has been sensible in laying it out. A country waiting for English hands.

And there is the lottery.

That interests him because it is honest in a way the sermons are not.

A man gives a shilling because he wants more than a shilling back.

Tom watches a drawing in Paul's churchyard on an afternoon when the rain has kept the riggers off the yards. A wheel turns. Names come out. A serving-man beside him shares one ticket with three friends and holds his quarter of it as carefully as Tom once held his first articles.

You in? the man asks.

Shipped.

Virginia?

Tom nods.

Better than a ticket, the man says.

Tom looks at the wheel.

Perhaps.

The man laughs as if Tom has made a joke.

A prize is announced. The crowd surges. Somewhere behind the noise a clerk writes down what the wheel has cost and what it has brought in.

Tom leaves before the next name.

He has no quarrel with the lottery.

That is what bothers him.

* * *

## III. West

He sails the following year on a ship whose master has crossed twice and intends to survive a third.

Tom likes him immediately for not calling the voyage an adventure.

It is work.

Water to be kept sweet as long as water can be kept sweet. Biscuit to be guarded from damp. New men taught which rope may be stepped across and which may take a leg when it runs. A mast that complains for three days before the carpenter finds the split beneath an iron band.

The ocean is new. The work is not.

That steadies him.

There are settlers aboard who know farms and settlers who know nothing useful on a moving deck. One gentleman carries three books and no spare stockings. A carpenter's apprentice is sick for twelve days and then becomes so hungry he steals the boatswain's onion.

Tom makes him eat it in front of him.

The boy waits for the punishment.

That was the punishment, Tom says. It was my onion.

By the time land is sighted, Tom has stopped thinking of west as escape.

It is simply west.

He prefers that.

* * *

## IV. Virginia

The river is wider than he expected.

That is his first thought.

His second is that England has sent too many dead men here.

He does not see the dead. He sees what should be standing if the numbers he has heard in London are true.

The fort, the houses, the sheds, the patches of cleared ground: all of it together is too small.

Tom asks a cooper how many men are in the settlement.

The cooper gives him a number.

How many have come out since the first landing?

The cooper gives him another.

Tom looks at him.

The cooper shrugs.

First summer takes them. Fever takes them. Bad water. Bad bellies. Bad luck. Some never learn the country.

He says it the way a sailor lists weather that cannot be helped.

Tom walks the store that afternoon.

Barrels against one wall. Meal. Tools. Powder. Cloth. A clerk's board with issues marked in chalk and another book somewhere else that records what came off the ships.

How do you lay the two together? Tom asks.

The storekeeper does not understand the question.

The landing count and the issue count.

Why?

To see where they disagree.

The man gives him the look given to people who have invented work.

If something is missing, we look.

How do you know it is missing?

The storekeeper taps the issue board.

Tom looks at the board.

Then at the door.

He lets it go.

There are larger problems here.

The rules are read aloud often enough that even men who cannot read can fear them correctly. Orders about work, worship, food, desertion, obedience. Punishments arrive quickly. A man who leaves his assigned labour can discover that the colony owns more of his day than he understood when he signed in London.

Tom watches one settler brought back after trying to live beyond the English settlement.

The man's wrists are tied.

He is thin.

So is the man tying him.

Neither seems victorious.

The ground itself is astonishing. Dark soil. Rivers. Forest enough to repair a fleet if a fleet could be persuaded to grow out of it.

And tobacco.

At first Tom sees only small patches.

Then more.

Men talk about Rolfe's leaf because samples have gone home and London has paid attention. A crop that brings money alters a conversation faster than any sermon. Ground intended for something else acquires tobacco because tobacco has a buyer.

Tom knows that logic.

He does not need to name it.

At the store he tries once more.

Two tallies, he says. One at landing. One at issue. Different hands.

The storekeeper hears him out this time.

Good idea on a ship.

On land as well.

We need corn, not another board.

Tom cannot argue with that.

He leaves the idea there and goes back to rigging.

Years later he will use two tallies somewhere they matter more.

For now Virginia does not want the solution he knows how to give.

That is new to him.

* * *

## V. The Woman on the List

In 1616 Tom takes a homeward berth.

The ship is crowded before the cargo is aboard.

Sir Thomas Dale is going home. John Rolfe. Rolfe's wife. Their child. Several people from her country. A priest or counsellor whose name the English sailors pronounce three different ways before breakfast.

Samuel Argall commands.

Tom knows Argall by reputation before he works under him. Fast passages. Hard decisions. A man who has spent enough time in Virginia to stop explaining it to newcomers.

The extra passengers mean extra partitions, extra water calculations and arguments over where baggage may be put without turning the after spaces into a trap in bad weather.

Tom is waiting beside the clerk's table for the water figures when the passenger names are copied.

One line catches his eye.

*Rebecca Rolfe.*

Then, smaller:

*called Pocahontas.*

The clerk asks Tom for the water.

Tom gives him the figure.

That should be the end of it.

It is nearly the end of it.

But he looks once more at the two names sharing one line.

Eleven years earlier a London paper could not be troubled to keep one woman's name at all.

Here the Company keeps two.

Tom does not decide what that means.

He has barrels to move.

* * *

## VI. Nine Weeks

The passengers belong aft.

Tom belongs everywhere the ship is failing.

That keeps the distance between them honest.

He sees Rolfe's wife on deck when weather allows. Small, younger than the stories made her sound, often with the child. She is ill some days and appears anyway. The people travelling with her watch the English sailors with the steady attention of people who have crossed into a country before the ship has reached it.

Tom speaks to none of them beyond work.

Bucket there.

Not that ladder in a sea.

Wait until the hatch is secured.

The ordinary language of a ship.

It is enough.

Fog finds them on the Banks and stays.

For days the world is lead, bell, wet canvas and the sounding line. Men who have crossed oceans all their lives move more quietly when they cannot see the length of their own ship.

Tom likes the fog better than he should.

Nothing in it asks where he belongs.

* * *

## VII. Plymouth

Land smell reaches them before land.

Smoke. Green things. Cooking.

Every man who can find an excuse comes on deck.

At Plymouth the quay thickens with people before the passengers are properly ashore. News has gone ahead somehow. It always does.

Tom is overseeing a sling when he notices the older Powhatan man from the passenger party standing apart with a stick and a knife.

A notch.

Then another.

He looks at the quay and cuts again.

Tom stops giving an order halfway through.

Maggie's notches are hidden beneath a bar in Wapping.

One for each boy the sea did not return.

This man's notches multiply in daylight.

People pass. Sailors. Porters. Women carrying baskets. Boys who have come to stare. The stick acquires marks faster than a fair tally should.

At some point the man stops.

He looks at the stick.

Then at Plymouth.

He puts the knife away.

He keeps the stick.

Tom finishes the order he had interrupted.

That night he thinks of the underside of Maggie's bar for the first time in months.

He does not enjoy it.

* * *

## VIII. The Road to London

The party goes east by road.

Tom remains with the ship.

A boatswain does not escort important passengers to London. He inventories nine weeks of damage, argues about rope and discovers that a chandler's definition of sound canvas expands when the Company is paying.

Talk comes back down the road.

The woman is received. There are dinners. Clergymen. Noble houses. London wants to see her. The Company very much wants London to see her.

A dock clerk says it will do Virginia good.

Tom is bent over a worn block when he says, How?

Subscriptions.

The clerk says it as if answering a child.

Tom looks at the block in his hand.

Then he understands why the question irritated him.

Not because the clerk is wrong.

Because he is not.

Tom goes back to the block.

* * *

## IX. The Letter from Plymouth

He writes Maggie that night.

He has not written to her since she put him out.

The first line takes longer than the rest of the page.

*Maggie—*

Then the crossing comes easily.

The fog.

A shark that followed them long enough for the cook to name it after Josiah Grimes.

Trees in Virginia so large that four men could fail to meet around one and blame one another for short arms.

The smell of England before sight of land.

He asks whether the Pelican roof still takes water at the north corner when the wind is south-west. Whether Hendricks still occupies the end of the bar as if paying rent by the year. Whether the old sign was finally painted.

He writes nothing about the settlement numbers.

Nothing about the bound man.

Nothing about the two names on the passenger list.

Nothing about the stick.

When he is finished he reads the page.

It sounds like a man trying to come through a locked door as a story.

Tom folds it.

He does not send it.

The letter goes into the bottom of his chest beside his mother's parish notice.

He tells himself he will decide later.

Later is useful that way.

* * *

## X. Gravesend

Winter passes in London without Tom going to Wapping.

He takes river work while he waits for a berth he has not chosen. Rigging inspections. A week on one ship. Three days on another. Enough to eat and enough to remain a man who is working rather than a man deciding what to do with himself.

In March 1617 he is at Gravesend to look at standing rigging on a vessel bound for the Low Countries when the Virginia party comes downriver for the *George*.

He sees them from the quay.

Not close.

Close enough to recognise the group.

They are delayed by weather.

Then word runs along the town that Rolfe's wife is ill.

Then that she has been taken ashore.

Then, days later, that she is dead.

The river continues moving through all three pieces of news.

Tom stands outside a chandler's shed with a list of blocks in his hand and hears the last one from a boy carrying bread.

Dead?

The boy nods.

Church has her.

Tom looks upriver.

Twenty-six miles, near enough.

He knows because sailors know distances to places they are not going.

A flood tide and a walk.

He could be at the Pelican before midnight if he wanted badly enough.

He has money in his pocket. No master expecting him until morning. Nothing physical prevents it.

That is worse than an ocean.

He walks to the river stairs.

A boat is taking passengers for London.

The boatman calls the last place.

Tom stands with one boot on the upper step.

In his head he sees the Pelican too clearly.

Maggie behind the bar.

Him coming through the door with a dead woman's story from Virginia and expecting, however carefully he denies it, that the story will prove something about the man bringing it.

He takes his boot off the step.

The boat leaves without him.

Tom goes back to the lodging house.

He never tells Maggie he was at Gravesend.

* * *

## XI. Same Man

Three days later he walks to Leadenhall.

He wants a long berth.

The East India Company has several.

The clerk knows his record. Boatswain. Swally. Good disciplinary reports except for one disagreement at a pay table which has somehow become, in the office version, evidence of forceful attention to crew welfare.

Surat?

Tom nods.

The paper comes across the rail.

He reads it from the bottom first.

Not because the bottom is always where harm lives.

Because he has learned that the parts a clerk assumes a sailor will skip are often the parts a sailor should not.

A name appears among the office headings.

Sir Thomas Smythe.

Governor.

Tom knows the name.

He knows it from the Virginia papers in his chest.

There it was Treasurer.

He looks up.

This Smythe.

The clerk waits.

Virginia Smythe.

The clerk frowns, then understands.

Sir Thomas? Yes.

Same man?

Of course the same man.

The clerk says it with the mild impatience of somebody asked whether Tuesday follows Monday.

Tom looks back at the paper.

He has spent three years treating two companies as opposite directions.

The man behind the rail has never needed to make the distinction.

Tom almost laughs.

The clerk asks, Is something wrong?

No.

And that is true.

The wage is clear. The berth exists. Tom understands the work. His father's debt is no longer the only reason he knows how to sign, but signing is still how he earns his living.

He reads the whole page.

Then signs it.

The clerk sands his name.

Six weeks, he says.

Tom takes his copy.

At the door he stops.

Does Smythe own both?

The clerk looks offended by the imprecision.

Own? No. Governor here. Treasurer there. Investor in half London if you listen to half London.

Tom nods.

That is more useful than the answer he asked for.

He leaves.

* * *

## XII. East Again

The ship lies at Blackwall.

Tom joins five weeks later.

His sea chest goes aboard first.

At the bottom of it is the letter from Plymouth.

Unsent.

Above it, his mother's parish notice.

Above that, shirts, spare line, a knife and the ordinary possessions of a man who has learned not to own anything he cannot move alone.

On the road west, somewhere beyond the yards, lies Wapping.

Tom never turns far enough to see it.

He checks the foremast shrouds.

He checks the water casks.

He checks the boys assigned to the first watch and discovers one does not know a clove hitch despite claiming he does.

One hand. One foot. Then the others, Tom tells him when the boy freezes aloft.

The boy comes down alive.

By evening the tide makes.

The ship drops toward Gravesend.

On one paper in Tom's chest, Sir Thomas Smythe is Treasurer.

On another, Governor.

Tom has stopped believing the title tells him which direction the world is going.

The river bends.

London goes out of sight.
