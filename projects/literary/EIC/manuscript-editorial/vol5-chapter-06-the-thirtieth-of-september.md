# The Thirtieth of September (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"For one merciful second the names mean nothing to her, are only two more of the autumn's dead."*
>
> *"The practice stops things. It does not avenge them."*
>
> — Wei Zhang, the cold yard, the morning after

***

## I. The Thirtieth of September

The last day of September is, until its final hours, a good one.

The weather has broken from its long gold summer into something crisper, a Sunday-into-Monday chill that makes the coppers welcome rather than merely tolerated, and the shop does brisk trade all through the Sunday on coats brought out early against a winter that has clearly decided to arrive ahead of its own calendar. Su spends the morning at the practice as she has every morning of her remembered life, and the afternoon on deliveries, and there is nothing in the ordinary rhythm of the day — the yard, the counter, the Prospect's chit settled at four, her mother's mending basket full of the week's small repairs — that marks this Sunday as different from the fifty-odd Sundays that came before it.

Catherine's bodice is still on the shelf in the back room, wrapped in its paper, waiting on the hopping money that has not yet come home. Elisabeth has not been in for a fortnight — not an unusual gap, in itself; Su has learned not to read weather into a gap.

It is the last ordinary evening either of them will ever have, though of course nobody in the shop or the Prospect or the whole wide unsuspecting reach of the river has the smallest way of knowing it, and Su will spend years afterward turning over the particular cruelty of that fact: that the worst nights arrive dressed exactly like the nights before them, offering no signal, keeping no different hours, asking nothing special of the day that is about to end.

The news comes up the dock road the next morning the way that autumn's news has always come: wrong twice before noon, then right, then very much worse than right.

Two women, this time, not one. Streets apart, within the same terrible hour, as though whatever moved through the city that night had grown impatient with taking its victims one at a season and decided, on this occasion, to take a full winter's harvest at once. The first found in a dark passage with her throat cut and, as the account has it, nothing else done — as though something, some sound or footstep, had come close enough to interrupt a design not yet finished. The second found in a square three-quarters of a mile further on, where nothing at all had interrupted anything, and where what had been done was of a character the papers will spend the following fortnight declining, in print, to describe, which decision, as Su has already learned to recognise, tells a reader considerably more than a plain description would have.

Su hears it in the Prospect itself, midway through the ordinary Monday errands, from a carman reading a late edition aloud to a room that has gone very quiet around him.

The paper does not have Su's names for either woman.

The paper has its own names — one it calls Long Liz, a name Su has never once used and does not, in this first confused moment, connect to anyone she knows; and a second name, a surname this time, that the carman stumbles over the way print makes strangers of people you actually know, the syllables coming out clumsy and unfamiliar in his mouth. For one entire, merciful second the two names mean nothing whatever to Su. They are only two more entries in an autumn that has already produced entries enough, two more women she has never met, added to a column that has been filling since the end of August and does not yet feel, in that first second, like a column with her own name anywhere near it.

Then the details arrive, riding in just behind the names the way a second wave always follows the first.

A tall woman. A Swede. Grief for a drowned family she used to tell strangers about, at a bar, over an accent she could put on and take off like a bonnet. And a second woman, small and quick, back early from the Kent hopping, broke as ever, everyone agreeing afterward that she'd come home broke, the hop money never having quite materialised the way the promises of September always insisted it would.

The two names the paper is using turn, in Su's hands, into the two names she has been using all this while, and she understands, all at once and with a completeness that leaves no room in it for doubt, precisely whom the night has taken.

She does not fall down. She stands with a basket of clean shirts on her hip and a delivery docket in her other hand, and she does not fall down, though something in her that has been standing perfectly upright for nineteen years shifts, in that one moment, onto a foundation it will spend years afterward learning to trust again.

There is a second thing the same column gives her, arriving in the same flat print, in the same breath as the names, and it is this second thing, more than the first, that Su will carry longest.

She learns, standing there, what the paper means when it calls them unfortunates. She learns, in the space of the same sentence that tells her Elisabeth and Catherine are dead, the thing she had never once in three years of Mondays and mendings thought to ask about either of them while they were living: why they were out on those streets, in that particular dark, at that particular hour, on the worst nights the worst autumn in living memory has been able to produce. It does not lessen either woman by a single farthing, this knowledge, arriving as late and as uselessly as it does. It only tells Su, with a clarity she would give a great deal not to have been given, how much of the two women she loved best of all her customers had lived their whole lives in a room she was never once shown into, under names she had never once heard spoken, in a danger she had been washing their linen straight through the middle of without ever once seeing it standing there.

She thinks, walking home with the basket gone strangely heavy on her hip, of the quarter of an hour at the counter with Elisabeth — the two of them understood, briefly and exactly, in a grief with floors and walls. She thinks of Catherine's half-songs, the girl on the quay whose ending neither of them will now ever learn, because the one person carrying the missing half of it down to Kent this September did not, in the end, come home to deliver it.

Her mother hears the second name before Su can bring herself to say either aloud, from a neighbour calling it in at the shop door with the particular relish some people cannot help bringing to bad news that is, thankfully, somebody else's. Sau-Ling Zhang, who has buried a country and two sisters and a son and calls weeping a Tuesday, sits down that evening over the parcel that has sat wrapped and waiting on the back-room shelf since the last Monday of the summer, and cries the way stones would cry if stones were ever given the chance.

She says, that evening, not quite to Su and not quite to anyone, the thing Su will keep the longest of everything said in that whole terrible week — says it to the paper on the counter, and to the God she was dipped into at the mission church by the docks, and perhaps to nobody at all. She has never once believed the holy book balanced, she says. If it balanced, a place like this — the docks, the Causeway, the whole poor huddled reach of the river — would be the proof there was nobody keeping the accounts honestly. She washes for the drowned and the used-up and the never-paid, she says, smoothing the parcel's paper flat under the side of her hand as though pressing out a seam that will not, this time, lie flat no matter how it is pressed, because a God who ran his books the way the fine gentlemen run theirs would not be worth the walk to church of a Sunday. The debt of a place like Wapping is meant to be carried at a loss, she says. That is the whole of what mercy is: a column that will never once come right, kept open anyway, on purpose, forever.

It is the nearest thing to a creed Su has ever heard her mother speak whole, and it is the precise opposite of her father's creed, whose every rule exists to make a ledger close, and Su, standing in the narrow yard between the two of them as she has stood her whole life, files her mother's words with everything else she is made of, and says nothing, because there is nothing to say that would not diminish what has just been said.

Her mother stands, eventually, and finishes the day's pressing, because grief in Shadwell is a luxury taken in instalments, and the shop still has washing owed to living customers who will want it in the morning regardless of what the night has taken from anyone else.

Her father closes the shutters himself that night, which he has not done with his own two hands in years — has left it to Su, without either of them remarking on the handover, since her wrists grew steady enough to trust with the high bolt. Tonight he does it himself, walking the shop-front slowly, testing each fastening the way a man reads a thing through his fingertips when his eyes have stopped being entirely sure of what they are seeing. The high bolt, the stiff one at the top, refuses him. His hand sets against it and closes, and Su watches the cold iron sit in his knuckles, and the bolt does not move, and he stands with his hand against it a moment longer than a man stands with a thing he still, technically, can do.

Su reaches past him without being asked and drives it home in one motion, ten thousand mornings folded into the single push.

Neither of them says anything. There is nothing to say that would not be worse than the quiet. But through the back of her own hand against his, for the length of that one motion, Su feels the exact thing Elisabeth once told her about words with floors and walls — feels it, here, in a language that needs neither of the two she owns: that her father is growing old, that she is the stronger of the two of them now in the one specific way that matters at a shop door at night, and that the night the whole East End learned to be properly afraid is the same night she found this out about her own house.

She does not cry either, that night, any more than she fell down in the Prospect that afternoon. Something else happens instead, further down and considerably worse: a long, patient heat, banked the way the wash-copper banks its coals overnight, that does not gutter, and does not spread, and — this is the part that frightens her, lying awake with it, more than any fear the newspapers have managed to print all autumn — does not, however she waits for it, go out.

She takes it to her father in the cold yard before the next day's dawn, because she has learned not to trust a fire she cannot account for, and because he is the only person she has ever known who has carried one exactly like it.

## II. The Rules for This

She has not slept, and does not try to pretend otherwise, and goes out to the yard a full hour before the dawn she would ordinarily meet there, because the banked heat under her ribs has kept its own hours all night regardless of what her body wanted, and lying in the dark doing nothing with it had begun, somewhere past three in the morning, to feel like its own kind of danger.

Her father is already there.

He does not ask why she has come early. He has, she understands without either of them saying so, been expecting her — has perhaps been standing in the cold longer than his own habit requires for exactly this reason, the way a man leaves a lamp lit past its usual hour on a night he suspects someone might need to find their way to him. He is working through the form slowly in the near-dark, breath smoking, the wash-copper not yet lit behind him, and he does not stop moving when Su comes to stand at the yard's edge, because he has never once, in her whole life, stopped a form partway simply because someone had arrived to watch the rest of it.

What are the rules, she asks him, into the cold. For this.

She does not need to say what *this* is. The whole East End knows what *this* is this particular morning; it has needed no further naming since the carman read the second name aloud in the Prospect the afternoon before.

Her father does not answer at once. He finishes the movement he is in the middle of — a slow turn of the wrist that Su has watched him make ten thousand times and has still, at nineteen, not entirely unlocked the full meaning of — and only then, without breaking into the next one, does he speak.

The rules don't change because you are bleeding, he says. That is what makes them rules, and not moods. A mood changes with the weather. A rule that changes with the weather was never a rule to begin with; it was only a preference wearing a rule's clothes, waiting for an excuse to show what it actually was.

He moves through another two counts before he goes on.

The practice stops things, he says. It does not avenge them.

Su stands with her arms wrapped around herself against the cold and says nothing, because she has learned, over twelve years in this yard, that her father's silences between sentences are not gaps to be filled but weights being set down carefully, one at a time, so that the next thing he says will have something solid under it.

If you go looking for a debt to collect, daughter, he says at last, you will find one. The world is never short of debts. It manufactures them faster than any ledger could ever hope to close them, and a person who goes looking with the specific intention of finding one to settle will always, always find it, because the finding was never really the difficult part. The difficult part comes after.

He stops moving, finally, and turns to face her directly, which he does rarely enough in the yard that the stillness of it lands almost as heavily as anything he has said.

And you will collect it, he says, because you are good at what I have taught you, and because being good at a thing and being right to do it are not the same fact wearing two names, however much a frightened heart wants them to be. And then, having collected it, you will be a different person than the one standing in front of me now. And I will have taught that different person everything she knows. Every stance. Every rule. The fan, the wrist, the listening through a touched arm. All of it, spent on a decision I never taught you to make, because I never once taught you that a debt existed which was yours to collect rather than the world's to carry.

Su is not yet twenty years old. She stands in the cold yard, her breath a pale rag in front of her face, and hears, for the first time laid out with this much precision, the exact width of the difference between stopping a thing that is happening and avenging a thing that has already happened — and understands, with a clarity that frightens her rather more than the newspapers' careful, appalling restraint has managed to frighten her all autumn, that the difference is no wider than a knife, and that she cannot yet say, standing here, which side of that knife's edge she herself is currently standing on.

I want it to be him, she says. Whoever it is. I want it to be someone I can reach.

I know, her father says. There is no reproach in it. Everyone in Wapping wants that this week. Every man in the Prospect who has picked up a cudgel this month wants exactly that, and every one of them believes, the way you believe it right now, that wanting it this badly is itself a kind of proof — that a want this large and this clean cannot possibly be the wrong thing to want.

Isn't it?

Her father considers the question with the same gravity he gives every question Su has ever asked him in this yard, weighing it the way he weighs a column of figures that will not close.

It is the most natural want in the world, he says. That is exactly what makes it dangerous. The wants that are easiest to justify are the ones that need the least watching, because nobody troubles to watch a thing everybody already agrees is reasonable.

He turns back into the form, and Su understands the conversation, for this morning, has reached the edge of what either of them can usefully say past. But before he finishes the turn he adds one thing more, quietly, not quite to her and not quite to the cold air either.

Grief and anger feel identical in the chest, he says. That is the whole trouble with both of them. It takes a very long practice to learn to tell them apart before you have already acted on whichever one it was.

Su stands in the yard until the sky greys properly over the wall, working through her own form beside her father's, matching his pace without either of them remarking on it, and by the time the wash-copper is lit and the first ordinary demands of the ordinary day have begun arriving — linen to sort, a docket to write, a shop to open onto a street that will, this particular Monday, be quieter and more watchful than any Monday Su has yet lived through — the banked heat under her ribs has not gone out.

It has only, for the length of one cold hour in a familiar yard, agreed to wait.
