# The Case (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"A man my age should keep a steadier hand."*
>
> — Dr. Reginald Cray, of a razor cut, unasked

***

## I. A Terrible Autumn

Cray offers his condolence the same week the second funeral is arranged, in the ordinary way he offers everything: unhurried, exactly weighted, costing him nothing visible to give.

A terrible autumn, he says to Su, settling his coin on the bar with the small precise motion he uses for every coin he has ever settled anywhere. I understand you knew one of the women. I am sorry for it.

There is nothing in his face but an old man's sympathy. Su has looked at that face for seven years across this same bar and has never once, until this particular evening, tried to read it the way she reads a length of rope for the place where the fibres have started to give — and she finds, trying now, that she cannot. It is either the truest thing about him, or the most frightening fact in the whole of this story, and Su understands, standing there with his condolence in her hands like a coin she has not yet decided whether to spend, that she may never in her life find out which.

I knew two of them, she says. Not one.

Something moves behind his eyes then — not guilt, she will tell herself later, and later still not be sure she believes it; only the correction a careful man makes when he learns he has understated a fact by half. Two, he says. Then I am doubly sorry. He does not ask their names. He has never once asked their names, this whole autumn, though half the Prospect has traded them back and forth across the bar like coin of a lesser value, and Su notices the not-asking only afterward, lying awake, the way she notices most things about him only afterward, always a beat behind the moment that mattered.

He is, this October, exactly as he has been every October for fifteen years. Two brandies. The same stool. The good leather case set against the leg of it, oiled, unremarkable, the clasp catching the lamp the way it always has. He asks after Sau-Ling's chest with the coming of the cold and recommends, as he always does, the camphor, still, even though the summer that made it seem an odd thing to say has been over a month now. He climbs no flights this autumn — nobody in the house has taken a fever worth climbing for — but he sets, in the second week of October, a child's dislocated finger with the same economy he brought to the docker's shoulder seven years back, the same level attention, the same nothing on his face while the child screams and the mother thanks God for the doctor being in and Su, watching from behind a stack of clean linen, watches the attention instead of the hand and finds, for the first time she can name, that the level nothing has stopped reading to her as calm and started reading as something she does not yet have the English or the Cantonese for.

She thinks of her father's own hands, that same week, closing on the high bolt and refusing him, the cold sitting in his knuckles a half-second longer than it used to. Two sets of aging hands, in the same small stretch of river, doing entirely different work with the years that are catching up to both of them — her father's failing by degrees he cannot hide, however he tries; Cray's, so far as the bar can tell, failing not at all, steady at sixty-one as they were steady at forty-six, and Su understands, without yet being able to say why the thought frightens her, that a hand which does not shake with age is not necessarily a hand that has been spared. It may only be a hand that was never, in the first place, connected to anything that could shake it.

She and Dr. Cray are, though neither of them would ever frame it this way, and though neither of them yet knows it will matter, the Company's two long echoes met in one room this whole terrible autumn: a surgeon its wars taught to be calm, and a girl its opium war washed out of Canton two generations back. The machine has been dead thirty years. Its consequences are only now, in the cold of this particular October, beginning to reach the bar in earnest.

## II. The Clasp

It is a raw evening in the last week of October when she sees inside the case for the first time.

He sets it on the bar while he pays her — pays the shop's account, settled monthly, a habit as old as his stool — just for a moment, just long enough to free both hands for his coin purse, and the clasp has come slightly loose, worn at the hinge the way fifteen years of the same motion will wear anything, and Su, who has spent a childhood noticing which knots are tied wrong on which crates because a wrong knot on the docks costs somebody money, sees, for less than a second, what a leather physician's case ought to hold and does not.

No rolled bandages. No vials, no lint, no small dark bottles of the sort a house call wants. Something wrapped in oilcloth instead, long enough to run the case's full length, folded close, the corners tucked the particular way a careful man tucks a thing he means to keep dry and quiet both.

By the time she has registered this the clasp is closed again and Dr. Cray is smiling his usual unhurried smile and asking whether her mother's rheumatism has improved with the turning weather.

She says it has. She takes her coin, and the coin is somehow the worst of it, warm from his pocket, ordinary as any coin in the till, exactly the coin it would be if the last five seconds had not happened at all. She does not run, because running would tell him something, and she has already understood, in the animal, unarguable part of the mind that does the understanding before the rest of you catches up, that this is not a man it is safe to alarm.

That night, lying awake with the shop's soap-smell still in her own hair and the ceiling refusing, as ceilings do on such nights, to be looked away from, her memory begins handing her things she did not know it had kept.

The first morning of October — the morning after the thirtieth — Cray at his stool an hour earlier than his habit, with a small fresh cut high on his cheekbone, and the way he touched it, unasked, and said, to nobody in particular: razor. A man my age should keep a steadier hand. She has folded ten thousand shirts to the rhythm of that room's talk and never once thought of the sentence since. She thinks of it now, turning it slowly, the way she turns a knot before she decides which strand to pull.

And because her father raised her to read a knot from both ends, always, or not at all, she makes herself think the other things too, with the same patience. Old men do cut themselves shaving; it is one of the small unfair jokes age plays on a steady hand, that the hand stays steady at everything except the one blade it uses on its own face every morning without troubling to look properly. Surgeons do own knives; a long blade wrapped in oilcloth is not, by itself, a crime — it is very possibly nothing more than a retirement, a tool a man cannot quite bring himself to sell along with the rest of a life he has otherwise put down. A man may walk out late on any given night because sleep, she has heard her mother say of her own father, leaves a body earlier and earlier the longer that body has been asked to carry itself through the world. Every strand of it, examined honestly, can be tied two ways. She has known this her whole life. She makes herself know it now, deliberately, the way you make yourself hold a stance a moment past comfortable because the discomfort is the only proof the stance is being held at all.

She starts asking, all the same. Not directly — not yet, not to him, not to anyone who might carry a question back to him before she is ready for it to arrive. She asks Flo, over a delivery, how long the doctor has kept rooms nearby, and receives an answer she already knew — fifteen years, since before Flo's own time at the bar properly started — offered without any weight in it at all, the way you'd answer a question about the weather.

## III. What the Doctor Saw

She goes back to the dockworker.

She has turned his September story over more times than she could count by the last week of October, the story he told sideways, unasked, to a room he thought was only half listening — the regimental surgeon who wrote *died of fever* over men flogged near to death, whose hand never once shook signing it, because the thing costing the room its steadiness had never, in the whole span of that war, cost the surgeon anything at all. She has told herself for a month that the story is only a story, a piece of one old soldier's bitterness carried home from a country neither of them has ever seen, and that carrying it forward onto a kind man on a familiar stool is exactly the kind of leap her father has spent her whole life teaching her not to make on an empty stomach of evidence.

She asks him anyway, on a quiet evening, carefully, the way you ask a question you have already half decided you do not want answered.

What was Company medical service, she asks him. In Bengal. Before the Mutiny. What did it actually mean, day to day, for the men who held the post.

He looks at her a long moment before he answers, the particular look of a man deciding whether a question has been asked out of idle curiosity or out of something with weight behind it, and Su understands, watching him decide, that he has already guessed which.

It meant good pay and a long leash, he says finally. It meant a man answerable to almost nobody who was not also, in some fashion, answerable to him. The regiment needed its surgeon kept sweet more than the surgeon ever needed the regiment, because a surgeon who left in temper could not easily be replaced eleven thousand miles from home, and every colonel out there knew it, and every surgeon out there knew that every colonel knew it. He pauses. It made some men gentle with the power, because gentleness cost them nothing and bought them a great deal of goodwill among men who might one day need to trust their hands. It made other men something else. Calm men, mostly. The power did not need using loudly, out there. A calm man could get everything he wanted from it simply by being the only person in a room who never raised his voice.

Su asks whether such a man — a surgeon, retired now, come home with his pension and his rooms and his good name — whether such a man would still carry the habits the post had given him. Whether calm, learned that way, in that country, under that kind of leash, ever properly comes off a man once the leash itself is gone.

The dockworker considers the question with a gravity Su recognises, because it is the same gravity her own father brings to a column of figures that will not close.

I couldn't tell you for certain, he says. I only fought there. I didn't doctor there. But I'll tell you what I noticed of the ones who came home calm and stayed calm, all the years after, whatever the provocation — and I noticed a fair few, because a man who has been under fire develops an eye for who else has, and who hasn't, whatever their papers say. The calm that costs a man something to keep, that calm wears thin eventually, somewhere, in front of somebody, because keeping a hard thing down takes a hand off something else, always, sooner or later. But the calm that never cost him anything in the first place — that calm doesn't wear at all. It doesn't need to. There was never any effort in it to begin with, so there's nothing in it to tire.

He does not say Cray's name. Su does not say it either. But she understands, walking home that night with the basket lighter than the walk should make it feel, that the two of them have just had an entire conversation about a man neither has named, and that the not-naming has made the conversation more honest, not less, because a name would have given them both something to disagree about instead of the plain shape of the thing itself.

She thinks of Lee's fever, that counted coin, the register's one flat word. She thinks of the docker's shoulder and the level nothing that set it. She thinks of the razor cut in October, the coat and the case and the clasp come loose, the oilcloth shape inside it exactly as long as a man's forearm. None of it, laid separately on her own private counter, amounts to an accusation any court in England would trouble to hear. All of it, laid together, amounts to something she has no name for in either of her languages, and does not go looking for one, because her father raised her to know that a name found too early is a name that stops a person looking any further.

## IV. The Shirt

It comes round by his housekeeper on the Tuesday: one shirt, good linen gone soft with decades of careful keeping, older than its owner's coats. The first washing Dr. Cray has sent the shop in fifteen years, arriving the week after his clasp came loose in front of her.

Her mother takes it in at the counter as though it were a shirt, because to her mother, who was not standing at the bar the evening the clasp gave, it is only a shirt — the first in fifteen years from a good steady customer who has finally, perhaps, decided the shop's washing is worth the trying. Su washes it as though it were a shirt too, in front of her mother, in the ordinary rhythm of the ordinary morning. It is not a shirt. It is a question, put the only way a careful man can put a question without ever once having asked one out loud: *what does the laundry girl's face do, over my linen, now that she has seen what she has seen?*

She reads every seam of it the way she reads a knot. Works the collar and the cuffs through her own fingers in the copper's rising steam, presses her face down once, briefly, into the wet cloth of it, the way any laundress tests for what a stain has actually been before she commits lye and effort to lifting it out.

There is nothing.

Soap, and under the soap, nothing — not tobacco, not the particular sourness of a man's own sweat carried three streets from his rooms, not medicine, not anything a linen usually keeps folded into it besides the wearing. It is linen with nothing at all to say, and from a man like Dr. Cray that is not innocence. It is grammar. A careful man does not send a shirt with something in it by accident, any more than he leaves a clasp loose by accident, and Su understands, wringing it through her hands in the steam, that she is being shown, deliberately, precisely nothing — which is itself the largest thing anyone has shown her all autumn.

She starves it of an answer the way she starved the question of one. Boiled, pressed, folded exactly as every shirt in the shop is folded, the ticket pinned where the ticket always goes, and her face, when the housekeeper collects it on the Friday, is the shop's face and nothing else's — the face of a girl who has washed a shirt, because that is what she has, in point of fact, done.

Only afterward, wringing an unrelated sheet in the yard with considerably more force than the sheet requires, does she let herself understand what the week has cost her, and what it has bought him.

He is watching her now. That is the shirt's plain meaning, whatever else it does or does not say, and it cuts in both directions at once, the way the truest things always do in this particular story: because a man who needs to know what a laundry girl's face does over his own linen is a man who has begun to wonder what a laundry girl saw. And a man who wonders that has, by wondering it, told her the one thing his fifteen years of unhurried kindness never once told her on their own.

That there was, after all, something in the case worth a man's wondering.
