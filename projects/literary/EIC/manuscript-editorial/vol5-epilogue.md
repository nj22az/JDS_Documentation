# Epilogue: The Dawn Yard, Again

> **A morning in the late 1930s**
>
> *"Some debts are not for closing."*
>
> — Sau-Ling Zhang, near the end of her own life

***

The yard is six paces by four, and it has not grown by a single stone in the seventy years since a boy first stood in it not understanding a word of what was being asked of him, and Su Zhang, old now in the particular way a person becomes old without ever quite noticing the transaction taking place, stands in it still, most dawns, though she no longer holds the horse the full length her father once required of her, and has made her peace with that the way she has made her peace with a great many other lengths this life has quietly shortened without asking her leave.

Her great-grandchild holds it instead, this particular morning, seven years old, uncomprehending, exact, the fourth small pair of knees Su has pressed out over the fourth small pair of feet in this same yard across the whole span of her own remembered life.

Four pairs, and behind them the fan, and behind the fan a line of hands Su could recite in her sleep and has, more than once, aloud, to children too young to know what they were being given.

Her grandfather's, which she never saw, on a hillside above a harbour none of them will ever see, correcting elbows in a school that shut its doors to a war fought over a drug.

Her father's, which raised it over her own elbow every dawn from the age of seven and went on raising it, a half-beat slower each winter, until a grey morning in 1897 when he held it out and said four words.

Her own, which have had it now for forty-one years, and which set it down on the low wall between them every morning of the two years he had left, where either of them could reach it.

And her eldest's, which will have it next, and which Su has watched since 1902 and considers — privately, in the ledger nobody else reads — the steadiest pair of hands this family has produced in four generations, including her own. The child does not yet know why the stance is called what it is called, nor in which of two languages the name is meant to be kept, nor that a boy once ran a crowded mile of a Hong Kong waterfront to press a stranger down into a waiting sampan for reasons the stranger never once learned, nor that a fan now worn to the colour and the smoothness of an old knuckle has corrected an elbow in this yard every single dawn since a school in Canton closed its doors to a war fought over a drug the child has never heard the name of and never, if the family's whole quiet effort across five generations means anything at all, will need to learn the name of by suffering it.

The child only knows that the stance is hard, and that it is required, and that the very old woman correcting the knee has a way of saying *good* so rarely that the word, when it finally comes, seems to weigh a great deal more than one syllable ought to be able to weigh.

Su has outlived nearly everyone this book has ever properly introduced.

Her mother, thirty years gone now, buried beside a husband and a son in a small churchyard the mission still tends. Erik, twelve years gone, his own knees having finally refused him altogether on a quiet Tuesday afternoon in his chair by the window, the crimson flower blooming its unreasonable June argument in the same glass a season after he stopped being able to see it, which Su has never once decided whether to find cruel or only ordinary. Flo, further back than that, the Prospect itself passed twice now to keepers neither of them ever met, the short-legged chair, so far as anyone still troubles to report it, propped level still with whatever playbill happens currently to be doing the propping, left for last the way it has always been left for last, the reason as thoroughly forgotten now as it was the first year Su ever asked after it.

The five names the papers still occasionally trouble to resurrect — a fresh theory every decade or so, a doctor this time, a foreign sailor the next, a policeman's confession printed forty years after the fact by an editor glad of the circulation — Su reads still, out of the same old discipline, whenever one crosses her path, and lays each fresh guess against the private ledger she has kept closed to every living soul since a November morning the century itself has nearly finished forgetting. The answer at the bottom of the sum has not changed in the whole of that time, and she does not expect, at her age, that it ever now will.

It fits, and it does not prove.

She has stopped, some years back, being troubled by the not-proving. There was a decade, in the middle of her life, when the unclosed account sat in her the way the banked heat once sat under her ribs the whole of that terrible autumn — patient, present, demanding to be accounted for by someone, someday, in some ledger somewhere. It does not sit that way any longer. She has come, in her old age, to understand what her mother tried to tell her at the very end of her own life, on a spring morning thirty years gone, about the honest loss being worth more than the tidy lie: that some sums are permitted to stay open, on purpose, forever, and that a life spent trying to force one closed by main force is a worse life than one spent simply carrying the weight of it, evenly, the way a good basket is carried, so that a stranger passing on the street would never once guess it was there.

Catherine's bodice hangs now, has hung for the better part of forty years, in a frame in the chandlery's back room, where it once sat wrapped in its paper on a shelf, paid for and never collected. Su could never bring herself to unpick the seams and use the cloth, and neither, in her own turn, could her mother, and the two of them settled, without ever quite deciding to settle it, on framing the thing instead — an account kept open in a shape a stranger might mistake for decoration, though anyone in the family who has ever asked after it has been told, plainly, exactly what it is and exactly whose it was.

The white bowl stands on its own high shelf still, dusted by whichever hand in the family happens, this particular season, to be the one charged with dusting it, a duty passed down now four generations without a single one of those generations ever once questioning why a plain rice bowl from a Hong Kong tea stall deserves the keeping. Some debts are not for closing, Su's mother told her, near the very end, and Su has taught the sentence to every child who has ever stood in this yard, in both her languages, the way her father taught her the four rules before either of them, in the sentence itself, existed yet as words a child could give back.

The great-grandchild holds the stance a moment longer than the small determined legs would naturally choose, and Su, watching from behind, feels the particular ache of an old woman's own knees answering the cold in fellow-feeling with a child's, and thinks, not for the first time this decade, that a life is a very long series of dawns in the same six paces by four, each one nearly identical to the one before it, and that the sameness, rather than being the flaw a younger woman might once have taken it for, has turned out, across the whole unlikely length of things, to be the entire point.

Bridge, she says.

The child brings small arms up into the beginning shape of something that will not, for some years yet, deserve the name *kiu sau*, and Su steps in, and lays her own worn forearm across the child's, and begins, once more, in the grey light, before the counter opens, the oldest conversation this yard has ever properly kept.

It is not a lesson and there is nothing in it to watch. Two people stand in a small cold yard with their forearms crossed at the wrist, doing, to all appearances, nothing whatsoever, for eleven or twelve minutes at a stretch. Su's own father did this with her every dawn for thirteen years and never once explained what it was for, and she has never once explained it either, and she has come to believe, at sixty-nine, that this is not reticence but accuracy: that there is no sentence in either of her languages which describes it and does not immediately make it sound like something else.

What is actually happening is that the child is learning to feel a stranger's intention arrive in her own arm before it has finished being an intention. That is all. It has no other content. It takes about nine years to be any good at and about forty to stop having to think about, and there is no shortcut anywhere in it, and the whole apparatus exists so that on one particular afternoon which may never come, a person will already be turning while everybody else in the lane is still deciding whether anything is wrong.

Su has had that afternoon. Exactly once, in sixty-nine years.

She has never told the child anything about it and never will, and neither will anybody else, because there is nobody left alive who could.

The papers on the counter this year have Germany in them again, in the same typeface and very nearly the same sentences as the last time, and the reach is doing what the reach did the last time, which is read them aloud to one another in doorways and then go to work.

Su has stopped reading that column. She read it for four years the first time and it did not alter one single thing that came out of the sky, and she has a shop to open, and there is a boy of nineteen in this family who has his great-grandfather's shoulders, and Su does not permit herself to think past that sentence and has not since the autumn the column started up again.

What she does instead is the thing this family has always done with what it cannot answer. She gets up at half past four. She stands in the yard.

She listens.

Through the small crossed wrist she can feel, the way she has felt it in four generations of dawns now, exactly where a weight is settling before the child has finished deciding to settle it there — and somewhere far off, past the wall, past the Causeway, past the whole waking business of a river that has taken everything this house has ever fed it and given back, in exchange, one clean stretch of ordinary mornings, a cart starts its iron complaint over the cobbles toward the dock, the way a cart has started that same complaint every single dawn since before any Zhang stood in this yard at all, and will, Su supposes, go on starting it every dawn after the last of them has finally, one morning, simply not come out to hear it.

The fan waits in the drawer behind her, worn smooth as an old knuckle, waiting on the hand that will next need raising it.

The yard has perhaps a quarter of an hour left of being only the yard.
