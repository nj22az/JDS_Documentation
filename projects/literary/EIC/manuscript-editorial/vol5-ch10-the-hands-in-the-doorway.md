# The Hands in the Doorway (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"A whisper against a foreigner is a torch."*

***

The word Leather Apron does something to the East End that the word Ripper, when it finally arrives, will only make worse: it gives the fear a face, and having no actual face to put to it, the fear goes looking for one among the faces the street was already a little afraid of before any of this began.

It is not the Zhangs' street first. It is a Polish Jew's, two neighbourhoods over, a bootmaker with a temper and a habit of shouting at his wife loudly enough that the whole court knows the sound of it — a man, in other words, whom a frightened street can point to and say, there, that one, that is the kind of man who does a thing like this, without needing to trouble itself over whether he has ever in his life laid a hand on anyone but his own household. He is walked into a police station bleeding above one eye within the week, for the crime, as far as Su can make out from what reaches the Prospect, of resembling somebody's idea of somebody.

It reaches the Causeway inside a fortnight.

Nothing so organised as a mob, not yet, not at their end of the docks — but a change in the weather that Su can read the way she reads a tide, from small signs long before the water itself arrives. A costermonger who has sold vegetables at the corner of the Causeway for as long as Su can remember stops nodding to her father in the mornings. A group of dockworkers, waiting on a tide, spend an idle half-hour outside a Chinese lodging-house loudly discussing, to nobody in particular and everybody in earshot, the sort of men who come from foreign parts and keep to themselves and who's to say what such men get up to behind their own shut doors. Windows along Pennyfields begin coming down at dusk instead of after full dark, shutters going up early on houses that have never in Su's lifetime troubled to shutter before midnight.

Her father starts standing in the shop doorway at closing.

He does not announce that he is doing it, and if Su asked him outright why, he would very likely answer with something about the evening air, or the state of the step, or some other plain fact that happened also to be true and also to be entirely beside the point. But she watches him do it three evenings running before she understands what she is watching: Wei Zhang, at the hour the shop would ordinarily be bolted and dark, standing instead in his own open doorway with the lamp behind him and both hands folded together in front of him, visible, still, unmistakably a man doing nothing with his hands but holding them where the street can see them doing nothing.

It is, Su realises, the practice turned to a use she has never once seen her father put it to. Not a stance. Not a form. A pure, deliberate absence of threat, offered to a street that has started looking at foreign faces and deciding, on no evidence beyond the foreignness, that violence might live behind them. Her father has spent his whole life carrying, in his own body, the one thing this particular fear is hunting for, and he has answered it the only way the rules permit: not by hiding what he is, which he could not do even if he wished to, and not by explaining himself, which nobody hunting a monster is in the mood to listen to — but by standing in the light, hands empty and folded, for as long as the street cares to look, until looking at him has become so boring, so plainly unrewarding, that the street's attention moves on to find its monster somewhere else.

It costs him something to do it. Su can see that too, though he would never say so. A man who has spent forty years being underestimated by larger men and turning the underestimating to his own account is not built to stand still and be looked at; every year in the yard has trained the opposite instinct, has trained him to be the one thing in the room a careless eye passes over. Standing in his own doorway, deliberately visible, deliberately unthreatening, asks him to hold himself directly against the grain of everything the practice ever taught him, and he does it anyway, every evening, because the rules that stop a fight in an alley are, it turns out, the same rules that can be made to stand quietly in a doorway and let a frightened street satisfy itself there is nothing here worth its fear.

Never first, Su thinks, watching him from behind the counter. Never for show. She understands, watching, that her father has found a fourth use for a rule built for three, and has not asked anyone's permission to do it.

Sau-Ling does her own version of the same work in a different register entirely. She has always gone to the mission church on a Sunday and always will, but this September she goes early, and stays late, and makes a point, in her furred, twenty-years-borrowed English, of being seen and heard thanking the minister loudly for his sermon, of asking after the health of every English neighbour in the pew, of being, in short, so entirely and visibly a Christian woman of the parish that a stranger would have to work very hard indeed to file her anywhere else. Su understands this for what it is too: her mother's own doorway, held the same way her father holds his, in a different coin.

The dockworker who fought in the Mutiny — the one who will, in a few weeks, hand Su the one fact she needs without knowing he has done it — comes into the Prospect one evening in the middle of this and says, to nobody and everybody, that he doesn't hold with any of it, the foreigner-hunting, never has. Man's a monster or he isn't, he says. Don't much matter what god he prays to or what his mother called him. A monster's a monster in every language, and I never once met a language that had the monopoly on making one.

Flo pours him an extra half without being asked. It is the nearest thing to a public defence the Zhangs receive that autumn, and neither the dockworker nor Flo ever mentions it again, and Su never thanks either of them for it directly, because there is no way to thank a person for a kindness without naming, out loud, the thing the kindness was defending against, and naming it would cost more than the thanks could ever repay.

Her father is in the doorway again the following evening, hands folded, lamp behind him, waiting for the street to grow bored of looking. It does, eventually, most nights. Su has begun to understand, watching him, that this is what the rules are actually for, underneath the alley and the wrist-locks and the fan raised against a lazy elbow: not the ability to end a fight quickly, which is only ever the last and smallest part of it, but the much harder, much longer discipline of living every ordinary day in a body the world has decided, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything you have actually done, to be a little afraid of — and doing it, every single evening, without once giving the fear anything real to feed on.
