# What the River Kept (1899–1930s)

> **A later year**
>
> *"The watchman's daughter is the watchman."*

***

## I. The Keeper of the Yard

The yard belongs to Su now, in every way a yard can belong to a person who does not own the freehold and has never once thought to ask who does.

She stands in it every dawn her father once stood in it, the horse held the same four-level depth he taught her, the fan in her own hand now, worn no smoother by her thirty years of carrying it than it was worn already by his sixty and his own father's unrecorded span before that. She corrects her own eldest's elbow the exact half-inch her father corrected hers, and recites the rules in Cantonese, and waits, and makes the child give them back to her in English, because a rule known in only one language is a rule only half known, and she has never once found reason to doubt that this, of everything her father taught her, was the truest thing he owned.

There are, by the turn of the new century, more small hands in the yard than her own alone raised. Her eldest, a daughter with her father's unhurried shoulders and her mother's watching eyes, stands in the stance at seven precisely as Su once stood, and precisely as Lee once stood before her, a fact Su does not say aloud to the child and does think, every single morning, standing behind her in the grey light correcting a knee that wants to lock the wrong way. A second child comes two years after the first, a boy, quicker to laugh than either of his sisters — for there is a third by 1902, small and serious, who takes to the listening, the chi sao, faster than any of the others and faster, if Su is honest with the ledger she still keeps of herself in a script only she can read, than Su herself ever took to it at that age.

She is the Causeway's bonesetter now, in the plain way her father was before her and his own father before him on a different waterfront on the far side of the world. A dockworker's dislocated shoulder, set on the flagstones of the very Prospect where a docker's shoulder was once set before her, years before she had a name for what her own eye was catching in another man's hands. A wrist broken in a fall from a lighter. A child's collarbone, cracked in a game gone too rough. She sets them all with the fan and the two hands the fan has taught, and she never once takes payment beyond the ordinary going rate a bonesetter takes on this reach, because never for payment was never a rule that stopped applying merely because the hands doing the setting had grown into a woman's rather than a girl's.

They come to the back door, mostly, rather than the shop door, because the back door is the one her father used and because there is a difference on that street between the place you take a shirt and the place you take an arm, and the street has always understood it without needing it explained.

She reads an injury with her hands before she looks at it. This is not a decision; it is simply the order the information arrives in. A man holds out a wrist and Su's fingers are on the two bones either side of the joint before the man has finished describing what happened, and by the time he gets to the part about the barrel she already knows more about it than he does, and is only waiting politely for him to finish because a person who has been frightened needs to tell it.

There is a lighterman with a jaw out on its hinge, in the winter of 1903, who has been carrying it for a day and a half because he was ashamed of how it happened. There is a nursemaid's child, over and over across the years — the small pulled elbow that any grown-up on that street will produce eventually by swinging a two-year-old up a kerb by one hand — which comes in screaming and goes out in under four seconds asleep, and which never fails to make Su think of her own son and a stool and a flat iron. There is a whole family of dock hands with the same shoulder, father and two sons across nineteen years, an inherited weakness in the joint that Su comes to be able to spot across a room by the way any of them puts on a coat.

And there is a Saturday in 1906 when a barge takes a man's hand between the gunwale and a stanchion and does to four fingers what such things do to four fingers, and Su, who cannot mend that and says so within ten seconds of touching it, does the two things she can: she splints it in the exact shape that will give a surgeon the most to work with, and she walks the man to the London Hospital herself, three miles, at his pace, because he is grey and shaking and would otherwise sit down in the road.

The fan does the finer work. It has done the finer work in this family for longer than any of them can document — a folded fan being, when you strip the romance off it, a short rigid instrument of exactly the right stiffness to splint a finger, lever a joint, or take a bruise out of a muscle in a series of short pressed strokes, and having in addition the advantage of being the least alarming object a nervous person can be approached with. Su's grandfather used it so. Her father used it so. She uses it so, and mixes the liniment to her father's own arrangement, in the same three brown bottles on the same shelf, and has never once written the arrangement down, and will teach it, in due course, by making three separate people stand beside her while she mixes it, which is the only way it has ever been transmitted and the only way she trusts.

She does not take payment beyond the going rate.

This is tested once, memorably, in the summer of 1909, by a gentleman from somewhere west who has brought his son down to look at a ship and had the boy come off a bollard onto his elbow, and who is directed to the back door of a chandlery in Limehouse by a dock policeman and does not care for the experience at all until the elbow is back in, at which point he cares for it a great deal and produces a guinea.

Su asks him for one and sixpence.

He tells her not to be absurd. He tells her, in the pleasantest way imaginable, that he can perfectly well afford it, and that she has plainly earned it, and that she should take it.

One and sixpence, Su says, is what it costs.

They stand there a while. The gentleman is not a bad man and this is not, in the end, a story about a bad man; he is simply a person to whom it has never once occurred that a price might be a fact rather than an opening, and Su is a person whose father spent thirteen years putting four sentences into her in two languages, one of which was *never for payment*, and who has understood since she was about eleven that the rule is not really about money at all — that a thing done for a fee becomes, quietly, a thing available for a fee, and that the whole distance between her family and a great many other families on that river is the distance between those two sentences.

He pays one and sixpence. He is, Su thinks watching him go up the lane with the boy, faintly offended, and will tell the story at dinner, and will get it wrong.

The shop keeps its custom. Sau-Ling works the counter still, slower now, her own hands gone soft at the knuckle the way hands do after fifty years of lye and hot iron, but present every morning regardless, because a woman who has buried a country and two sisters and a husband and a son does not, Su has come to understand, stop working merely because the working has grown difficult. It is, if anything, the one thing that has never once grown difficult for her mother, in a life that has made nearly everything else so.

## II. Sau-Ling's Last Years

She has nine years as a grandmother, and is unrecognisable in the office.

Su, who was raised by this woman and can testify in detail as to the terms, watches Sau-Ling Zhang do things for those three children that were never once available to her own childhood — sit on a floor, invent a song, be climbed on, permit an outrage — and does not resent it for a single hour, having worked out somewhere around the birth of the second that her mother was not a different woman then and is not a different woman now, but is simply, for the first time in her life since a village north of Canton in the 1850s, standing on ground that is not actively moving.

A woman raising a child in a country that has not decided whether to let her stay is not permitted to be soft with it. A woman with a shop that has stood forty years and a daughter who runs it and a husband in the churchyard and three small people who will be English in a way she was never asked to be may be as soft as she likes.

Her English never improves. It has not improved since about 1874, and by 1900 it has fossilised entirely into a private dialect of her own — a working stock of perhaps nine hundred words, deployed with total confidence and complete disregard for tense, which every customer on the Causeway understands perfectly and which no stranger understands at all. The grandchildren speak it fluently as a second language and are surprised, at school, aged six or seven, to discover that nobody else does.

She keeps going to the mission church every Sunday of her life, and keeps, so far as anybody can establish, an entirely unresolved relationship with what she finds there. Su asks her once, in about 1905, straight out, whether she actually believes any of it.

Sau-Ling considers this for some time.

I believe there is a book, she says at last. I do not believe anybody here has read it.

The one subject she will not be drawn on, ever, is her sisters. Su has three facts about her aunts at the age of thirty-six and will have the same three facts at the age of ninety: that there were two of them, that they were older, and that they did not come. Everything else in that account went into the water somewhere between Hong Kong and Gravesend in 1868 and has never once been brought back up. Su asks, twice more across the last decade of her mother's life, and gets, both times, the same thing she got as a child — not a refusal, which could be argued with, but a small complete stillness, the conversational equivalent of a door that has no handle on this side.

Wei's death takes something out of her that does not come back. Not her competence, which is untouchable, and not her temper, which improves. What goes is a sort of forward lean. For thirty-one years Sau-Ling Zhang got up in the morning against something; after 1899 she gets up in the morning, and the difference is visible only to the one person in the house who has been watching her do it since 1875.

She dusts the white bowl herself, every day, for the whole nine years she outlives him. Nobody asks her to. Nobody has ever asked anybody to.

Her mother goes quietly, in the end, in the spring of 1908, in the room above the shop where she has slept every night for forty years, with Su beside the bed and the mission-church minister sent for and arrived in good time, unlike a certain other man once sent for on a worse night thirty years before, a fact neither Su nor her mother has ever once needed to say aloud between them for both to be thinking it.

Sau-Ling does not weep at her own death, any more than she has ever wept the way stones weep at anyone else's, though she tells Su, in one of her last lucid hours, the thing Su will keep the longest of everything her mother ever said to her.

I have kept my ledger my whole life, she says, her English still furred at the edges after fifty years the way it was furred the day she landed, and it has never once come right, and I have never once minded that it didn't. A book that always balances is a book somebody has been lying to. I would rather have kept an honest loss than a tidy lie, and I think, looking back over the whole of it, that I did.

She asks, near the very end, whether Su still has the bowl.

On the high shelf, Su tells her. Where it has always been.

Good, her mother says. Some debts are not for closing. Your father knew that before either of us did, and I have spent forty years married to a man who kept an entire shrine to the idea and called it only dusting.

She dies before the following dawn, and is buried beside Lee, and beside Wei, in the small mission churchyard that has taken every Zhang this book has lost and given none of them back, and Su stands at the grave with her own three children beside her and understands, in the particular way a person only understands such things once there is nobody left standing between them and the front of the queue, that she is now the oldest Zhang on this street, and that the ledger her mother kept, the open one, the one that never once came right, is hers to keep now, whether she ever asked to inherit it or not.

She is thirty-nine the year her mother dies and she becomes, in the space of one spring morning, the oldest person in her own house — which is a promotion nobody applies for and which arrives with the whole of the family's memory attached to it, unsorted, and no forwarding address for any of the questions she has not yet thought to ask.

She starts, that summer, doing something she has never done before, which is asking her own children to remember things.

Not the war stories and not the crossings; the small ones. What their grandmother said about the eel shop. Which of them she taught to count in Cantonese and which of them she gave up on. The song she sang at the mangle, which had four words in it that nobody now living can identify and which Su's eldest can still hum at eighty-one.

She makes them repeat it back. She makes them do it more than once. She does not explain why and they do not ask, and it is only when the eldest is herself a grandmother, sitting in that same back room, catching herself doing exactly the same thing to a fidgeting child, that she works out what her mother had been up to that summer.

The shop goes on. It goes on being the same shop through the whole of the Edwardian decade, while the reach around it changes in the small relentless ways a reach changes: the arches going up, the gas going in and then the electric, three shops in the same frontage across eleven years, and a steady thinning-out of the particular kind of business the Zhangs' whole trade was built on, because ships were changing and so were the men on them and so was what those men wore.

Su reads the thinning correctly by about 1906 and does the arithmetic and takes, without any drama at all, the decision that keeps that family solvent for the next forty years: she stops trying to be a laundry and starts being a laundry and a chandlery and a place where a person can get a thing mended, and she moves the balance a little further toward the mending every single year until, by the war, more of the shop's income comes off her needle and her hands than out of her copper.

Nobody records this. There is no moment. It is simply that a woman looked at a street for four or five years and then quietly rebuilt her family's living around what she had seen, and Su would not, asked directly, have called it a decision at all. She would have said the mending was coming in.

## III. Slack Tide

Erik comes ashore for good in 1911, his knees no longer equal to a deck's pitch in heavy weather, his hair gone the same iron-grey his father's went before him on the same coast outside Stockholm, and takes, without much ceremony, to a chair by the chandlery window that becomes, within a season, unmistakably his — not because anyone declares it so, but because a house learns a returning sailor's shape the way it learned Su's father's shape at the dawn yard, by simple accumulated habit, until the chair and the man have worn each other into an agreement neither ever needed to put into words.

He is easier to know, ashore for good, than he ever was returning between voyages, though Su understands within the first year that this is not because he has finally decided to ask her the questions he spent two decades of marriage refusing to ask. It is only that a man with nowhere left to sail to has, at last, nothing left to keep stowed below the line for the next departure, and the holds that were always closed to her — Trinidad, the manifest, whatever private cargo a lifetime at sea packs into a man and never quite unpacks — stay closed still, out of the same old seamanship, but closed now beside her rather than at a remove of eight months and half an ocean.

They row out on slack tides still, when the knees allow it, though the boat is hired now rather than rowed by his own arm the whole distance, and the river gives them the same clean hour it has always given them, owing nothing in either direction, indifferent as ever to the two small figures it has watched grow old across its own changeless current.

He asks her, once, late, in the quiet of an evening neither of their children is in the room to overhear, whether there was ever anything in the fog that November, before he knew her, that she has never once told a living soul.

Yes, Su says.

He does not ask what.

That is all, he says, and reaches for her hand instead, and it is, in its way, the nearest thing to a confession either of them ever makes about the one closed hold left standing in an otherwise entirely open marriage, and Su understands, sitting with her hand in his in the last of the evening light, that a good crewmate, even after thirty years of marriage, does not go down another person's hold uninvited, and that this, more than anything either of them ever said aloud, is the whole of what she has ever needed a marriage to be.

He gets fifteen years ashore, which is more than his father got and a great deal more than most of the men he sailed with, and he spends them in a chair by a window three streets from a river he has stopped going out on, and does not complain about any of it in Su's hearing even once.

The chair moves twice in those fifteen years, both times by about four feet, both times because the light changed — the warehouse opposite going up two storeys in 1919 and taking the morning out of that window, and the whole arrangement shifting along the wall to catch what was left. Neither move is discussed. Su comes down one morning and the chair is somewhere else and the amber and the flower have gone with it, and that is the whole of the negotiation.

He goes on being easy to have in a room and impossible to get anything out of. The children, grown now, bring him their difficulties, which he receives in the flat manifest voice and answers about one time in three and otherwise simply holds, and all three of them will say the same thing about their father in old age, which is that he never once told any of them what to do and they none of them ever did anything important without going and sitting in that room first.

The chest goes at the end. Not the heart, which everybody expected, and not the knees, which had been threatening it since 1911 — the chest, slowly, across the last two winters, in the ordinary way of a man who spent forty years on wet decks in cold latitudes and was never once dry through the whole of a February.

He dies on a Tuesday afternoon in the June of 1926, in the chair, with the window open.

Su is at the counter. There is nothing in it — no sound, no call, nothing to come running for. She finishes a ticket and goes through with a cup of tea and he is sitting exactly as he was sitting eleven minutes before, and the flower is doing its unreasonable June argument on the sill beside him, six red petals, four feet from his face, and she stands in the doorway with the tea in her hand for what she will afterward estimate at a full minute before she puts it down.

She does not shout for anybody. She goes over and puts two fingers where she has put two fingers on several hundred people across thirty years of bonesetting, at the angle of the jaw below the ear, because her hands want to be certain before the rest of her is required to be, and they are, and she takes them away.

Then she sits down on the floor beside her husband's chair, which is not a thing she has done in that room in her life, and stays there a while.

What she thinks about, sitting there, is not the marriage. It is a hired boat on a slack tide in September 1893, and a large unhurried man failing conspicuously to row it evenly, and a proposal delivered as a cargo manifest — *I have no house yet, only a berth and a wage and a father's watch that does not properly keep time past four in the afternoon* — and the fact, which strikes her for the first time in thirty-three years and strikes her so hard she says something out loud in an empty room, that he had told her the truth in that boat about every single item on the list, including the watch, which never did keep time past four and which is upstairs in a drawer at this moment and has not been wound since Sunday.

There is no closed hold in the end. She never tells him about the fog, and he never asks, and he dies without knowing, and Su will be asked once, decades later, by a granddaughter who has no idea what she is actually asking, whether she ever kept anything from her husband.

Only the one thing, Su says. And he knew there was one, and he left it alone, and there is not a woman on this river who was ever given a better present than that.

They bury him at the mission church, in the ground that has taken every Zhang this book has lost, which is not where a Swedish Lutheran off the timber ships had any particular expectation of ending up, and which is, Su thinks, standing at the graveside between her son and her eldest daughter, entirely correct: he was freight once too, in his own way, on the same water, out of the same century's arrangements, and the ground on this reach has never once in three hundred years asked a body where it started.

The chair stays by the window. Nobody sits in it. It is still there, with the amber and the flower on the sill above it, when the great-grandchildren are old enough to be told whose it was, and it is still there after that.

## IV. The Nights of the Airships

The war comes to the Causeway the way everything has always come to the Causeway, which is up the river first and down the street afterward.

It arrives, in the first instance, as a change in the shipping. Then as a change in the custom — whole categories of shirt simply ceasing to arrive, and other categories arriving in quantities the shop has no arrangement for. Then, in the September of 1914, it arrives as her second child, the boy whose wrist her left hand once reached before her eyes did, standing in the doorway of the chandlery at seventeen years and eleven months old in a state of barely governable excitement, having that afternoon told a recruiting sergeant in Poplar that he was nineteen and been believed, on the reasonable grounds that the sergeant wanted to believe him.

Su does not weep and does not argue. She asks him one question, which is whether he has told his father.

He has not.

Then you will tell him tonight, Su says, before you tell anybody else on this street, because your father has been at sea since he was fourteen and there is nothing you are about to say to him that he has not already thought about for longer than you have been alive.

He goes to France in the March. Erik, who has not been able to get up the shop steps without stopping since the winter, sits in his chair by the window with the crimson flower and the amber on the sill beside him and says almost nothing at all for three days, and then resumes saying things, and the subject of his son is not raised again in that house except in the specific and practical form of letters, which are read aloud at the counter when they come and answered the same evening, and which never once, in three and a half years, contain a single line either party would have been ashamed to have read out to a stranger.

The airships start in the summer of 1915.

It is difficult, afterward, to explain to anybody born later what was strange about them, because the strangeness was not the danger. The reach had never in its life been short of danger; it had cholera and it had the dock gate and it had a river that took four or five men a month without comment. The strangeness was the sky. Nothing had ever come out of the sky before. Not once, in the whole history of everybody standing in that street, in all the generations any of them could name, had anything at all ever arrived from above, and the discovery that it now could rearranged something in the reach that Su could feel but could not have written down — a sort of universal small readjustment of the shoulders, as though every person on the Causeway had been informed simultaneously that a wall they had always leant on was in fact a curtain.

They come at night, in clear weather, when the river is a silver road pointing straight at the docks and there is not one thing anybody can do about the river being where it is.

You hear them before anything else. Su will remember the sound for the rest of her life and will never find a comparison she is satisfied with: not thunder, which has weather in it, and not machinery, which has purpose in it — a low, even, disinterested note, somewhere above the cloud, going about its business, taking its time. Then the searchlights, swinging up out of the parks and off the ships, crossing and uncrossing, finding nothing, finding nothing, and then very occasionally finding it: a long pale shape up there in the beams, absurdly slow, absurdly beautiful, a hundred and fifty feet of nothing you could reach.

The worst of them, for that stretch of the river, is a night in the autumn of 1916.

Su is forty-seven years old. She is at the Prospect end of the reach at half past ten in the evening, walking home from a delivery she should not have been making at that hour and made anyway because the customer was ill and the linen was clean and there was nobody else to take it, and the note starts up somewhere behind and above her, and within ninety seconds the whole street is out of doors and moving.

They go for the railway arches, because the arches are what there is. Two hundred people, near enough, converging out of six streets on a brick opening perhaps eleven feet wide with a flight of steps inside it going down into the dark, in the middle of the night, with something overhead making a noise like a thing that does not care.

Su gets to the mouth of the arch at the same moment as the crowd does, and does not go down the steps, and does not, for the next twenty minutes, go anywhere at all.

She simply stops in the opening, off-centre, with her back to the brick on the hinge side where the flow is narrowest and the pressure is worst, and she puts her feet where her feet want to be, and she begins doing the only thing there is to do, which is to take the crush apart into people.

She has one hand out, low, at the level of a man's hip. Not pushing — she is not big enough to push and has never in her life pushed anything that could be arrived at another way — but resting, and moving, finding the one shoulder in each surge that is about to turn the whole mass sideways into the brick, and easing it, half an inch, a quarter turn, so that the surge goes down the steps instead of into the wall. Her voice is doing something separate from her hands. It is saying, over and over, in a register pitched to carry to the back and no further, the same four or five words in whatever order they are wanted: steps, mind the steps, one at a time, there is room, there is room. She has no idea whether there is room. There is room if they go one at a time, which is the same thing said honestly.

An old man goes down on the third step and is very nearly the end of it for everybody behind him, and Su is there — she does not know how; the space closed and then she was in it — with her forearm braced across the frame of the arch and her shoulder into the man behind him, holding a gap eighteen inches wide open for the four seconds it takes for two other people to get him up, and the four seconds cost her something in the shoulder that she will feel every cold morning for the rest of her life and does not notice at all at the time.

Twenty minutes. When the arch is full and the street is empty she goes down the steps last, and stands at the bottom in the dark among two hundred people breathing, and hears, through eleven feet of brick and a hundred years of London clay, the flat distant coughs of the thing going on somewhere north of them, and thinks about her husband in a chair three streets away who cannot get down any steps at all and who has flatly refused, since the first raid, to be moved anywhere on account of anybody's airship.

Nobody in that arch, then or afterward, thanks her for anything, because nobody in that arch is aware that anything has been done. That is the whole nature of the work. A crush that does not happen leaves no evidence of itself whatsoever; there is only a night on which two hundred frightened people got down some steps in the dark, which is what two hundred people do, and there is nothing in it for anybody to remember. Three of them will tell the story for years afterward as the night they went down the arches, and not one of the three will mention a small Chinese woman in her forties standing in the opening with one hand out, because not one of the three saw her, because there was nothing to see.

Su files it, that night and permanently, as: a bad night at the arches.

The house on the corner of the next street but one loses its whole frontage in that raid.

She goes in. Of course she goes in; there is a woman in the road saying a name over and over, and the number of people on that corner at one in the morning with any useful idea of what to do is precisely nobody, and Su has been going in through doors since 1888 when the alternative was standing outside them.

Inside there is no floor in the front room and there is most of a floor in the back one, and there is a dresser — an enormous Victorian thing, eight feet of oak and crockery — lying at an angle across the back-room doorway with one end on the floor that is still there and the other on the floor that is not, and under the low end of it, in the gap, there is a boy of about nine who cannot get out and is not, so far as Su can tell in the dark by the light of somebody's bicycle lamp, badly hurt.

She does not try to lift the dresser. She has never in her life tried to lift anything she could instead persuade. She gets her back against the door jamb and her feet against the base of the thing and puts the whole of her weight through her legs in a low even press, not fast, not a heave — a long push of about four inches with everything she has arranged behind it — and the dresser goes up and stays up, and she says, quite calmly, to the boy: now.

The boy comes out.

She lets it down slowly, which is harder than putting it up and which she also does not think about, and she carries the boy out to the road and gives him to whoever is shouting for him, and she has, by then, been in the building rather less than three minutes.

In the morning two men from the salvage come to shift that dresser and take four of them to do it, and there is nobody present at either event with any reason to compare the two, and no reason on earth why anybody should.

Su's own accounting of the whole night, given to her husband at four in the morning in the chair by the window, runs to eleven words: they got the arches open, and there was a boy in Berry's.

Erik asks whether she is hurt.

No, Su says.

Good, he says, and does not ask her anything else, because he never has.

The other thing that autumn brings is older than the airships and does not surprise her in the least.

It starts, as it started in 1888, in the newspapers, and it takes almost exactly the same shape: a great deal of print about foreigners in the East End, and about what is being smoked in Limehouse and by whom, and about the general question of whether a district full of people with the wrong names can be relied upon to want the right side to win. A shop two streets over has its window put in twice in a fortnight. A man Su has done washing for since he was an apprentice crosses the road to avoid speaking to her outside the mission church, and then, three weeks later, crosses back, and buys her a great deal more soap than he needs, and neither of them ever refers to either crossing.

Su's son is at that time nineteen years old and somewhere east of Albert, in the mud, in a uniform, with a Swedish surname and a Chinese grandmother and a rifle issued to him by the same country whose newspapers are asking, that month, in print, whether people like his mother ought to be interned.

Her mother, Su thinks — and Sau-Ling has been eight years dead by then and thought of daily — would have had one sentence for it, and would have said it once, at the copper, and would then have lifted the sheet.

Su does not have her mother's sentence. What she has instead is a yard, and the yard is where she takes it, every dawn of the whole war, in the grey light before the counter opens: the hard work first, at forty-seven, at forty-eight, at forty-nine, shorter than it was and slower than it was and not one morning missed; and then, at the end of it, the long breath, the slow unwinding part her father added to let a body settle, the part a four-year-old once said looked like swimming standing up. Twelve minutes of turning circles in the cold with nothing in them at all.

She would not have told you it helped. She would have said the counter opens at six.

Her son comes home in the February of 1919 with a chest that will never be entirely right again and both arms and both legs and the general air, common to that whole generation on that street, of a man who has agreed with himself not to discuss something.

Su does not ask him about it. Not once, in the thirty-one years she has left with him.

She has, after all, a fog of her own.

The twenties are hard on that reach in a way the war was not, and Su, who has now watched three separate decades try to shut her family's shop, watches this one come closest.

The trouble is the docks. It is always the docks. Men are taken on by the half-day at the gate, in a crowd, by a foreman pointing, and in a bad year the pointing goes on for four minutes and then stops with three hundred men still standing there — and every one of those three hundred has a household, and every household has washing, and washing is the first thing a household stops paying for and the last thing it starts again.

The shop's slate in 1922 is the longest it has been since 1888. Su carries it. She carries it the way her mother carried Catherine's, on the working assumption that people mean to pay and that a debt never quite settled is its own kind of relationship, and she is proved right about roughly two-thirds of it, which is a better return than any bank on the Highway was getting that year.

In the May of 1926 the whole thing stops for nine days.

Su has no politics, has never had any, and would tell you, asked, that a woman with a copper to draw has no time for a view. What she has instead is a shop on a street where every third household has a man out, and what she does for nine days in May is what her mother did in every crisis of her own forty years: opens at six, stops charging for anything under a penny, and says nothing whatever to anybody about the rights of it.

Her son is out for the nine days with the rest of his depot. He comes home on the fourth evening and sits in the chair opposite his father's and says one sentence about it, which is that it will not work.

Erik, who has four weeks left to live and does not know it, says: no.

But it wants doing, her son says.

Yes, says Erik.

That is the entire political discussion ever conducted in that room in forty years. Su, at the counter with the book, does not look up, and has remembered every word of it since.

## V. What the Papers Still Ask

The case is never solved. Su reads every fresh theory the papers print about it across the following decades, out of a discipline she has never fully explained to herself, whenever a new writer decides the old autumn is worth another column — a doctor this time, a foreign sailor the next, a madman committed quietly by a family with means, a policeman's private confession printed thirty years after the fact by a paper glad of the circulation. She lays every theory against the private ledger she has kept since a November morning in 1888 and finds, every single time, the same answer waiting for her at the bottom of the sum.

It fits, and it does not prove.

She never once writes to a paper to correct a theory, however far from what she alone in the world actually knows a given writer's guess has wandered. She has told herself, across forty years of practising the restraint, that speaking now would answer nothing that matters and would only cost her the one thing she still has left of that November: the fact that it belongs to nobody else's account but her own. The five dead women the papers still argue about, decade after decade, are, to Su, always and only two women first — a Swede who came on Mondays, a singer who never finished a song — and the arguing itself, however clever, however confident, has never once, in all the years she has watched it continue, managed to put either of their actual faces back in front of her the way her own memory still can, without any paper's help at all.

There is a bad year, in the middle of the 1920s, when it comes very close.

A book appears — one of the confident ones, a doctor's name on the spine and a great deal of certainty inside it — and the theory in it is not the doctor she knows, and is not close to the doctor she knows, and is argued so well and reviewed so respectfully that for about six weeks the whole thing is settled in the papers and the correspondence columns move on to other business.

Su reads it twice. She reads it a second time specifically to see whether it will hold, because a part of her that she is not proud of and has never entirely put down would like very much for somebody else's answer to be right, and would like to be permitted, at fifty-six, after thirty-eight years, to set the thing down and go and be an ordinary old woman in a chandlery.

It does not hold. She can see the place it does not hold — a date that is made to do work it cannot do, and a habit assumed rather than shown — and she can see it because she has spent thirty-eight years learning exactly how much weight a date will carry, and she puts the book down on the counter at eleven o'clock at night in an empty shop and says, out loud, to nobody: no.

That is as close as it ever gets to the surface. She never writes to the author. She never writes to anybody.

Her own children ask her, once each, growing up, whether she was ever afraid, in the year everyone still calls the terrible autumn. She tells each of them the same true and incomplete thing: that everyone in Wapping was afraid that year, and that fear, like grief, is a thing a person learns to carry quietly rather than a thing that ever properly leaves.

None of them ever learns the rest of it. She has decided, and decided again every year since, that this is the one account she will carry the whole distance herself, the way her father carried a white bowl he was never permitted to send home, on a shelf, dusted, entered nowhere a stranger could ever read the entry.

## VI. The Next Small Hands

She is sixty-one the winter her own eldest daughter's child stands in the yard for the first time, seven years old, uncomprehending, exact, in a horse stance held the same four-level depth her great-grandfather taught on a hillside above a harbour none of them will ever see.

Su presses the child's knees out over her feet, the way her father's hands once pressed her own, and says the thing he said, in the two languages he insisted she own both of before either one could be trusted alone.

You will stand in it every day of your life, she tells the child, and most days it will teach you nothing, and you will stand in it anyway, because the days it teaches you nothing are the rent you pay on the day it teaches you everything.

The child does not understand a word of it, and does the thing exactly, which is, Su thinks, watching the small determined shoulders hold the stance a moment longer than the child's own legs would naturally choose to hold it, the first lesson, and the hardest, and the only one that has ever mattered.

She has been teaching, by then, for thirty years, and has learned in that time the one thing about it her father never told her, which is that no two people arrive with the same difficulty.

Her eldest came to it like a door opening. She had the legs for the horse at seven and the patience for it at eight, and never once in her whole childhood asked what any of it was for, and Su understood by the time the girl was ten that she was watching somebody who would keep the form correctly for sixty years and never in her life need it, which is the outcome the four rules are actually designed to produce and which nobody ever describes as a success.

Her son came to it sideways. He was quick — quicker than either of his sisters, quicker than Su at the same age — and quickness is the single most expensive gift a student of this art can be handed, because it lets a child do everything nearly right for four years without once doing anything properly. Su spent the whole of his ninth year taking his speed off him. She made him do the form at a quarter pace until he cried about it, and then at a quarter pace again the following morning, and the morning after that, and she has never been entirely certain, in all the decades since, whether she was right to; and he went to France at seventeen and came back at twenty-one and stood in the yard the second morning he was home, without being asked, at a quarter pace, in the cold, for forty minutes, and did not explain, and Su did not ask, and that is the only answer to the question she is ever going to get.

The third took to the listening. Nobody taught her to; she was six years old and standing at the wall in a blanket the way they all did, and one morning she put her wrist against her grandfather's — this is the last winter of his life, and he is two months from the yard wall — and he let her, and afterward he said to Su, in Cantonese, with what was for him an enormous amount of feeling: this one hears.

And there were the others. There are always others. Parents on the Causeway have been sending children to that yard since before Su was born — sent, mostly, not for the art at all but for the hour of it, because a child who has stood in a cold yard at half past five is a child who has been got out of a bed and given something to be tired from, and a great many families on that street have used the Zhangs for that and nothing else and been welcome to.

Some of them stay eleven days. Some of them stay eleven years.

There was a boy, in about 1913, whom Su would not teach.

He was fourteen, and he came on his own account rather than his mother's, which she liked, and he was strong and willing and stood in the horse on the first morning for four minutes longer than she asked him to, which she liked less. On the third morning he told her, unprompted, in the tone of a person offering a credential, what he intended to do to a particular older boy on Pennyfields once he knew enough.

Su sent him home.

She sent him home kindly, and gave him back the sixpence his mother had insisted on, and told him the true reason, which is that a person who has already chosen the door he intends to walk through cannot be taught how to open doors. She told him he could come back at any time in his life if the intention ever fell off him. She meant it, and she said so, and she watched him go up the lane furious and humiliated and entirely certain she was a fraud, and she went back into the shop and opened the counter, and thought about it, on and off, for the next forty years.

He did come back. Not in 1913, and not in 1920. He came back in 1936, a man of thirty-seven with two children of his own and a bad leg from somewhere, and stood in the shop doorway and said he had been meaning to do this for a very long time, and Su, who was sixty-seven and had known him at once, said only: is it off you?

He said it was.

He stood in that yard three mornings a week for the next four years, and was never any good at it, and Su thought him one of the two or three most satisfactory students she ever had.

What she teaches them, in the end, is not very much. Four rules, one stance, one bridge, one soft slow form at the end to let the body settle, and the whole of it repeated in the cold before a counter opens, ten thousand times, until the day arrives — for perhaps one student in forty, perhaps one in a hundred, perhaps none, and there is no way of knowing in advance and no way of finding out afterward — when a person walks round a corner into something and their body has already answered before the rest of them has finished arriving.

She tells every one of them, at the beginning, the same four sentences her father told her, in the order he told them, in both of the languages he insisted she own before either one could be trusted alone.

Never first. Never for show. Never for payment. Never in anger.

She has never once, in the whole of her long watch over that yard, explained the fourth one properly. She has come to believe it cannot be explained; that it is the only one of the four that has to be arrived at rather than received, and that a person who has arrived at it will not need it explained and a person who has not will not be able to hear it.

She hopes, of all of them, that none of them ever arrives.

What none of them ever ask her, because it would not occur to anybody to ask it, is what the art is like from the inside after fifty years of it.

The honest answer is that it is not like anything. That is the part she could not have explained at twenty and cannot explain at sixty and has stopped trying to: that the whole enormous apparatus — thirteen years of a cold yard, four rules, ten thousand mornings, a grandfather's fan, a school in Canton that shut its doors before her father was born — resolves, in the end, into nothing you could point at. There is no sensation of carrying it. There is no moment in an ordinary day when a woman folding a shirt in a chandlery in Limehouse is aware of being anything other than a woman folding a shirt. The art does not sit on top of the life like a coat. It went into the life somewhere around her twentieth year and has not been separable from it since, and what is left, at sixty, is simply the way she happens to stand, and the way she happens to lift things, and the fact that she has never in her life been knocked over by a crowd, and the fact that she does not think about any of these things at all.

There is an evening in 1928 when the whole of it comes closest to surfacing, and does not.

Two men come into the shop over a bicycle. Not a shirt — a bicycle, propped against the window, and whose it is, and it is nine o'clock at night and the shop is shut and Su has opened the door because they were shouting in the lane and the lane is under her window. She stands in the doorway with the light behind her and asks them, pleasantly, whether they know what time it is, and lets the pause sit, and the pause does its work, and they go.

And afterward, putting the bolt across, Su thinks: that was like the two men and the shirt.

That is the whole of the thought. Two men and a shirt, thirty years ago, a Wednesday, three weeks after Erik sailed — she can still see the ticket in her hand. She thinks about it for perhaps four seconds, with mild affection, the way a person thinks about any small repeated feature of a long working life, and then she goes up to bed, and it never crosses her mind again.

Not once does she go the further step. Not once, in ninety-odd years, does it present itself to her that the reason two grown men leave a doorway when a woman her size stands in it is anything other than that men are, on the whole, more reasonable at nine at night than they sound from an upstairs window. The doorway, the shop floor in 1898, the mouth of an arch under a bombing in 1916, an old man on the third step, a dresser in a house on the corner, a child's wrist, her own father's weight coming down out of the dark — these do not, in her mind, form a set. They are not filed together. There is no drawer in that ledger with all of them in it.

There is only one person in the world who could ever have put them side by side and seen what they were, and she is the person, and she never does, and that is the exact shape of the thing.

The reach changes around her through the thirties. The chandlery outlasts two of the shops either side of it and is outlasted, in turn, by neither of the ones that replace them. The Causeway gets electric light, and buses, and a picture house, and a fresh generation of newspaper interest in what is supposedly being smoked in its back rooms, and the newspaper interest is answered, as it has been answered since 1888, by the street simply continuing to get up in the morning and go to work.

Su goes on standing in the yard.

The fan is old now, older than any hand that has held it, worn to a smoothness that predates every Zhang currently living by the better part of a century. Su keeps it in her own sleeve some dawns still, out of the same private habit she formed in the autumn of 1888 and never entirely broke, though she has had no earnest cause to use it in the years since a November fog and has come, slowly, to be grateful rather than restless about the long unbroken peace. Most mornings now it stays in the drawer, beside the white bowl's high shelf, two debts kept open on the same short stretch of wood — one that could never be repaid, and one that has simply never yet come due again.

She teaches the practice the whole rest of her life, to her own children and then to theirs, and to a scattering of other Causeway children besides, sent to her yard by parents who remember, or have heard, that the Zhangs keep something worth the keeping behind their chandlery. She never once takes payment for the teaching beyond what a fair exchange requires. She never once teaches it for show. She never once, in the whole span of her own long watch over that yard, raises her voice in it.

The watchman's daughter is the watchman now, and has been for the better part of forty years, and will go on being it a good while longer, standing in the grey light before the counter opens, a small unreasonable argument for the world's continuing, the same way a crimson flower argues for it every June from a chandlery window between two lamps, roots easy, minded against every frost that has come since, and every frost still to come.
