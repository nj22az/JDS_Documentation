# The Years Between (1889–1899)

> **1892**
>
> *"A good crewmate does not go down another's hold uninvited."*
>
> — of Erik Johansson, years into the marriage

***

## I. What the Winter Left

Su goes back to folding shirts the next morning, because the shop still needs the custom and her mother still needs the help, and because that is what the women of her family have always done with the days after the worst days: they finish the pressing.

That winter Flo presses a small clay pot on her across the bar, wordless, the way Flo gives everything that matters most — a rooted cutting of the house's own flower, the crimson dancer that has hung in the Prospect's window since before either of them was born, split and re-split a hundred years by whichever keeper happened to be minding the sill that season. It roots easy, Flo says. Mind it in frost. That is the whole of the conversation. A gift with no questions folded into it, which is, that particular winter, the only kind of gift Su has room left in her to take.

It goes into the chandlery window, between the two lamps, and does nothing at all through the cold months, and then, the following June, blooms all at once, extravagantly, a small unreasonable argument for the continuing existence of the world made out of six red petals and a green stem nobody in the family remembers watering with any particular care. Catherine, Su thinks, looking at it — Catherine who never once saw a bright thing she did not sing at, even a fragment, even a front with no back — would have liked it very much. That is not nothing. Some mornings, folding the day's first basket in the grey light before the shop opens, it is very nearly the whole of what there is to hold on to.

The song stays a front with no back to it, forever. Su never does learn how the girl on the quay makes out. Catherine carried the missing half of it down to Kent that September and neither the song nor the singer ever came home to deliver it, and for years afterward, folding in the quiet hour before the copper wants tending, Su will catch herself at the end of that first remembered verse with her mouth already shaping itself around a second line that never once learned how to exist, and nothing arriving to fill it, and the shirt in her hands going into the basket half a beat wrong while she waits for a note that is never coming. There are tunes, she comes to understand, that a person is only ever permitted to know the fronts of. It is a small grief, set down quietly beside the larger ones, and like the larger ones it does not get smaller with the years. It only learns, eventually, to keep quieter about itself.

She thinks of Elisabeth more than she expects to, in the years that follow — the tall Swede and the drowned family that never drowned, the story worn for public use because the true one, whatever it was, could never once be worn in the street. The papers called her Long Liz. Su never has, in all the years since, and never will, not even in the privacy of her own thinking, because a name is either a person's or it is the world's, and Su decided, on a wet Monday in a March four years gone now, whose it was going to stay. She understands Elisabeth completely now, in a way she did not, could not, that last ordinary summer. She has her own Princess Alice, after all: a fog, an alley, a clasp she took her own hand from before the answer could be known. A story that holds her upright and can never be told in full to a single living soul, carried the exact way the river carries everything given to it — without a mark left anywhere on the surface, without another soul on the whole reach ever quite knowing it is down there, patient, unclaimed, waiting on nothing.

## II. Erik Johansson

He comes up the shop steps four years after that autumn, on an ordinary Tuesday in the spring of 1892, with a sea-bag of salt-stiff shirts over one shoulder and a name with soft rounded corners in it that the shop has never once had cause to fold a ticket around before.

Erik Johansson. A big, unhurried man off the timber ships, smelling of pine pitch and cold canvas even at three streets' remove from the water, polite to Sau-Ling in three separate languages and, as Su's mother informs the room afterward with real satisfaction, wrong in every one of them. He sets the bag on the counter the careful way a man sets down a thing he has carried a long way and has grown fond of the weight of, and asks, in an English built mostly of good intentions, whether the shop does shirts for a man who has been at sea long enough to have forgotten what a shirt is properly meant to smell like.

Su reads him the way she reads everyone, before a single further word has passed between them: the particular set of the shoulders that belongs to a man carrying something lashed down below decks and riding quiet over it, the trim of a person who has made peace, long ago, with keeping a whole cargo hold of himself closed to casual weather. She has seen exactly that trim on exactly one other man in her whole life, and she grew up standing in his yard every morning before the counter opened.

She takes the bag. She does not, this first Tuesday, think anything further about him at all, beyond the ordinary noticing a laundress gives any new custom — the state of the collars, the honesty of the cuffs, whether the coin that changes hands is coin that has been carried close against a body the whole walk over, which his is.

He comes back the following month, and the one after that, on no very reliable schedule, the way a timber-ship man's custom always arrives — in bursts, between voyages, with weeks of nothing between one visit and the next and then three visits in a fortnight while his ship stands idle waiting on a cargo. Over the better part of a year, in the ordinary unhurried way that laundry-counter friendships grow whether either party has quite decided to grow one, Su learns him the way she has learned to learn everyone on this street: in fragments, across many Tuesdays, never asked for directly and never once withheld when the moment for a fragment happens to arrive.

The first fragment arrives in the June of that first year, over a shirt with a scorch mark across the shoulder that has plainly been pressed by somebody who has never in their life pressed anything.

Su holds it up to the window light without comment.

A woman in Riga, Erik says, watching her do it. She was very kind. She was not a laundress.

Su asks what she was, then.

She kept a house where a man could sleep dry for eleven kopeks, he says, and she offered, and it seemed to me an ill way to answer a kindness, to say no to it on the grounds that it would cost me a shirt.

It is the first time Su laughs at anything he has said. She notices, hearing the sound come out of her own mouth, that she has not laughed at this counter since some time in the summer before the terrible autumn, and that the noticing is not, this once, followed by the small internal collapse that has followed every other noticing of its kind for four years. She writes the ticket. She does not charge him for the scorch, which cannot be lifted, and which she will spend forty minutes lifting anyway, out of a professional objection to the state of the thing rather than any particular softness toward the man who carried it in.

The second fragment arrives in August, and Su very nearly misses it entirely, because the August Tuesday it arrives on is the worst Tuesday of that whole summer at the counter.

Two ships have paid off within a day of each other, which means the shop has four days of custom arriving inside a single afternoon, and the queue at the counter runs out of the door and down two steps into the lane by three o'clock. There are eleven men in it and a woman from Pennyfields with a child on her hip, and the men have been paid that morning and have been drinking since, and the queue has begun, in the way a queue does when it has been standing too long in August with money in its pockets, to develop opinions about the order it is standing in.

Su works it the way she works every queue.

She keeps her own body square to the counter and her weight low and even between her feet, so that she is never once obliged to make a large movement to reach anything, and so that the whole queue's attention, without any of them deciding to give it, settles on the one still point in a room where nothing else is still. She takes the tickets in the order they were handed to her and says the name on each one aloud, clearly, in a voice pitched to carry exactly to the door and not one inch past it, because a queue that can hear the order being kept does not need to be told the order is being kept. When the big Yarmouth man three back begins to lean out of line with his shoulders already turned toward the man in front of him, Su sets a wrapped parcel down on the counter a half-second before he has finished turning, in the precise spot his eyes were already travelling toward, and says his name, and he turns back to collect it and the turn he had begun does not finish. When the woman from Pennyfields shifts the child from one hip to the other and the child's weight goes wrong and the whole of her starts to go with it, Su's hand is under the child's back before the woman has registered the slip, and off it again just as fast, and the woman says thank you, love, and Su says nothing at all, because there is a ticket in her other hand and it has a name on it that wants saying.

The queue is down to four by half past three. Nobody has thrown anything, which on a two-ships day in August is the whole of what anybody at the counter would call a success, and it does not occur to Su, then or ever afterward, that anything has occurred that day beyond an ordinary afternoon of laundry moved briskly across a counter by somebody who has been moving laundry across it since she was six years old. She is tired to the shoulders. She has the copper still to see to. In her own accounting of the day, the entry reads: busy.

Erik is at the back of the queue for the whole of it. He waits his turn without once trying to catch her eye, and when he reaches the counter he sets down his bag and says only that he can come tomorrow if tomorrow suits her better, and Su says tomorrow is Wednesday and Wednesdays are worse, and he laughs, and pays, and goes, and says nothing whatever about the previous half hour, because there is, so far as he has seen, nothing whatever in the previous half hour to say anything about. A shop was busy. A woman ran it. He has been in a great many shops.

He tells her the gale story in the October.

It comes out over a torn cuff, unprompted, in the flat manifest voice, because Su has asked him — the first real question she has ever put to him — whether he has ever been properly frightened at sea, and he thinks about it for long enough that she has finished the cuff before he answers.

Once, he says. Off the Skaw, in the November before last. Deck cargo. Sawn pine, eleven feet high above the rail, and the lashings were done by a man in Sundsvall who was in a hurry.

He describes it without any weather in it at all, which Su comes to understand, over the following years, is simply how he describes everything that has genuinely cost him something: the ship going over further than a ship is meant to go and staying there a beat longer than a ship is meant to stay, the whole eleven feet of deck cargo above the rail beginning, all together, in a way he could hear before he could see it, to lean. Nine hundred tons of sawn timber deciding, at two in the morning in a black sea, whether or not to go over the side and take the rail and everything standing at it along with it.

What did you do, Su asks.

Nothing, he says. There was nothing to do. That is what was frightening. I stood at the after end of the house where a man stands when there is nothing to do, which is the place he can be found from afterward, and I waited to learn what the cargo had decided, and the cargo decided to stay, and in the morning we passed new lashings and I did not say anything to anybody about the two in the morning, because everybody aboard had had the same two in the morning and none of them wanted mine on top of it.

Su thinks, folding the cuff, of her father in a doorway with his empty hands turned out where a street could see them. She does not say so. But she thinks it, and it is the first time in her life she has caught herself laying another man alongside her father in the same measure and finding the measure fits, and she puts the thought away carefully, the way she puts away everything that seems likely to matter later.

The amber comes in the spring of the second year.

He puts it on the counter without preamble, a piece of Baltic amber the size of a thumb joint, unpolished, cloudy, the colour of the inside of a lamp, with a black seam of something running through the middle of it that might be a splinter and might be an insect and cannot, at this remove, be honestly called either.

For the window, he says. Beside the flower.

Su asks what it is.

Pine, he says. Only very old pine. The resin runs out of a tree and it sits in the ground for — he stops, and does the thing he does when the English will not carry the size of a number, which is to abandon the English altogether and put both hands flat on the counter about a foot apart, as though the distance between his palms were a length of time. A long while, he says. Before there was anybody to give it a name. And then the sea takes the coast apart and gives it back up the beach, and a boy walks along the beach in the morning and picks it up, and now it is in a laundry in Wapping.

Su holds it up to the window. The light comes through it the colour of weak tea, and the black seam in the middle of it does not resolve into anything, and will not, she suspects, however long she keeps it or however many mornings she carries it to the window to try.

It is the first thing anybody has ever given her that she has not immediately been able to think of a use for, and she puts it on the sill between the two lamps beside Flo's crimson flower, where it stays for the next sixty-one years, and where every child born into that house will eventually be lifted up to look at it, and every one of them will ask the same question about the black seam in the middle, and Su will give every one of them the same answer, which is that some things arrive already having kept their own counsel for longer than anybody has been alive to ask them anything, and that a person can either respect that or go on picking at it, and that the picking has never once, in the whole history of the world, made a piece of amber tell.

He comes in one Tuesday in the June following with his shoulders wrong.

Su reads it across the width of the shop, before he has reached the counter, before he has set the bag down — the trim gone out of him, the particular unlevelness of a man carrying something that has not yet been stowed anywhere at all. She does not ask. She takes the bag, and writes the ticket, and puts the kettle over instead of turning back to the copper, which is not something she does for custom, and which she does not, doing it, examine her reasons for.

A man off his ship has gone into the dock at Millwall between the quay and the hull, on a Friday night, in the dark, and was not missed until the Saturday muster. Erik tells it in the manifest voice, in the order the facts arrived rather than the order that would make them bearable, ending with the detail that has plainly been the one riding him for four days, which is that the man had a wife in Gothenburg and a letter half written to her in his chest and no way now of anybody knowing what the unwritten half of it was going to say.

Su says nothing at all, for a while, which is what her mother would do, and which is, she understands now with four years' distance on it, the only correct thing to do with a grief in the first week of it.

Then she says that there are tunes a person is only ever permitted to know the fronts of, and that she has one of her own, and that it does not get smaller with the years, only quieter, and that she is sorry.

He looks at her across the counter for what is, by the standards of a man who has spent two years asking her nothing, a very long time.

Then he says: thank you, and drinks the tea, and does not ask her which tune, and Su understands, watching him not ask, that she has just been shown the whole architecture of the man in a single held silence — and that she has, without any part of her deciding to, handed him a corner of the one thing she has never handed anybody, and that he has taken it and set it down gently and left it exactly where it lay.

He asks her almost nothing, that whole first year. She notices this long before she understands it, and files the noticing the way she files everything, without yet knowing the drawer it belongs in. A man who asks so little of a woman he has plainly noticed, she thinks at first, is either entirely uninterested or entirely too shy to manage the asking, and she cannot for a long while decide which account to enter him under.

## III. The Manifest

She learns the truth of it — that the asking-nothing is neither indifference nor shyness, but something closer to a discipline she already half recognises — on a wet evening the following spring, over a torn seam she is mending in his good coat while he sits across the counter in his shirtsleeves and waits, because a man cannot very well walk the docks half undressed and a coat this good is worth the waiting for.

Your father, he says, out of nothing, watching her needle. He has the yard behind the shop. I have seen him there, mornings, through the gap in the fence, before the light is properly up.

Su says that he does, and offers nothing further, the way she has learned, this last year, that offering nothing further is very often the surest way to receive something in return.

My own father kept a boat, Erik says. Not so different a thing, I think. A boat and a yard both want a man there before the light, doing the same motions over and over until the motions have stopped needing him to think about them at all.

He tells it slowly, in the flat unhurried voice of a man reciting a ship's manifest, because the cargo happens, this evening, to be his own family, and a manifest, Su comes to understand over the following months as he adds to it piece by careful piece, is the only shape he has ever properly learned for carrying anything that matters. His father was Swedish, out of a small fishing village on the coast outside Stockholm, a quiet coast, he says, where a boy either goes to the boats or goes away, and his father went to the boats. His mother was born on a sugar estate in Trinidad, to a family that had been shipped out from Calcutta a generation before under indenture papers signed by men who could not read what they were signing, in the years just after a certain trading company had finally, formally, ceased to exist — though the ships that had carried its old cargoes, he says, did not trouble to change their business merely because the letters painted on their sterns had changed, and went on carrying whatever paid, wherever it paid to carry it, indenture included.

The boat had a name, he tells her on another evening, over the second mending — *Hoppet*, which he translates for her as The Hope, and then immediately spoils by explaining that roughly a third of the boats on that whole coast were called the same thing, so that a man calling to another man across the water in fog had to add his father's name to it to be understood at all, which rather defeated, he says, the purpose of naming a boat anything.

It was twenty-six feet and it was his grandfather's before it was his father's, and it went out at four in the morning in every weather that would not actually sink it, and it came back at noon or it came back at three, and what it carried back determined what the house ate that week with an exactness Su recognises immediately, because a laundry copper works the same way and always has.

His father, he says, could tell where he was in a fog by the sound the water made against the rocks off two particular headlands, and could not have explained to another living soul how, and was asked once by a customs man to explain it and could not, and was very nearly taken up as a liar on the strength of not being able to.

Su asks whether he could do it himself.

No, Erik says. I left before it went into me properly. That is a thing that takes a man his whole childhood and then his whole young manhood on top of it, standing beside the person who already has it, doing the same nothing over and over in the dark until the nothing turns into knowing. I had eleven years of it. It wants thirty.

He says this without any grievance in it whatever, as a manifest entry rather than a regret, and Su, mending, thinks of a yard six paces by four and a fan and thirteen years of standing beside the person who already had it, and does not say that either.

His mother's half of the manifest comes later still, in pieces, across the whole following winter, and is more granular than the rest of it because his mother, unlike his father, told it to him on purpose, repeatedly, in the specific way of a woman making sure a thing is carried forward whether or not the carrier ever wants it.

Her people came out of a district north of Calcutta in a year Erik gives as either 1857 or 1858 and has never been able to settle between, because the two people who could have settled it disagreed with one another until they died. There were papers. The papers said five years. The papers said, at the bottom, in a clause the recruiter read aloud and nobody in the family could check, that a return passage would be found at the end of the five years for any who wanted one. Nobody in the family could read what was above the recruiter's finger or below it. They put their marks on it in a shed at the depot at Garden Reach, and were put aboard, and were one hundred and eleven days at sea, and came ashore at Port of Spain in the spring, and were sent up-country to an estate whose name Erik pronounces carefully, the way a man pronounces a word he learned by sound from somebody who learned it the same way.

The five years became eight. The return passage was found, in the end, for exactly nobody Erik's mother could ever name, and by the time anybody in the family was in a position to ask about it seriously there was a second generation on the estate who had never seen the district north of Calcutta and had no particular use for a passage back to a place they could not have found on any water.

His mother was born in the barrack rooms in the year the estate changed hands for the third time. She met his father in Port of Spain when a Swedish barque came in for sugar and stayed eleven days over a repair, and she went back to a fishing village outside Stockholm with him, which Erik describes, with the only bitterness Su has heard in him in two years, as the single most reckless act of navigation in the whole history of his family and the only one that ever paid.

She was, he says, the only brown-skinned woman on forty miles of that coast for the whole of her life, and she was buried there, and the stone has her name on it in letters the parish cut wrong and never corrected.

Su asks him what the letters should have said.

He tells her. She has him say it twice, and then a third time, slowly, and then she says it back to him, and gets it wrong, and he corrects her, and she gets it right on the fourth attempt, and he looks at her across the counter with an expression she has no name for at all.

Nobody has ever done that, he says.

Su says that in her family a name kept in only one language is a name only half kept, and that this is not her opinion but her father's, and that she has never in her life found a reason to doubt it.

Su does not say, when he finally reaches the end of it, months on, over a second mending and a third, what she has been thinking since somewhere in the middle of the first telling: that the same machine, dead thirty years and more before either of them was born, moved both their houses across the whole width of the world in its holds, and never once, in all the moving, entered either family in its own books as anything but freight. She keeps the thought the way she keeps most of her largest thoughts, folded small, kept close, offered to nobody until the offering has some clear use.

Erik asks her, that same evening, whether she has ever wanted to see the sea properly — not the river, which she has seen her whole life from its edge, but the open water, the kind a timber ship crosses for weeks at a stretch with nothing at all on any horizon.

Su says she has not thought about it.

Think about it now, he says, not urgently, in the unhurried way he says everything, and goes back to waiting for his coat.

## IV. A Marriage

He asks her properly on a slack-tide Sunday in the September of 1893, five years almost to the week since the autumn that took Elisabeth and Catherine, out on the river itself, in a hired boat he has clearly practised rowing evenly for the occasion and does not, in the event, manage to row evenly at all.

He does not kneel, because the boat will not permit it, and he does not make a speech, because a manifest is the only shape he owns for saying a large thing, and he uses it now exactly as he has used it for every other large thing he has told her across two years of Tuesdays.

I have no house yet, he says, only a berth and a wage and a father's watch that does not properly keep time past four in the afternoon. I am at sea eight months of most years, and the eight months will not shorten themselves for a wife's asking, because the sea does not shorten itself for anyone's asking. I would want you to keep the shop, if your mother is willing, because a shop is a thing that can be kept by a woman whose husband is not always in the room to keep it with her, and I have watched you keep it three years now and think you would keep it better than I would keep anything.

Su asks him, because she has learned by now that a plain question receives from him the plainest possible answer, whether he is asking her to marry him or asking her to manage an absence.

Both, he says, without hesitating over it at all. I do not know how to ask for one without the other. I have not the words in either of my languages for a marriage that pretends the sea is not also in it.

She says yes on the river, which she will think, in the years after, was exactly the right water to have said it on — the same water that took her family across the world as freight and gave nothing back for the taking, the same water that closed over a drowned man with a question unfinished on his face and never once answered it, the same water Elisabeth's whole borrowed grief was built to explain and never quite did. She says yes to a Swede with a manifest instead of a proposal, on the one stretch of the Thames that has never, in her whole recorded life, given her anything at all without first taking something larger.

They marry the following spring, quietly, at the mission church where Sau-Ling was dipped twenty-five years before and where Su herself has gone, without much devotion but without much argument either, every Sunday of her life. Wei gives her away with a stillness Su recognises from every cold yard morning of her childhood — not calm, she understands now, watching his face across the small gathered room, but the practice doing, one final time in this particular use, what it has always been built to do: containing a thing too large to let show, holding it inside a shape precise enough that the shape itself becomes the only visible part of the feeling.

The morning of it is grey and then, by ten, unreasonably fine, in the way an English spring will sometimes decide to be fine on a day somebody has already made their peace with it being otherwise.

There is no white dress, because a white dress is four days' takings for a garment a woman wears once, and because Su has spent her whole working life at a copper watching what happens to white, and has views. She is married in grey wool with a collar her mother turned twice, and she carries, instead of flowers bought for the purpose, a single cut stem from the plant on the chandlery sill — the crimson dancer, four years rooted now and grown leggy and enormous and entirely indifferent to every frost since, split off from a pot Flo pressed on her across a bar in the worst winter of her life without asking a single question about why she looked the way she looked. It is the only ornamental thing about the whole day. Su cuts it herself, at six in the morning, before the practice, with the shears they use for tape.

Flo comes. Flo shuts the Prospect for two hours in the middle of a Thursday, which nobody on that reach can remember her doing for a wedding, a funeral, or the Queen, and stands at the back in a hat that has plainly been somewhere before, and cries steadily and without any embarrassment whatsoever from the second verse of the first hymn to the end of the service, and afterward says only that it was the hat's fault, the hat has always done that, and takes the whole gathering back to the Prospect and stands them a round on the house and will not be argued with about it.

The dockworker comes, in a coat, which is how Su learns that he owns one. He shakes Erik's hand for slightly too long and tells him something in an undertone that Erik will not afterward repeat, and stands at the back of the bar for an hour and a half looking at the river through the window with the expression of a man attending, quietly, alongside the wedding in front of him, some other business of his own that nobody in the room has been invited into.

The service is in English, because the mission church is in English and always has been, and Su makes her responses in it, clearly, in the voice she uses at the counter. But when the minister comes to the giving away, and asks the question in the form the book gives him, Wei answers in Cantonese — four words, quietly, not loudly enough for the back rows, in a language nobody in that building but his wife and his daughter has any part of at all — and then, after a pause exactly long enough for the room to begin wondering whether something has gone wrong, gives the same four words again in English, correctly, in the order the book wants them.

Nobody afterward remarks on it. Su hears it, standing in front of him with her hand out, and understands that her father has just done in a church, in front of a minister and a Swede and forty years of the Causeway, the only thing he has ever consistently insisted upon in his whole life: that a thing worth saying is a thing worth owning in both of the languages that made it, and that whichever one a room happens to prefer is the room's business and not the thing's.

Sau-Ling does not cry. Sau-Ling stands through the whole of it with her hands folded, in the same dark stuff she has worn to every wedding and every burial on that street for twenty-five years, looking at the front of the church with an expression Su cannot read from where she is standing and has to wait until the following morning to have explained to her, when her mother, at the copper, apropos of nothing at all, says: I was thinking of my sisters.

Su asks which one.

Both, her mother says, and lifts the sheet, and that is the whole of the conversation, and it is, Su thinks afterward, across the rest of her life, the most anybody in her family has ever said about that particular loss in a single sitting.

There is one further thing, which nobody sees but Su.

She comes back to the shop alone at some point in the afternoon, for a reason she cannot afterward recall — a key, a forgotten parcel, some errand invented by a woman who has been in a room full of well-wishers for four hours and needs eleven minutes of nobody — and finds her father there ahead of her, in the empty shop, in his good coat, standing at the high shelf with the white bowl in his hands.

He is not doing anything with it. He is not dusting it. He is simply holding it, the way a man holds a thing he has held ten thousand times and is holding today for a different reason, and when he registers that she is in the doorway he puts it back on the shelf, exactly square, the way it has stood since 1868.

She would have liked this, he says.

He does not say who. He has never once, in Su's whole life, said who. He goes back to the Prospect, and Su follows him three minutes later, and the subject is never raised again by either of them for as long as he lives.

Erik asks her almost nothing, the whole of the marriage that follows, which she understands within its first year is not incuriosity and never was. It is seamanship. A man long enough on the water learns, he tells her once, that every soul who comes off it is carrying cargo stowed below the line, unlogged, private, and that a good crewmate does not go down another person's hold uninvited, whatever the temptation, because a hold a man was not asked into is a hold he has no business claiming to have seen the bottom of. He never once asks what happened in the fog the November before they met, though Su is certain, by their second year together, that he has heard some version of it from someone on the reach, the way every story on this river eventually reaches every ear that stays long enough to hear it. He never asks. She never tells him. It becomes, without either of them naming it as such, the one closed hold in a marriage built otherwise almost entirely of open ones.

The eight months are eight months. Su learns them the way she learns everything, by doing them: a marriage measured out in departures from the Shadwell basin and returns to it, in letters that arrive four at a time or not for eleven weeks, in a wedding ring worn on a string round her neck through the working day because a ring and a wringer are a bad combination and every laundress on that reach could tell you the same, and in a shop that goes on wanting opening at six whether there is a man in the house or an ocean where the man ought to be.

She keeps it. That was the bargain and she made it on the water with her eyes open, and she finds, somewhat to her own surprise, that she is better at the keeping than she expected to be — that a woman raised in the back of a chandlery by two people who between them buried a country, two sisters, a brother and a son and opened the shop the next morning regardless has been, in some sense, in training for a manageable absence her entire life.

She is carrying her first when the summer of 1895 turns hot, and she works the copper through the whole of it, because the copper does not care and neither, particularly, does she.

What her body does that summer, it does without consulting her about any of it. The heavy baskets come up off the floor with the knees rather than the back, as they always have, but lower now and wider, the feet finding a broader base without her once looking down to place them. The wringer takes the sheets with a roll that begins somewhere below her waist and arrives at her wrists as a kind of afterthought, so that the great sodden weight of a double sheet comes out of the water and over the rollers with almost nothing in it, and the work, which by rights ought to be killing her at seven months gone in an August, is merely tiring. When she has to reach across the counter for a ticket in the high rack she does not stretch for it; she steps, one short step, and comes back square, and the stepping is so entirely a part of the reaching that she could not have told anybody afterward that she had moved her feet at all.

Her mother, watching her lift a full basket in the last fortnight before the child comes, tells her to be careful.

Su says she is being careful.

Sau-Ling says nothing further, because her daughter has lifted the basket correctly, the way she has lifted every basket in that shop since she was nine, and there is nothing in it for a mother to object to beyond the general and unanswerable objection of the situation itself.

The first is a daughter, in the September. The second is a boy, two years behind her. There is a third by the turn of the century, and by then the shop and the yard and the two rooms above have arranged themselves around the arithmetic of small children the way a river arranges itself around a moored hull, and Erik has been at sea for something over half of the marriage so far and has come home each time to a household that has plainly managed, and has never once, in all the years, made the mistake of remarking that it has plainly managed.

Su goes on standing in the yard every dawn through all of it. She stands it nine months gone, shortened, holding the horse for a count her father drops without comment from three hundred to eighty and back up again afterward at his own pace. She stands it eleven days after each birth, badly, furiously, and her father watches her do it and says nothing about the eleven days and corrects her elbow. She stands it with an infant asleep in a basket four feet away and one ear on the basket the whole time, and the correction still lands, and the count still finishes, and the long breath still unwinds itself at the end of it in the grey light before the counter opens, and by the time the eldest is old enough to ask what her mother is doing out there every morning the answer has become so entirely obvious to everybody in that house that nobody thinks to give it.

The counter goes on being the counter.

There is an afternoon in the spring of 1898 — a Wednesday, unremarkable, three weeks after Erik's ship has sailed for the Baltic — when two men come in over a shirt.

The shirt is one shirt. It has been lost, or has been sent to the wrong ticket, or has never existed at all, and the two men have already settled the question of whose it is between themselves in the lane outside, badly, in raised voices, and have brought the settling in through the door with them. One of them is a big man with a fresh grievance and the other is a smaller man with an older one, and by the time they reach the counter they have stopped being about a shirt at all in the way that men in a shop have generally stopped being about the thing they came in about.

Su comes out from the copper drying her hands.

She does not raise her voice. She does not, at any point in the next ninety seconds, touch either of them. She simply comes round the end of the counter — which is not where a laundress stands, and which puts her, without anybody in the room noticing the geometry of it, at the open corner of the triangle the two men have made, so that each of them, to go on facing the other, has to keep her at the edge of his eye — and she stops there, square, weight low, hands loose and visible and empty at the level of her own waist, and asks the smaller man for his ticket.

The smaller man gives her his ticket, because a woman has asked him a plain question about a piece of paper he happens to be holding.

She reads it aloud. She asks the bigger man for his. He gives it. She reads that aloud too, and then she says the one true thing available, which is that neither ticket is for the shirt in question and that the shirt in question came in on a third ticket belonging to somebody who is not in the room, and that she will find it by Friday, and that they can both come back Friday.

The bigger man says something short about Friday.

Friday, Su agrees, pleasantly, and does not move, and does not fill the pause, and lets the silence sit in the middle of the shop until it has gone from being a silence one man might push against into a silence a man would look ridiculous pushing against, which takes, in the event, about four seconds.

They go. The smaller one goes first, which is how she knows it is finished. She writes a note for the Friday, puts the kettle over, and thinks nothing at all about the previous minute and a half for the rest of her life, except once, thirty years later, in a wholly different context, and even then not as anything to do with a yard.

The children learn the counter the way she learned it, which is by being underfoot at it. And there is a morning — the boy is two, or nearly — when he gets up onto a stool nobody left there and reaches, with the total commitment of a small child who has spotted something forbidden and interesting, for the flat iron standing hot on the stand.

Su is turned away, at the rack, with a ticket in her hand and her back to the room.

Her left hand arrives at his wrist before her eyes do. She has no memory afterward of turning; the turn and the hand and the small yelp of outrage from the boy all occupy the same instant in her recollection, undivided, and the iron does not so much as rock on its stand, and the ticket is still in her right hand and has not been dropped.

Her mother, at the mangle, says: he wants watching, that one.

He does, Su agrees, and sets him down, and finds the ticket she was looking for.

That is the whole of the entry. Nobody in the room, then or afterward, ever files it as anything else. Her son will grow up, and marry, and be sixty years old before he dies, and will never once in his life know that the first thing his mother's body ever did about him was arrive.

The homecomings are their own country, and nobody warns a woman about them.

The first two are straightforward, because the first two happen before there are children, and a man coming back to a wife after eight months is a thing with a shape to it that both parties can find in the dark. It is the third that teaches her what the arrangement is actually going to cost.

He comes up the shop steps in the February of 1897 after nine months and eleven days, having been held six weeks over his time in the Gulf of Bothnia by ice, and the daughter, who was fourteen months old and portable when he left and is now two and a quarter and has opinions, looks at the enormous cold-smelling man in the doorway and does not know him.

She does not cry. That would be easier. She simply looks at him for a moment with the flat unembarrassed assessment of a small child, and then turns round and puts both arms up to her grandmother, and Sau-Ling picks her up, and the child puts her face into her grandmother's neck and is done with the subject.

Erik stands in the doorway with his bag still on his shoulder.

Su watches her husband's face do nothing at all, in a way she has seen exactly once before in his life, on the evening he told her about a man who went into the dock at Millwall with half a letter in his chest, and she understands, standing behind her own counter with a ticket in her hand, that this is the bill, and that it arrives every time, and that he has been paying it privately in every port he has ever sailed out of and has never once mentioned it to her because mentioning it would have been asking her to carry it, and a good crewmate does not put his own cargo into somebody else's hold uninvited.

That evening, after the shop is shut, she does the only thing she can think of, which is to hand him the child's boots.

Not the child. The boots.

He looks at them.

She wants them off at bedtime, Su says, and she will not have anybody do it but whoever did it last night, and last night it was me, and tonight I am doing the copper.

It takes him four days. He does the boots, and then the boots and the washing of a face, and then, on the third evening, he is discovered sitting on the floor at the bottom of the stairs being shown, at some length and with total seriousness, a wooden spoon; and on the fourth morning the child comes down before anybody and goes straight past her mother and her grandmother both and stands in front of the big man asleep in the chair by the window and hits him firmly on the knee to wake him, which in that house, at that age, is as near to a formal declaration as anybody gets.

He is at sea again inside the month.

It happens again. Not so badly, and not with all three of them, and never again quite the way it happened that February — but it happens, in some measure, every time, and Su comes to understand that this is simply the freight charge on the arrangement she agreed to on a slack tide in a hired boat, and that her husband pays it in a currency she does not have to spend and cannot help him with, and that the four days of the boots are the closest thing to help that has ever been available.

She never once says any of this to him. He never once says it to her. They are, in this as in everything, two people who were each raised by somebody who believed that a thing properly carried does not need to be described.

On his slack-tide Sundays home from the sea he rows her out on the same stretch of river that took her family across the world as freight, that drowned her question and has never once been asked to answer it, that gave her Elisabeth's grief to understand and Catherine's ending to grieve without ever knowing — and the water, which has taken everything this house has ever fed it, going all the way back to a white bowl and a boy who said *Run* on a Hong Kong pier, gives the two of them back, in exchange, one clean hour with nothing owing in it either direction. It is not much of a bargain, set against everything the river has taken. Su comes to think, over the years, that it is exactly the bargain the river has always been prepared to offer, and that the trick of a whole life is learning to take the hour and stop totting up the rest of the account.

The children are three separate countries and Su has never once been able to govern any of them by the same set of laws.

The eldest is her grandmother's, from about the age of four, comprehensively and without any of the three adults involved having the smallest say in it. She follows Sau-Ling round that shop from the moment she can walk, learns the counter before she learns her letters, and acquires — this is the thing that undoes Su every time she thinks about it — her grandmother's exact private English, the nine-hundred-word dialect with the tenses knocked out of it, which she speaks fluently at six and is astonished, at school, to discover nobody else does. She spends most of her seventh year translating between two versions of the same language for customers who cannot understand one and have no idea there are two.

The boy is his father's and is at sea in his head from the age of five. He has the Baltic amber off the sill in his hand so often that Su eventually stops putting it back and simply lets him keep it in a pocket for two years, on the understanding that it lives on the sill and is only visiting. He wants the manifest recited. Not read to — recited: the ports, the cargoes, the eleven days in Port of Spain, his grandmother in the barrack rooms, the misspelt stone on the coast outside Stockholm. He gets it perhaps forty times between five and twelve and can do the whole thing himself at nine, in his father's flat voice, which Erik hears once, from the next room, and does not comment on to anybody.

The third is nobody's. She is watchful from birth in a way that unsettles her own mother, and she does not talk until she is nearly three and then talks in whole sentences, and she takes to the listening — the crossed wrists, the twelve minutes of nothing — faster than any of them, at six, without being taught, by putting her arm against her grandfather's at the wall in a blanket. Su has never worked out where she came from. She has decided, in old age, that this is simply what a third child is: the one the family has stopped having a theory about by the time it arrives, and who therefore gets to be itself.

All three go to the mission school and all three are the only Chinese in it, and all three come home with the same set of experiences in a different order, and Su handles it the same way every time, which is badly, and knows it.

What she says is: you will have to be better than them.

What her mother says, the one time she is present for it, is: no, she will not, she will have to be *better than that*, which is not the same thing at all — and Su, who is thirty-one and has three children and a shop and a father in the yard, is put back to about the age of nine by a single sentence at her own counter, and thinks about it for the next forty years, and has never entirely decided which of the two of them was right.

They all stand in the yard. That is not negotiable and is never presented as negotiable and none of the three of them ever thinks to ask why, because by seven they have watched it every morning of their lives and being finally allowed to is a promotion.

What they do not get, and what Su notices only when the eldest is about nine, is the thing she herself got, which is thirteen years of standing beside a person for whom the whole apparatus was still, in some part of him, load-bearing.

Wei learned it in a family that had lost a courtyard to a war. Su learned it from him in a house two streets from the Strangers' Home in the decade of the terrible autumn. Her children are learning it in a chandlery in Limehouse from a mother who runs a solvent business, in a country their grandmother's English cannot manage and their own is native in, and for the three of them it is — Su can see this happening and cannot prevent it and eventually decides she does not want to — an inheritance rather than a necessity.

She thinks about this a great deal in the middle of her life and arrives at a position she never states to anybody, which is that a family gets exactly one generation in which the art is a tool and every generation afterward in which it is a discipline, and that the whole point of the first generation's work is to produce the second kind, and that it is nevertheless a loss, and that both of those things are true at once and neither cancels the other.

Her father would have had a sentence for it. She is fairly sure the sentence would have been: good.

The letters are their own institution and outlast both of them.

Erik writes about four times a voyage, from wherever a ship's mail can be got off, and the letters are exactly what a manifest would be if a manifest could be fond: the port, the cargo, the weather in one clause, the state of two named shipmates, and one thing seen. Always one thing seen. A whale off the Skagerrak. A church in Riga with a green roof. A man in Danzig who could juggle four eggs and had, so far as Erik could establish, no other means of support whatsoever.

Su reads them aloud at the counter to whoever is in the shop, because a letter in that house has never once been treated as private property, and her mother listens to every word and pronounces judgement on the weather.

She writes back the same evening, always, in English, in a hand that got as far as the mission school and stopped. She tells him the shop, the children, the street, and one thing seen, because he asked for that in the second letter of the first voyage and she has never once missed it since. A barge on fire at Blackwall and everybody perfectly calm about it. A cat that has learned to open the chandlery door. A man on the Highway selling a machine that did nothing at all, very convincingly, for a fortnight.

Neither of them writes anything in a letter that could not be read aloud in a shop, because both of them know it will be.

There are eleven of his letters left by the time Su is old. The rest went where paper goes in a house with a copper in it. She could recite two of them entire at eighty and did, once, to a granddaughter who had asked an idle question and got rather more than she was expecting.

Her mother works the counter through all of it and does not, in the whole of the nineties, take a single day off her feet.

They fall into a division neither of them ever proposes. Sau-Ling has the counter and the book and the customers; Su has the copper, the loft, the round, the mending, and — from about 1896 — the ordering, because her mother's arithmetic is faultless and her mother's English is not, and there came a point at which arguing with a soap merchant in writing became a thing that needed doing in a language Sau-Ling has never consented to write.

They quarrel perhaps four times in a decade, always about the slate, always in Cantonese, always at the copper where the customers cannot hear, and always to a conclusion that is a draw.

Su thinks, later, that the nineties were the best decade of her life and that she did not notice one single day of it while it was happening. There was a husband who came and went, and three children, and a father in the yard, and a mother at the counter, and a shop that paid, and nobody died in it, and that — she has come to understand, from the far side of a great many years in which somebody did — is the entire specification for a good decade, and there is no possible way of knowing you are inside one at the time.

## V. Wei's Hands

Her father's hands go on failing by the same slow degrees Su first noticed in the autumn of the killings, and go on failing for the better part of a decade after, the way a rope frays — not all at once, never dramatically, only ever one more strand giving way each season until the day arrives, unannounced, when the whole of it can no longer be trusted with a real weight.

He stands in the yard every dawn regardless, because a form is not a thing a man stops doing merely because his knees have begun to argue with the cold, and Su stands beside him every dawn in turn, matching his pace the way she has matched it since she first understood, at nineteen, exactly how much the matching was beginning to mean. The horse stance holds him less long each winter. The fan, when he raises it now to correct his daughter's elbow, or the small wandering elbow of a granddaughter who is out in the yard at that hour chiefly because there is nowhere else in the house to put her — for there are grandchildren from the September of 1895 onward, in the yard on their own feet long before there is any question of teaching them anything, present the way Su herself was present for years before the learning ever started — travels a half-beat slower than it used to, though it still, without fail, finds the elbow it means to find.

He never once complains of it. Complaint, he tells Su once, on an evening she has pressed him further than her usual restraint permits, is only a ledger kept for an audience, and he has never in his life kept a ledger for anyone but himself and the one God his wife believes runs an honester set of books than his own.

There is a morning in the winter of 1896 when his knee goes.

It goes at the worst point of the form, in the low transition where the whole weight comes across onto one leg for a beat and a half before the other foot is permitted to arrive, a place in the sequence Su has watched him pass through perhaps nine thousand times without a single one of them being of the smallest interest to either of them. This time the leg does not take it. There is no warning in his face, because there is never anything in his face; there is only the knee, folding inward the wrong way, and sixty-eight years of a man beginning to arrive at a stone yard from a height.

Su is on him before he has finished starting to fall.

She does not decide to be. She is four feet away and facing the other direction, in the middle of her own form, and what happens is that the sound of her father's breath changing pitch arrives at her and her body is simply already moving, dropping its own weight under his, taking him across her shoulder and hip rather than her arms, so that the whole of the falling goes down through her legs into the ground and none of it goes into either of their backs. She sets him on his feet. It takes less than a second and a half. Neither of them has said anything.

Her father stands there breathing for a moment, testing the knee against the flagstone in small careful presses, the way a man tests ice.

Well caught, he says at last.

I was close by, Su says.

And that is the whole of the exchange, and they finish the form, and the yard has thirty-five minutes left before the counter opens, and Su carries the morning around with her all day filed in the ordinary drawer where she keeps everything about her father's knees, which is a drawer that has been getting fuller for six years and has never once, in all that time, been labelled anything but *he is getting old*. That her own body performed, in a second and a half, without instruction, in the dark, facing the wrong way, a thing that a man could train at deliberately for a decade and still fumble on the day — this does not present itself to her as a fact about herself at all. She was close by. Anybody close by would have.

Nobody who was close by would have. There was nobody close by. That is the entire point, and there is not one soul in the world, that morning or any morning after it, in a position to say so.

The bonesetting is what finally does it.

It is a boy from the ropewalk, in the March of 1897, brought in by his mother with a finger dislocated at the middle joint — a small injury, a nothing, the kind of thing Wei Zhang has put right on a doorstep in under four seconds several hundred times across thirty years on that street.

He takes the hand. He finds the joint. Su, at the counter, watching the way she has watched him do this since she was a child, sees the thing happen that she has been waiting three years to see and hoping, in a part of herself she does not much like, never to: his thumb goes to the place and the place is correct and the pressure does not come. It wavers. It is a quarter of a stone too light and it arrives a half-beat late, and the boy, who would have been crying with relief by now, is crying with something else, and Wei stops.

He does not try again.

He says, in English, to the mother, that his daughter will do it, and he steps back, and Su comes round the counter drying her hands and takes the boy's hand and does it in one, and the boy stops crying, and the mother thanks the wrong Zhang, and Wei lets her.

He does not say anything about it that day, or the next, or for the six days after.

His hands, by the last years of the decade, have stopped being reliable for the finer bonesetting work — the delicate turn of a wrist eased back into its socket, the exact pressure that splints without bruising — and he passes that part of the practice to Su formally, in the yard, on a grey morning in 1897, with no more ceremony than he has ever brought to anything that actually mattered to him.

Your hands now, he says, holding out the fan.

Su takes it. It is lighter than she remembers it being, the first morning he ever raised it over her own seven-year-old elbow, though she understands, taking the weight of it into her own palm, that the fan has not changed at all in the years between. Only the hand offering it has.

He teaches for two more years, though he cannot set.

Teaching, it turns out, wants almost nothing from a man's hands. It wants his eye, which has not gone anywhere, and his patience, which was never in his hands to begin with, and a fan, which he has given away and which his daughter brings out to the yard every dawn regardless and lays on the low wall between them where either of them can reach it.

The eldest granddaughter is four in that last autumn, and is in the yard because she has decided, on her own account and against every effort to keep her in bed, that the yard at that hour is where the interesting people are. Nobody is teaching her anything. She is not old enough to hold a stance and will not be for three years, and Wei will not start a child early for any reason, having views on the subject that he has never once explained to anybody.

So she stands at the wall in a blanket, and watches, and imitates badly, and nobody corrects her, and this — Su understands only much later, watching her own great-grandchildren do the identical thing forty years on — is itself the method, and always was, and is the reason not one child in this family has ever had to be persuaded to stand in that yard at seven. By seven they have been watching it for four years. By seven it is simply the thing the people they love do in the morning, and the only strange part is being finally allowed to.

Once, near the end, the child asks her grandfather why he is doing it slowly now, meaning the long breath, the soft unwinding part at the end that she likes better than the hard part because it looks, to a four-year-old, like a person swimming standing up.

Wei tells her that fast is easy and slow is honest, and that a man who can only do a thing fast does not know whether he can do it at all.

The child says she is going to do it slowly then.

Good, her grandfather says.

Su, three feet away, hears her father say *good* to a four-year-old for imitating a form badly in a blanket, and is aware of a small unreasonable pressure behind her own eyes, and turns back to her stance and finishes her count, because the counter opens at six and the copper wants drawing and there is, as there has always been on this exact patch of ground, a very great deal still to get through before the light is properly up.

## VI. The Last Morning

Wei Zhang dies in the yard, in the last hour before dawn, in the winter of 1899, doing the one thing he has done every morning of his adult life without once missing a day for illness, weather, grief, or the ordinary reluctance that keeps most men in their beds a little longer on the coldest mornings of the year.

Su finds him. She has come out early, as she often does now that the shop is half hers and half her mother's and the mornings belong to whichever of the three of them the work happens to need first, and she finds her father settled against the cold stones of the yard wall in the horse stance's own low crouch, as though he had simply decided, partway through the form, to rest there a moment longer than the form required, and the resting had quietly become something else.

There is no violence in it. That is the single mercy Su will hold onto, in the years of turning the morning over that follow: that her father, who spent his whole life containing violence inside a shape precise enough to be called a discipline, was given, at the very end of it, a death with none in it at all — only the cold, and the stillness he had practised into his own bones across sixty years, arriving at last to claim the one thing it had always, in the end, been rehearsing him for.

She does not scream. She kneels instead, in the exact spot she has knelt every dawn of her adult life, and takes up his hand — the hand that raised the fan over her own elbow, that pressed her knees out over her feet the first morning she stood in the horse and understood nothing, that set a wrist as she now sets wrists, that dusted a white bowl every day of forty years for a debt he was never permitted to repay — and she recites the rules to it, quietly, in both languages, the way he has made her recite them her whole life, not because reciting them will bring anything back, but because it is the only conversation she has left with him, and she will not let the last one go unfinished for want of the words.

Never first. Never for show. Never for payment. Never in anger.

Her mother comes out to the yard some minutes later, drawn by whatever a marriage of thirty years teaches a person to feel through a closed door, and finds her daughter still kneeling, still holding the hand, and does not ask what has happened, because the whole of the yard is already telling her.

Sau-Ling Zhang does not weep the way stones might weep, this particular morning, though she has told Su more than once across the years that this is the only kind of weeping she believes in. She stands a long moment in the grey light, looking at the two of them — her husband settled against the wall he built with his own hands out of another country's stone, her daughter kneeling beside him with the fan already, without either of them quite deciding it, resting in her own lap rather than his — and then she says the thing Su will carry the longest of everything said that whole terrible week, quietly, to the yard and the cold and the God she has never once managed to make her husband believe in.

He kept every rule he ever taught, she says. Even this one. He did not go looking for it. It only came, in its own time, the way debts do, and he did not flinch from the collecting.

The wash-copper stands unlit that morning, for the first time in Su's whole remembered life. It is lit again the following day, because the shop still needs the custom and the living still need clean linen, and because that is what the women of this family have always done with the days after the worst days.

They finish the pressing.
