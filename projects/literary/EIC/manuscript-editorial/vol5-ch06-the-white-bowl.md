# The White Bowl (1888, and Hong Kong, 1868)

> **1868, Hong Kong**
>
> *"Bring it back yourself. It's my mother's."*
>
> — a tea-stall woman, to Wei Zhang, the night before he sailed

***

Her father tells the story of his crossing perhaps twice a year, and always the same two ways: once in the summer, lightly, over the mending, as a joke against himself; and once in the winter, low, by the yard fire, as something nearer a prayer, though he keeps no God to say it to. Su has heard it enough times to know where he will pause and which details he will get deliberately wrong so that her mother, sewing across the room and pretending not to listen, can correct him from memory without either of them ever admitting that is what they are doing.

It is the only story in which Wei Zhang appears as a fool from start to finish, and he tells it with something close to relish, because rule three has a story inside it, and this is the story, and Su learned the rule from the telling years before she understood she was being taught anything at all.

It goes like this.

The year his own father died, the funeral took what savings there were, and the landlord took the yard within the month, and the colony — which in twenty years of the family teaching its boys had never once looked at them directly — became overnight a place Wei was only waiting to leave. He was seventeen. The last weeks were bad in the small grinding way that does not make stories on its own: the salt fish tougher as the money got thinner, a jar of rice spirit in the evenings that burned going down and still burned less than the days did, going over accounts that would not come right no matter which way he turned them, because there was simply less in the columns than there had been the week before, and less again the week after that.

He bought a steerage passage to London, because rope and tar and lamp-oil are wanted wherever there are ships, and London was where the ships were built to serve.

The evening before he sailed, the woman who kept the tea stall two doors from what had been the family yard — who had fed him through the worst of the bad weeks without once making him ask, the way some people simply decide a thing needs doing and do it before the person it's being done for can find the shame to refuse — filled a white bowl with rice and pickle for his last night ashore, and told him to bring the bowl back in the morning, before he went out to the anchorage.

Bring it back yourself, she said. It's my mother's.

Not send it back. Bring it. Her father says this part slowly, every telling, the way a man repeats a line he has turned over so many times it has worn a groove he can no longer walk around. Nineteen years old and he did not understand, that night, that she had just handed him an errand designed to make him come home safely, dressed up as a request about crockery — that a woman with nothing to give a boy sailing into the edge of the world had given him the one thing she could: a reason, however small, that the world owed him a return trip.

In the morning the harbourmaster's office got him instead.

A clerk found something wrong with his pass — nothing that would not have righted itself in five minutes with the right man's signature, except that the right man was at his breakfast, and the clerk, having found one irregularity, found the appetite to look for more, and stamped everything twice, even the blotter, with the particular thoroughness of a man to whom no tide in the history of the world has ever once mattered because no tide has ever once been his to catch. By the time Wei came out into the sun with his papers finally in order, the water had turned, and the last lighter out to the anchorage was already a speck with oars, pulling for a steamer that would not wait for one late Chinese boy with a canvas bag.

He tried explaining. The steamer, the passage already paid, everything he owned already stowed aboard her in that one bag. The harbourmaster answered him in English, aimed just over his head at some more important middle distance, and turned away before his own sentence to Wei was finished. Her father stood on the wharf, nineteen years old, and began, carefully, the way you test a floorboard before trusting your weight to it, to understand that he was ruined.

Then the boy found him.

Thirteen, perhaps fourteen — bare feet, a ledger clamped under one arm, some serang's runner or a comprador's boy, nobody's exactly, and whose master he ran for, Wei never once learned. The boy looked him over once, boots to bundle, the unhurried appraising look of a stevedore pricing a crate before he'll agree to shift it.

You are Zhang, the boy said. Not a question.

Then, before Wei could answer even that: Run.

They ran the whole crowded mile of the waterfront — through porters bent double under their loads, through fish barrows and sedan chairs and a customs officer's shout that rose behind them and then, mercifully, did not follow, under mooring lines strung low enough to take a careless man's head off, over a rail some clerk had put up for reasons neither boy nor man had time to respect. And her father stops the story here, every single telling, for the part he considers the entire point of it: that somewhere in the middle of that run, ruined, soaked through with his own sweat, with everything he owned in the world rowing away from him across open water, he was happy. It felt, he says, extraordinarily good to run. He has never apologised for that sentence, and Su, at nineteen, understands it now in a way she could not have at nine: that a body given one clean thing to do, after weeks of accounts that would not balance, will take the doing of it as a mercy even when the doing is only flight.

At the end of the last pier — the rotten one, the one nobody sound of mind would trust a rope to — a sampan sat waiting where no sampan had any business being, and the boy handed him down into it and settled the price with the oarsman without once consulting Wei, as though the whole transaction had already been entered somewhere Wei couldn't see and only wanted the ink drying on it. Wei got his coins out anyway, some reflex of gratitude looking for a place to land. The boy looked at the coins. Looked at Wei. Tapped the ledger under his arm once, as if to say the morning was already written down in a column Wei had no way of reading — and was gone back into the crowd before the boat had cleared the pilings.

Never first, her father says, when he reaches this part, though the rule as he teaches Su does not yet exist in the story's own time — the boy had it already, whole, without four words to hang it on. He did not wait to be asked. He did not do it for show; there was no one there to show it to. He did not do it for payment; he refused the payment, twice, the second time with his back already turned. And he was not angry — there was nothing in the whole transaction to be angry at, only a man in trouble and a boy who had decided, for reasons that will never now be known, that this particular morning's trouble was answerable.

The first meal aboard was rice out of a ship's copper with something sharp spooned over it — vinegar and oil and a heat that found the back of the nose and stayed there — eaten shoulder to shoulder with strangers in the swaying dark of the steerage deck, and her father maintains to this day that it is the finest thing he has ever eaten in his life, a life which has since, he adds, glancing sideways at his wife across the room, included twenty years of her cooking. Sau-Ling throws her thread at him without looking up. It is part of the telling. It has been part of the telling for as long as Su can remember, the same beat landing in the same place, a small worn joint in a story that has otherwise been handled so often it has gone as smooth as the fan.

Partway through that first meal a cabin boy came along the deck with a can of water, caught his foot on a lashing nobody had coiled properly, and doused Wei's shoulder — and then dropped straight to his knees on the boards with both arms up over his head, because he was perhaps ten years old and had been at sea long enough already to know exactly what a grown man's hand does next to a boy who has spilled something on him.

Her father set his own hand down slowly on the child's shoulder instead, and left it there, still, doing nothing at all, until the shaking stopped and the boy looked up to see what was coming. An old man on the next crate laughed first — at the boy's face, at Wei's wet shirt, at the plain foolishness of the whole world arranging itself this way twice in one day. Then the boy laughed, in the surprised, unbelieving way of a child who has just discovered a blow is not, after all, coming. Then Wei laughed too, and the three of them sat under the waterline laughing quietly together while the only life any of them had known went out behind them like a lamp being carried out of a room.

On the second day at sea he found the white bowl, still wrapped, packed at the very bottom of his own bundle — packed the night before, so that nothing could happen to it before he had the chance to give it back.

It stands in the shop to this day, on the high shelf above the counter, wrapped still in its own cloth: never used, never sold, dusted by nobody's hand but his. Once, when Su was eight or nine, she asked him why he did not simply send it home — there were ships for Hong Kong every week from the very dock at the end of their own street, it would have cost him almost nothing, and the woman, if she still lived, would have her bowl. Her father gave the question the same weight he gives a column of figures that will not close.

Because she asked me to bring it, he said. Not to send it.

That was the whole of his answer, and it was years before Su understood it was a whole answer at all — that the bowl and the boy on the pier are the same lesson wearing two different coats: the debt a person cannot repay, and the repayment a person is not, in the end, permitted to make. You cannot send a promise back by another hand. You can only carry it yourself, all the way, or you have not kept it — you have only discharged it, the way the Company discharges a debt, and her father has spent his whole life teaching her the difference between those two words as though a man's soul depended on knowing it, which, in the only ledger he keeps that matters, it does.

He never went back to Hong Kong. The tea-stall woman is thirty years dead by now, or as good as, and the bowl has outlived every possibility of ever reaching the hand it was meant for. He keeps it anyway. He dusts it anyway. He has built, without once calling it this, an entire shrine out of an unkept promise, and goes on keeping the promise's shape even after every chance of keeping its substance has gone under the waterline with everything else that year took from him.

Su grew up in a house with a debt on a high shelf that could never be paid and a mother who believed, on principle, that the debts worth keeping were exactly the ones that never came right. Between the two of them they built something in their daughter that has no single name in either of her languages: an interior ledger that must balance, and does not, and is kept open on purpose, forever, the way you keep a lamp burning for a ship that is not, this time, going to come in — not because the lamp will bring it home, but because a house that stops burning the lamp has agreed to something Su's family has never once, in two generations and two oceans, been willing to agree to.

She thinks of the boy on the pier more than she says. Thirteen, fourteen, a ledger under one arm, gone back into a crowd before the debt could even be offered, let alone paid. Whoever he grew into, wherever the years took him, he is entered — she is quite sure of this, has been sure of it since she was old enough to understand what her father was actually telling her, twice a year, in the two different registers of joke and prayer — in some column her father keeps that has nothing to do with money at all, and everything to do with the one rule that came before the other three, the one the boy already knew before anybody had troubled to write it down: that a debt run up in a good cause does not need to be collected to be real.
