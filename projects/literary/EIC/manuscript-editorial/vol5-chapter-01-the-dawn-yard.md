# The Dawn Yard (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"A weapon that walks about all day must have better manners than a knife that stays in a drawer."*
>
> — Wei Zhang, the yard behind the chandlery, Limehouse

***

## I. The Horse and the Bridge

The yard behind the chandlery is six paces by four, and Su Zhang has known every stone of it in the dark since she was seven years old.

It is a summer dawn, and the dark is only just going grey over the wall, and the air already carries the whole inventory of the river: tar and rope-oil from the shop at her back, mud and low water from the Causeway beyond the wall, coal smoke starting up from a thousand grates as Limehouse wakes to boil its first kettles. The wash-copper is not yet lit. That is the one certain mark of the hour — before the copper, the yard is the yard; after it, the yard is the laundry, and the day has the family and not the family the day.

She stands in the horse.

*Sei ping ma*, her grandfather called it, in a city she has never seen; the four-level stance, the one you sink into until the thighs burn and then keep sinking, until the burn is a fact you live beside rather than a thing that is happening to you. Her father set her in it the first morning, both his hands pressing her knees out over her feet, and told her she would stand in it every day of her life and that most days it would teach her nothing, and that she was to stand in it anyway, because the days it taught her nothing were the rent she paid on the day it would teach her everything. She was seven. She did not understand a word of it and did the thing exactly, which he told her, much later, was itself the first lesson and the hardest, and the only one he had ever worried she might not have in her.

She has it in her. She stands in the horse while the light comes up, weight down through the centre of each foot, spine stacked, breath going low into the belly the way he taught her before she could have said what a belly was for, and she does not shake. The shaking stopped years ago. What is left, on a good morning, is a stillness with a great deal of readiness folded up inside it, like a spring lying flat.

Her father comes out of the shop with two cups of tea and the fan.

Wei Zhang is a small man who has spent forty years being underestimated by larger ones, which he considers a professional advantage and has never once corrected. He sets one cup on the step for her, for after, and keeps the other, and stands sipping it and watching her hold the stance the way another father might watch a daughter at her letters — with attention, and without praise, because praise, in the yard, is a thing you spend once and cannot get back, and he is saving his.

The fan he keeps in his hand. It is a plain folded thing of dark wood, worn to the colour and the smoothness of an old knuckle, older than her father and older than the grandfather who carried it out of Canton in a bundle with a white bowl. It is not a weapon and it is not a toy. It is the yard's one instrument of correction and, when the yard has done a man harm, its one instrument of repair, because on their street in Canton the man who taught the art was also the man who set the bones — *dit da*, her father says, the hitting and the mending kept in the same two hands, because a person who has learned to break a wrist has learned the one thing needful to put it back, and ought to be made to do both, so that he never forgets what the first thing costs.

Bridge, her father says.

Su brings her arms up out of the stance into *kiu sau*, the bridge-hands, forearms forward and rooted, and her father steps in and lays his own forearm across hers, and they begin.

This is the part the sailors' stories never get, because the sailors only ever see the end of it, in a lane, in the half-second when it stops looking like anything at all and starts looking like a man sitting down against a wall who did not decide to sit. What the stories never see is this: two people standing almost still in a grey yard, arms crossed and touching, rolling slowly, each reading the other through the skin of the forearm. *Chi sau*, in her grandfather's tongue; the sticking hands. Her father translates it, when he translates it at all, as *listening*, and makes her give it back to him in English, because a thing you can only say in one language is a thing you only half know, and he did not carry this across the world so that his daughter would half know it.

She listens.

Through two crossed wrists in the dark she can feel where his weight is before he has finished deciding to put it there. She can feel the small forward lean that means a push is coming and the smaller settling-back that is the push's twin and its lie, the feint that lives inside every true thing. Wing Chun keeps to the centre and gives nothing away from it; Hung Ga plants like a tree and dares the weather; her family, out at the poor thin end of both lineages, kept what a laundry family could use of each, and what it could use turned out to be everything that costs nothing but years. The stances came from the one. The listening came from the other. The rope and the sheets, though it took her the longest time to understand this, came from the shop, and did as much as either.

Her father tests her with a push she should not be able to feel the front of, and she feels it, and is not there for it, folding with it and stepping into the place his balance has just left, and the point of her elbow arrives, gently, exactly under his floating rib, where a hand that meant it could stop a larger man's argument before he had finished forming it.

Good, her father says.

He does not say it often. He says it now, and Su files it the way she files everything, in the interior tally that has no name in either of her languages, the running book she has kept since before she can remember, in a script only she can read. Her mother's God keeps books; her father's art keeps rules; and somewhere in the narrow yard between the two of them they made a third thing in their daughter, an account that must balance whatever the balancing costs, and this morning it has taken in one word of her father's, worth more than its weight, and entered it, and gone on.

The fan raps her trailing elbow a half-inch higher without a word being spent.

She lifts it. He nods. They roll again.

While they roll he recites, because the art comes with its rules the way the tide comes with the river, and a form done without the rules is only exercise, which is to say only vanity with sweat on it. He says them in Cantonese and waits, and Su gives them back in English into the grey air, and this is as close as the two of them come, most mornings, to conversation.

Never first.

Never for show.

Never for payment.

Never in anger.

Four sentences. Eleven words in English and rather fewer in Cantonese, and Su has had every one of them since she was seven, and it has taken her most of the intervening twelve years to work out that they are not four rules but one rule said four ways for people who will only understand one of the four.

*Never first* is the plain one and the one everybody thinks they understand. It is not about who throws the first blow, which is a question for magistrates. It is about arrangements. A person who has arranged to be in a particular lane at a particular hour, so that a particular thing may present itself and be dealt with, has already broken it, whatever happens afterward and whoever moves first.

*Never for show* is the one Su has broken most often, in the smallest possible ways, and knows it. Not in a fight — she has been in three in her life and none of them had an audience. In the yard: a form done slightly better because somebody is watching from a window. Her father catches it every single time and does not name it and simply makes her do the thing again, badly, slowly, until whatever went into it that did not belong has come back out.

*Never for payment* is the one that sounds like modesty and is not. It is a rule about what a thing becomes. A skill exercised for a fee is a skill available for a fee, and a skill available for a fee eventually belongs to whoever is paying, and Wei Zhang has spent his whole life on a river where a great many people's hands belong to whoever is paying and has been very clear with his daughter about how those hands generally end up.

*Never in anger* is the fourth and Wei will not explain it.

Su has asked. She asked at nine and got the rule repeated; at thirteen and got the rule repeated; at seventeen, after the man in the shop, she asked a fourth time, in Cantonese, which is the room in which he is likeliest to give her something, and her father looked at her for a while and then said that a rule a person can be talked into is a rule that can be talked back out again, and that she would either arrive at that one herself or she would not, and that he could not do it for her and would not try.

She has not arrived at it yet, this summer. She thinks she has. That is not the same thing, and both of them know it, and only one of them is worried about it.


The practice stops things, he says — *the practice*, in the same flat voice he uses for the shop's accounts, and it took her years to understand that the flat voice was not modesty and was not concealment; it was accuracy. *Gong fu* is only skill and the time you spend to get it. A man who has spent thirty years learning to cook rice has *gong fu*. What she has is *gong fu*, and it happens to be the kind that can put a man on the ground, and her father will not dress that up in a bigger word, because a bigger word would be the first step onto the road that ends with rule two. The practice stops things, he says. It does not answer them. You are not learning to win a fight. You are learning to end one that has already started, in the fewest moves that will end it, and then — and this is the part he leans on, every time, the part he considers the whole of it — to live correctly, afterward, with having ended it.

Whoever holds the art, he says, carries a weapon everywhere, all her life, in her own body. His father's words, worn smooth by two crossings of the world. And a weapon that walks about all day must have better manners than a knife that stays in a drawer.

Then the hard part of the morning is over, and the other part begins, the part her father never named for her the way he named the rules, because he considered it beneath naming — not a technique, only a settling, the thing a body does after exertion the way a kettle does after the boil, if the kettle is asked to do it properly and not simply left to cool on its own.

Slow now, he says, though he has said it so many mornings of her life that the words themselves have worn nearly to nothing, a shape rather than a sound.

They move together through what her grandfather, so far as her father ever passed the phrase along, called only *the long breath* — no stances named in it, no strikes hidden in it waiting to be sprung, nothing a stranger glancing over the yard wall could have mistaken for a fight rehearsed in miniature. It is circles instead. The weight rolling from one foot to the other so slowly that the roll and the breath going out become, after enough mornings of doing it, the same single motion wearing two names. The arms rising as though lifted by water rather than by muscle, turning, sinking again, palms curving around an armful of nothing that nonetheless has, if you have done this long enough, a definite shape and a definite weight, the way a memory has a shape and a weight though there is nothing in the hand to show for it. Her father's own arms, when he moves through it beside her, no longer look like a fighting man's arms at all. They look like a man standing in a river up to his chest, letting the current do the work of holding him upright, moving only enough to stay exactly where the current has already decided to put him.

It is not a fight ended in three moves and lived with afterward, the way the hard practice is. It is nothing ended at all. It asks for no correction, most mornings, because there is no error in it sharp enough for the fan to find — only a kind of patience the fan cannot teach and can only, at best, get out of the way of. Her father does this last, always, after the horse and the bridge and the rules, because a hard thing done and set down, he told her once, wants a soft thing done afterward to carry it properly, the way a hot iron wants setting down on its heel rather than flat, so the heat has somewhere honest to go instead of scorching whatever it is laid against next.

Su has never once, doing it, thought of it as part of the same art at all. It does not feel, in the doing, anything like ending a fight. It feels like nothing so much as standing very still while being extremely slowly poured from one shape into another, and she could not, if asked, have said with any confidence that the slow turning circles her arms make in the grey light before the counter opens have anything whatever to do with the exact same arms folding a fan over a stranger's wrist in a dark lane four months from now. She does not connect the two. She has never once been asked to. Her father did not teach her the long breath so that she would see the join. He taught it to her so that the join would simply be there, load-bearing, invisible, the way the keel of a boat is there under the water the whole voyage and is never once seen by the passengers who trust their whole lives to it without knowing its name.

The light is up over the wall now. Somewhere down the Causeway a door goes, and a bucket rings on a coping, and the first cart of the morning starts its iron complaint over the cobbles toward the dock. The yard has perhaps a quarter of an hour left of being only the yard.

Her father steps back and lowers his arms, and the roll is over, and he picks up his tea again as though it has been waiting, which it has.

Su comes up out of the stance at last. The blood goes back into her legs like a tide turning, that particular ache she has had every morning of her life and would not know how to live without. She takes up her own cup from the step. The tea has gone to the temperature the whole ritual is secretly built around, hot enough to comfort, cool enough to drink straight down, and she drinks it straight down, standing, the way her father does, the way his father did on a hillside above a harbour she will never see.

On the high shelf inside the shop window, wrapped in its cloth, stands the white bowl.

She can just see the shape of it from the yard through the open shop door: a plain rice bowl, not fine, not Chinese-fine the way the missionaries' cups are English-fine, only a working bowl of the sort a tea-stall keeps a dozen of. It has stood on that shelf her whole life. It is never used and never sold and never washed by anyone but him, and when she was small she asked why he did not simply send it back to Canton — there are ships for Hong Kong every week from the dock at the end of their own street — and her father gave the question the same grave consideration he gives a column of figures, and said, *Because she asked me to bring it. Not to send it.* And that was the whole answer, and Su was most of the way grown before she understood that the bowl was a debt he was not permitted to pay, kept on a shelf where he would have to see it every day and go on not paying it, forever, on purpose; and that this, too, was a rule, older than the four, the one they all come out of: that some accounts are to be carried at a loss, kept open, never closed, because closing them would be the theft and not the keeping.

Her mother would call that mercy. Her mother, who was dipped in the river's cold water at the mission church and keeps a God who runs His books at a loss on principle, would say the bowl is the nearest thing her husband owns to a prayer, and her husband, who keeps no God and every rule, would say nothing at all, and go on dusting it.

Su is nineteen years old this summer. She lives in the yard between the two of them and is made of pieces of each, and she has not yet had the morning that will ask her which of them she is — the mother who keeps the loss open, or the father who keeps the rule that stops things — because it is summer, and the river is only the river, and the worst thing in the newspaper on the shop counter is a debate about the drains.

She rinses the two cups at the yard butt and goes in to light the copper.

Her mother is already at the counter, sleeves back, hair pinned, doing the thing she does every morning of the world, which is to turn a pile of other people's dirt into a pile of other people's whiteness and take four farthings for it, warm from being held the whole walk over. Sau-Ling Zhang was sent for across the world the year the shop could feed two, and has been poor in two countries, and holds, on the strength of it, opinions about who deserves clean linen that come down, when you boil them, to *everyone*. She does not look up. She says, in Cantonese, that the sailors' shirts from the boarding house have to go back today whatever else does not, because the man pays on the last Monday and only then, and a shop that lets the last Monday slide once will find the man has taught himself it slides always.

Su says, in Cantonese, that she will take them.

The copper catches, the kindling first and then the coal, and the cold water above it begins its long climb toward the boil, and the smell of the day starts up — lye and blue-bag and hot iron, the smell that gets into the family's own clothes and stays there, so that Su has never once in her life smelt of anything but the work, and has stopped, years ago, being able to smell it on herself at all.

Out on the Causeway the carts thicken. The river goes about its enormous business a wall away, moving the freight of an empire past every door on the street as it has done since before the shop or the family or the wall, carrying cargo it will never trouble to name and, folded invisibly in among it, the consequences of things done and decided half a world and two generations off, riding quiet under the manifest, coming home.

The bowl stands on the shelf.

The fan goes back in the drawer.

What goes with her into the shop is a thing she has never once thought to call by the yard's name. She lifts the first basket of the morning from its shelf with both knees bending and her spine kept long, exactly the way a low stance bends, and thinks of it only as the sensible way to lift a heavy thing without hurting her back, a fact her mother taught her at the same age her father taught her the horse, and it has never once occurred to her that the two lessons were teaching the identical fact in two different rooms of the same house. She wrings a sheet with both wrists turning against each other in the precise rolling motion that unlocks a joint her father spent years training into her forearms, and she calls it, if she calls it anything at all, only *getting the water out properly*. She reads a customer's mood the instant he crosses the threshold — the shoulders arriving half a second before the face does, cheerful or short-tempered or carrying a grief he has not yet decided whether to mention — and she called this, before she had any other word for it, only *knowing people*, the ordinary competence any woman develops behind any counter busy enough to teach it to her. The practice does not announce itself in the doing. It has gone all the way down into the ordinary shape of her days, the way a river goes all the way down into the shape of the land it has been running through so long that the land has stopped remembering it was ever anything else, and a stranger watching Su Zhang fold a shirt would see only a laundress folding a shirt, competent, quick, entirely unremarkable — and would have no possible way of knowing that competence and quickness were built, stitch by stitch, dawn by dawn, out of the single hardest art either of her two countries ever produced.

And Su Zhang, who can stand in a dark yard with her eyes shut and tell you through two crossed wrists which foot a man's weight is on, ties an apron over the strength nobody who buys their laundry will ever guess is there — nobody, and least of all herself, who has long since stopped being able to feel the seam where the yard ends and the shop begins — and opens the shop, on the last ordinary summer the century will let her have.

The school in Canton had a name and Su has it, and there is nobody left in England to say it to.

Her grandfather taught there, or under it, or beside it — the arrangement was never a thing Wei could describe in English and possibly could not have described in Cantonese either, because it was not an institution in the sense a person raised in Wapping means by the word. There was a courtyard behind a herbalist's. There was a man who taught, and men who paid him in whatever they had, and a hundred years of somebody teaching in that courtyard before him, and the whole of it existed, as far as Su has ever been able to establish, in exactly nobody's records.

The art in that courtyard was Hung Ga — Hung Kuen, the fist of the Hung people, hard and low and built out of the stance upward, the horse first and everything else afterward. Her grandfather had it from a man who had it from a man, and could name four of them backward and stopped there, and told Wei once that any lineage claiming to reach further back than four names is a lineage doing something other than remembering.

He had the soft work too, from a different room and a different teacher — the close-range listening the southerners call Wing Chun, the sticking hands, the whole business of learning a stranger's weight through a crossed wrist. The two arts are not the same art and were never taught as one, and Wei has been at some pains, across thirteen years, to make sure his daughter knows which half of what she does came from where, because a person who cannot say where a thing came from will eventually start saying it came from her.

The courtyard shut in the eighteen-forties.

There was a war. It was fought, from the Cantonese end of it, over the right of a foreign company to go on selling a drug into a country that had asked it repeatedly to stop, and it was won by the company's side, and one of the ten thousand small consequences of the winning — far too small to appear in any account of the war written in English — was that a herbalist's courtyard behind a street in Canton stopped being a place where a man could reliably be paid in vegetables to teach other men to stand correctly.

Su's grandfather went to the coast and taught nobody in particular for twenty years and set bones for a living, which is how the *dit da* half of it came into the family and why there are three brown bottles on a shelf in a chandlery in Limehouse mixed to an arrangement that has never been written down.

He died in Hong Kong the year before Wei sailed. The fan came out of the bundle with the white bowl. Everything else — the courtyard, the four names backward, the herbalist, the whole apparatus of a hundred years of somebody teaching in a particular square of dirt — did not come, and could not have come, and is now, in 1888, held entire in the head of one middle-aged rope-and-tar man in Wapping, who is passing it, six paces by four at a time, into the head of one nineteen-year-old laundress, who has never seen China and never will.

That is what is actually happening in this yard every morning, and neither of the two people doing it has ever once described it that way, or would.

## II. The Causeway

There are perhaps three hundred Chinese in the whole of London this summer, in a city of five and a half million, and Su Zhang could put a name to a good third of them.

They are not gathered the way the newspapers, when they trouble to notice, like to imagine — no walled quarter, no secret city, nothing so tidy. They are a thin scatter along two streets at the elbow of the river: Limehouse Causeway and Pennyfields, a few dozen doors between them where a Cantonese word will be understood, and the Zhang chandlery is one of the doors. Most of the three hundred are not settled at all. They are sailors, here between ships — Cantonese and a few from Amoy and Shanghai, off the tea boats and the mail steamers, ashore for a week or a season with a wage in a knotted cloth and nowhere in all this enormous grey city that was built with any thought of them. They come up the West India Dock Road looking for the one building that was: the Strangers' Home, put up thirty years back by men who wanted the stranded Asiatic sailor to have a bed that was not a gutter and a keeper who was not a crimp, and who could not quite decide, in the building of it, whether they were performing a charity or a quarantine, and so performed both.

Su knows the Home the way she knows everything on the Causeway, which is to say by its custom. Its linen comes to the shop.

This morning it is her errand, and she goes with the big basket on her hip, fourteen pounds of it, still warm from the flat-iron and breathing soap and hot cotton into the summer street. She has done the arithmetic of the load without thinking, the way she does the arithmetic of everything: what her arms can do with it, how it changes what her hands are free for, which is a calculation her father put into her so early she cannot remember learning it and would not now know how to stop. A basket is a thing you carry. It is also, if the street asks the question, a thing you can set down in the half-second it takes to be somewhere the street did not expect you to be. She has never once had to. She makes the calculation anyway, every load, all her life, and it costs her nothing, and that is the whole of what her father means by the practice being a weapon that walks about all day: not that she is waiting for a fight, but that she is never, in the animal part of her, not ready for one, and has learned to carry the readiness the way other women carry a shawl.

Outside the ropewalk two carters have let their argument over a right of way grow past the point either of them quite wants it to have reached, voices up, a hand gone to a coat-front, the small ordinary spark that a hot morning and a blocked lane will strike off almost any two tired men sooner or later. Su does not slow, and does not speed, and passes between them at the exact width the lane still allows, her basket turning a half-inch on her hip to clear a gesturing elbow before the elbow has finished its swing — a correction so small she does not experience it as a correction at all, only as the ordinary business of getting a basket of washing down a narrow lane without incident, the same unremarkable competence she would credit to any woman who has carried loads down this particular street for as many years as she has. The two carters do not so much as register that anyone has passed between them. Su does not register that she has just read two grown men's shoulders the way her father taught her to read a stranger's, and adjusted a fourteen-pound weight around the exact geometry of a blow neither man ever came close to throwing, because in her own accounting of the morning nothing at all has happened. A girl carried a basket down a lane. That is the whole of the entry, and it is the only entry she would ever think to make.

The Causeway at this hour belongs to the women and the very old and the ships' cooks buying for the day. A man Su has called uncle since she could talk, though he is no relation, sits in his doorway shelling something into a bowl and lifts two fingers to her without breaking the rhythm of his hands. Two doors on, the boarding-house keeper's wife is beating a quilt against her own wall, and the dust of it hangs gold in a slant of early sun, and she calls out to ask after Su's mother's chest, in Cantonese, and Su calls back that it is well, in Cantonese, and neither of them slows down. This is the street where Su has a whole self that the rest of London never meets: the Cantonese self, quick and easy and at home, the one that has a place in a hundred small accounts of who is owed a greeting and who a longer word and who, this week, a silence because of a thing done last week. She keeps that self here, on these two streets, the way you keep a good coat on a hook by the door, and takes it off when she crosses back out into the English city, and puts on the other one, the careful one, the one that says thank you in its politest English and does not tell the watch committee what it was deciding on the dock road.

Two languages, her father says, and two rooms to keep them in, and you do not let them run together, because a rule that has learned to say itself in both is a rule you own, but a self that has forgotten which room it is standing in is a self somebody can walk into. She has kept the two rooms so long she has stopped feeling the wall between them, most days. On the bad days of this coming autumn she will feel it like a bruise.

At the Strangers' Home the porter knows her and the linen goes in against a chit, and in the long bare room off the hall a dozen men are sitting out the gap between one ship and the next in the particular stillness of sailors ashore, which is not rest. One of them is playing a two-stringed fiddle very quietly to himself, a tune from some coast Su has never seen, and it goes into her the way Catherine's half-songs go into her, a front with the back left off, a whole country folded into eight notes and then gone. She does not stop. She has learned not to stop for the fiddle. A girl who stopped every time the Causeway handed her a piece of somewhere she would never go would never get the laundry delivered.

Her father crossed the world to this street with one bundle and a white bowl in it. Her mother was sent for four years after, when the shop could feed two — sent for across the whole earth like a chest of goods, is how Sau-Ling puts it herself, without bitterness, only accuracy, a woman packed and shipped and delivered and signed for, and then, at the far end of it, met on a dock by a small serious man she had seen exactly twice before the wedding and has now cooked for and argued with and buried a son beside for twenty years, and would, she says, do the crossing again, because the alternative was Hong Kong and a harbour going hard, and here at least the loss is a loss she chose. Sau-Ling was dipped in the cold water at the mission church by the docks the year she landed, and took the English God the way she takes in washing — practically, at a fair rate, with strong private opinions about how the work ought to be done. She goes on a Sunday. She sings the hymns in an English still furred at the edges after twenty years. And she keeps, underneath the borrowed God, a heresy entirely her own, which Su has heard her speak aloud exactly once and files as the truest thing her mother owns: that a God worth walking to church for is a God who runs His books at a loss on purpose, forever, the way a decent laundress takes in the drowned man's shirt and the used-up woman's Sunday bodice and the sailor's slops that will be paid for late if at all — because a place like Wapping is meant to be carried at a loss, and mercy is only that: a column that will never once come right, kept open anyway, deliberately, world without end.

Her father keeps no God and every rule, and closes his columns to the farthing, and the two of them have been the opposite ends of the same argument across a shop counter for two decades, and love each other in the way of people who have found the one other person whose wrongness they can stand to live inside of.

Su is made of both. She has her mother's open ledger and her father's closing one, both, at once, which is a thing that has no name in Cantonese or English and which she carries the way she carries the basket — as a weight distributed so evenly that a stranger would not know it was there.

She comes back down the Causeway with the empty basket light on her hip and turns, at the end of it, out of the Chinese street and into the roar of the English one — the Highway, the docks, the whole heaving trade of the greatest port on earth going about its morning — and the wall in her comes up between one room and the next, quiet, automatic, and she is the other Su now, the careful one, walking her clean baskets into a city that will let a Chinese girl through so long as she is carrying its washing and keeping her eyes down over it.

The rest of the day's linen goes where the rest of it always goes: to the river, and the men on it, and the one door on the whole English side of her life where the two rooms are allowed, a little, to stand open. The sailors' shirts and the publicans' aprons and the constables' collars are all settled, by an arrangement older than Su, at the bar of the Prospect of Whitby, because half the men who owe for their washing can be found there faster than anywhere else on the reach, and because the house has kept the laundry's account, fair and slow and open, since before Su was born.

She shifts the basket, and turns down toward the water, and does not yet know — how could she; it is summer, and the worst thing in the paper is still the drains — that of all the doors on the river the one she is walking toward is the one where the whole of the coming autumn is already sitting, on its usual stool, drinking its two brandies, asking after her mother's chest with what sounds exactly like kindness.

The round takes two hours and ten minutes in fine weather and Su could walk it with her eyes shut, and the order of it has not changed since she was fourteen, because the order is not arbitrary: it is the shortest walk that gets the heaviest baskets off her first and finishes within sixty yards of the Prospect, where the shop's chit is settled.

Out of the yard and left. Two houses on the Causeway itself — a ship's cook with four shirts a week who pays on the nail, and a widow who takes in two lodgers and whose washing is therefore three people's and is charged as two, an arrangement arrived at in 1882 and never once mentioned since by either party.

Then Pennyfields, where the round is Chinese for four stops together, and where Su speaks nothing but Cantonese for eleven minutes and comes out of it, every single time, feeling faintly and unaccountably rested, in a way she has never examined and would not know how to describe.

Then the Strangers' Home, which is the largest account the shop has and the one that keeps it solvent — a great barrack of an institution off the West India Dock Road for Asiatic and African sailors, with a chapel and a reading room and beds for two hundred, and a linen requirement that does not care what the weather is doing. The porter knows her. The linen goes in against a chit. In the long bare room off the entrance there are men from Bombay and Aden and Canton and Zanzibar sitting about between ships, and Su has walked through that room twice a week since she was twelve, and has heard in that time a fair proportion of the languages spoken on the surface of the earth, and has learned to say *good morning* in eleven of them and nothing else at all in any.

Then Narrow Street, and the two public houses that send their table linen, and the ropewalk foreman's house, and the eel shop.

Then the sufferance wharf, and the four houses behind it, one of which will want a delivery on a Thursday evening in November in a fog.

Then back along the Wall to the Prospect with the last basket and the book.

She does the whole of it with the basket on her right hip going out and her left coming back, changing sides at the Strangers' Home, which is the halfway point, and which she reached at the same minute of the same hour for six years without ever once owning a watch.

The shop's day is a machine and Su is most of the moving parts of it.

It starts with the copper, which is the whole business in one object: a great riveted vessel set in brick with a fire-hole under it, filled by hand from the yard tap two buckets at a time, and drawn — that is, lit — at ten past five, because the water wants an hour and a quarter to come up and the first sorted load wants to go in at half past six and not a minute later or the whole day runs behind itself until dark.

Su has drawn that fire every morning since she was thirteen. She can do it in four minutes and could do it in three if her mother would let her, which her mother will not, on the grounds that a fire laid fast is a fire laid twice.

Then sorting, which is the skilled part and which nobody outside the trade believes is skilled. Whites, coloureds, greasy work-clothes, and the fourth pile nobody has a name for that is anything with something on it that will set if it goes in hot. A shirt sorted wrong costs the shop the shirt. Su sorts by touch and smell as much as by eye, and can tell a dock-tar mark from an engine-oil mark with her eyes shut, and learned the difference at about seven from a mother who tested her on it the way other mothers test spelling.

Then the wash: dolly-stick and posser in the tubs for the heavy stuff, the copper for the whites, soda and soft soap, and the particular arm-work of the dolly, which is the one job in that shop nobody has ever pretended is anything but punishing. Then rinsing, two waters. Then the blue — a small linen bag of ultramarine swirled through the last rinse to make white look whiter, which is a trick of the eye and not of chemistry and which every laundress in England knows and almost no customer has ever heard of. Then starch for collars and cuffs, mixed thin or thick according to who is fussy.

Then the wringing, and then the mangle, which is two wooden rollers under a weighted box and which will take a woman's fingers off if she is thinking about anything else, and which Su has turned ten thousand times without once thinking about anything else, because her mother took her hand at the age of eight and held it four inches from the running rollers and said one sentence about it and never had to say it again.

Then the drying, which is where the whole trade lives or dies. In fine weather, the lines in the yard and across the lane; in wet, the loft over the back room with its slatted racks and its stove, which is why the Zhangs can promise a Tuesday delivery in November when three other shops on that reach cannot.

Then the irons — flat irons, four of them, heated in rotation on the range, spat on to test, wiped on a cloth kept for it, each one good for about ninety seconds of real work before it goes back and its sister comes off. There is a particular rhythm to ironing with four irons that is entirely unlike ironing with one, and Su has it so completely that she does not look at the range at all; her left hand simply puts one down and takes up the next at the moment the next is ready, and if you asked her how she knows, she would say that you can hear it, which is true and useless.

Then folding, ticketing, wrapping, and the rack.

Then the round.

It is fourteen hours in summer and thirteen in winter, six days a week, and the sums at the end of it are these: a shirt, two pence; a collar, a halfpenny; a sheet, threepence; a week's washing for a small family, one and threepence with the mending thrown in if the mending is small. Against that: coal, soap, soda, blue, starch, the rent, and the slate — the slate being the money owed by people who mean to pay, most of whom eventually do.

Sau-Ling has run that arithmetic since 1873 and has never once been more than a shilling out, and cannot read a word of English.

Su learned the whole machine before she was twelve and has never worked anywhere else and never will. She is, by the summer of 1888, quicker at every single station in it than her mother, which neither of them has ever said aloud, and which both of them arranged around some time in the winter of 1886 by the simple expedient of Sau-Ling moving permanently to the counter.

## III. The Prospect

The Prospect of Whitby stands where the Wall meets the water, and has stood there long enough that nobody in it can tell you truly how long — only that there was a tavern on the spot when the Company was young and the river ran browner, and that the flagstones underfoot have been walked smooth by more feet than the parish has ever counted living.

Su comes in with the last of the day's baskets and the room takes her the way it always takes her: without turning a head. That is the Prospect's manner and always has been. It is not a room that stares at a Chinese girl with a laundry basket, because it is a room that has been receiving the whole traffic of the river through its door for three hundred years and has long since spent its capacity for surprise on things a great deal stranger than Su. Sailors off every coast, watermen, lightermen, a magistrate now and then slumming, once — the story goes, and Su half believes it — a hanged man's widow come to drink at the very bar her husband had drunk at before Execution Dock got him. The room has seen it. The room pours the ale.

Flo is behind the bar, sleeves pushed up, drawing a pint with the particular boredom of a woman who can do it in her sleep and frequently nearly does. She is somewhere north of forty and has been at the Prospect since before Su was born, and she and Su have an understanding conducted almost entirely in nods: the laundry basket gets its corner, the chits get settled against the slate, and the two of them exchange perhaps a dozen words in a delivery and consider it a full conversation. Flo has a way of putting a thing in front of a person before they have asked for it that Su recognises, because her own mother has it — the reflex of a woman whose whole life has been the anticipation of what a room needs next.

In the far corner the chair with the too-short back leg sits empty, as it usually does. It is propped level with a folded playbill, and the playbill has been there, everyone agrees, for going on two centuries, replaced when it rots but always by another, nobody left alive who could say why the chair is left for last or who first decided it should be. Su has asked. Flo does not know. The oldest waterman in the house does not know. It is simply a thing the Prospect does, the way a family keeps a saint's day whose saint it has forgotten: the empty chair, propped level, left till last. Su, who was raised on a shelf holding a bowl nobody is allowed to use, does not find it strange at all. Every house worth the name keeps one account it can no longer read.

The laundry account is one of the ones it can. It is older than Su and settled at this bar because half the men who owe for their washing can be found here faster than at their own lodgings, and because the house has carried it — fair, slow, open — since a keeper two keepers back shook hands on it with a Zhang. The sailors' shirts come round in the wicker basket and are claimed against the slate; the constables' collars, the publican's own aprons, the slops off the boarding houses. Su has washed, in her time at this counter, for living men and for two or three she later learned were dead by the time the shirt came clean — the last washing arriving before the last news, as it so often does on a river. She thinks nothing of it. It is the trade.

At the end of the bar, on his own stool, sits Dr. Cray.

He is there most evenings and has been for fifteen years, since he gave up the Company's medical service and took his rooms nearby, and the Prospect holds him the way it holds the chair and the flower and the smoothed flagstones — as one of its fixed things, a fact of the room. Dr. Reginald Cray, retired surgeon, Bengal service before the Mutiny, a pension arriving quietly from Leadenhall Street for years nobody asks him about. Two brandies of an evening, never a third, drunk slowly enough that the smell of them barely clears his own coat. The same stool. The good leather case, oiled saddle-leather, on the floor against the stool's leg, where it always is, where his hand can find it without looking. He is unhurried in everything. He watches the room the way some men read a newspaper, taking it all in at a comfortable distance, and if you catch his eye he gives you a small courteous nod and goes back to whatever it is he is quietly totting up.

The bar loves him. This is a plain fact and Su has never had cause, this summer, to think it anything but a good one. When the potboy took a fever in the spring, Cray climbed the two flights to the boy's room under the roof, twice, and charged nothing, and the boy mended and is downstairs now, alive, collecting pots. Cray asks after Sau-Ling's chest with what sounds like real knowledge, and the liniment he named for it — camphor and something Su's mother now buys by the ounce — actually works, which is more than the mission dispensary ever managed. He remembers the barmaids' names. He is courteous to them in a manner some of them find gentlemanly and one or two find difficult to name a reason for disliking, and cannot, and so keep pouring his brandy and thinking no more of it. He is, by every account the room can render, a kind man, and a clever one, and a piece of luck for a poor parish that has exactly one stethoscope in three streets and it is his.

Su has one memory of him that she has never quite been able to put in the drawer it seems to want.

She was twelve. A docker went down under a swung crate at the quay and came up with his arm hanging wrong — grey, sweating, making the low sound big frightened men make when their pride goes — and they walked him into the Prospect because it was nearest and because Cray was on his stool. Su was in the room with a basket, waiting on Flo, and she watched the whole of it, because her father had already taught her, without either of them calling it that, to watch a skilled man's hands.

Cray finished his brandy first.

Not hurried, not cruel — simply finishing it, the last quarter-inch, setting the glass down square on the wood before he rose, while the docker stood there grey and shaking and held up by his mates. Then he knelt on the flagstones and took the ruined arm in both hands and *read* it — Su had no other word for it then and has found no better one since — read it through his thumbs the way her father reads a knot he cannot see the far end of, feeling for the thing under the surface that would tell him what the surface could not. He told the man, in the voice you would use to remark on the weather, that this would be brief, and that he might shout if he liked, most did. And then he did one small exact thing, a turn and a press that Su's eye, trained as it was, could barely follow, and the shoulder went back into the man with a sound like a heavy drawer pushed shut, and the docker shouted, and it was over, and Cray was already wiping his hands on his own handkerchief before the shout had finished coming down off the rafters.

The bar thought it was wonderful. It *was* wonderful. Su has turned it over for seven years and cannot make it be anything but wonderful, and that is the whole of the trouble.

Because what her twelve-year-old eye caught, and filed, and could not name, was that nothing crossed the doctor's face the entire time he did it. Not distaste. Not effort. Not the small private flinch that even a good butcher gives, that her own gentle father gives when the fan has to break before it can splint. There was only a level attention — the attention of a man doing a thing he is very good at to a piece of material that happened, that evening, to be a person. The material shouted; the attention did not alter. He read the arm the way he reads the room, the way he reads, Su thinks now and did not think then, a ledger: totting up a thing whose columns only he can see.

She filed it, at twelve, without knowing the drawer.

She does not know it this summer either. She will learn the drawer in an alley, on the last third of a November night still four months off, and after that she will never be able to un-know it, and will spend the rest of her life unable to decide whether the not-knowing was innocence or only the mercy of a girl who had not yet been shown. For now it sits in her, that one memory, filed wrong, faintly askew, the single splinter in an otherwise whole liking. She notices it about once a season, the way you notice a stair that is a half-inch off, and steps over it, and thinks no more of it.

Tonight there is no shoulder to set, no fever to climb to. There is only the ordinary evening: the room filling, the ale going down, the river doing its enormous business a wall away. A sailor newly paid off and three drinks past his own good sense reels back from the bar just as Su crosses behind him with a tray of empty tankards, and there is, for the length of one breath, every reason to expect the whole tray to go over the flagstones in a crash the entire room will turn to look at. It does not happen. Su's free hand finds the small of his back before he has finished falling into the space she is standing in, a touch so light and so brief he will not remember it happened at all, and steadies him upright and past her in the same motion that keeps the tray level, and is three steps further on toward the bar before the man himself has fully registered that he swayed. Flo, who has watched the whole small non-event over the sailor's shoulder, says nothing about it, because there is nothing to say about it; a laundry girl carried a tray of tankards through a crowded room and nobody spilled anything, which is precisely the kind of evening the Prospect has every single night of its long life, and Su herself, setting the tray down at the bar, has already forgotten the sailor's stagger by the time her hand leaves the tray, because in her own reckoning nothing at all needed remembering.

The room has perhaps twenty people in it at this hour and Su could tell you the trade of nineteen of them.

There is the dockworker at the end of the bench, who is somewhere past sixty and was in India as a young soldier and does not discuss it, and who has been coming in four evenings a week for as long as Su can remember and has never once been drunk in her presence. There are three lightermen who arrive together and leave together and have arrived and left together since before Su was born. There is a ropewalk foreman with a bad chest. There is a widow of about fifty who comes in for one gin at seven and goes at ten past, and to whom the whole room is unfailingly, elaborately polite. There is a young man off a collier who does not know yet that he is going to be one of the regulars and will be, for the next thirty years.

There is the short-legged chair.

It stands at the far end where the floor has gone out of true, propped level with a folded playbill that gets replaced perhaps twice a year by whoever notices, and it is left for last — always, invariably, by everybody, without discussion. Su asked Flo about it once, at fifteen. Flo said it had always been left for last. Su asked why. Flo said she had asked the same question of the woman before her and had got the same answer, and that as far as she was concerned that settled it.

Nobody sits in it tonight and nobody will, and in fifty years' time, under two keepers neither Flo nor Su will ever meet, it will still be propped level with a playbill and still be left for last, and the reason will be exactly as thoroughly forgotten then as it is now.

And there is the honest position on Dr. Cray in the summer of 1888, which Su has never afterward been able to state any other way: that there is a cold place in the middle of what she thinks about him, and that it has been there since she was eleven years old and a man counted his fee in a room with her brother in it, and that it is not suspicion of anything, and does not become suspicion of anything for another four months.

And that she likes him.

Su settles the last chit with Flo and is turning to go when Cray, without quite raising his voice, asks after her mother.

The chest, he says. Better, with the warm weather?

Better, Su says. Thank you, doctor.

Good. He nods, the small courteous nod. Tell her the camphor, still, even in summer. The damp off the river does not take a holiday because the calendar says June.

I'll tell her.

And he goes back to his brandy and his ledger of the room, and Su takes up her empty basket, and that is the whole of it: a kind old doctor asking a kind question and giving good advice, on a warm evening, in a good house, in a summer when the worst thing in the newspaper is still a quarrel about the drains.

She carries the basket out past the empty chair, propped level with its two-hundred-year playbill, and into the blue summer dusk, and does not look back, and has no reason on earth to. It is only later — much later, folding in the quiet, in a winter that has not yet been invented — that she will set this evening beside a certain other one, and understand that the most frightening man she would ever meet had, the whole time she knew him, exactly the manners of the kindest, and that she had been standing in the same warm room with the difference for seven years, and had filed it, and had not known the drawer.
