# Lee (1888, and a winter eight years gone)

> **1880, a winter**
>
> *"Fever."*
>
> — Dr. Reginald Cray's register, the whole of what it says

***

## I. The Third Night

There was a brother.

Su does not talk about him, this summer or any summer, and the family does not talk about him either, not in the way other households talk about their dead, with an anniversary and a set of good clothes kept for the churchyard and a habit of saying his name out loud once a year so the saying of it does not become a thing anybody has to work up to. The Zhangs keep Lee the way they keep the white bowl: on a high shelf, dusted, never used, entered somewhere that is not quite conversation. Su was four when he was born and eleven when he died, and in between those two ages there is a brother-shaped seven years that she carries the way she carries the basket — distributed so evenly across the whole of her that a stranger would never know the weight was there at all.

He was born after Su, in the ordinary run of things, and given, like her, two names in two rooms — a Cantonese one their father used at the practice and an English one for the register and the mission church, because a family living where they lived needed both, the way it needed both languages, kept carefully apart so that neither one would ever have to answer for the whole of a person by itself. He came into the yard at seven, as she had, and stood in the horse the first morning not understanding a word of what was being asked of him, which their father told her, years later, was itself the lesson, and the hardest one, and the one he had worried, with her, that she might not have in her. Lee had it in him too. He had a year of his own dawn mornings behind him by the time it mattered, one year of standing in the cold with his elbows in the wrong place — always the elbows, their father used to say, laughing despite himself, because there is one part of every child's body that refuses instruction longest, and in Lee it was the elbows — one year of the fan tapping them level, one year of tea on the step for after.

He was seven when the fever took him. One year in the yard. His stance still all elbows.

What Su has of him, at nineteen, is not very much, and she has been careful with all of it.

She has the elbows. She has the fact that he could not whistle and would not stop trying, for the whole of one summer, everywhere, at all hours, until their mother said something in Cantonese so unrepeatable that both children went silent for an entire evening and Lee, from that night on, whistled only in the yard. She has him at five sitting on an upturned crate in the shop doorway conducting an imaginary transaction with an imaginary customer over an imaginary ledger, in an English lifted so exactly from their mother's that Sau-Ling came out and stood behind him for a full minute without his knowing and then went back inside without a word. She has the specific weight of him asleep against her shoulder on the step outside the mission church one Sunday when he was six and had been up since four for no reason anybody could establish.

She has one argument. They fought about almost nothing, being seven years and four years apart in temperament as well as age, but there was one — over a spinning top, over whose it had originally been, a question neither of them had any evidence for — and Su won it, comprehensively, by being eleven, and Lee went off up the stairs with his ears red, and the whole thing was over inside twenty minutes and neither of them ever thought about it again.

She thinks about it perhaps four times a year now. That is what a death does to an argument about a spinning top.

And she has one thing she has never told anybody in her life, which is that on the last ordinary evening — the evening before the fever, the last one in which the household was still simply a household — Lee asked her to show him the wrist thing, meaning a turn their father had shown her that week and not yet shown him, and Su said no, because it was hers and she had had it for four days and he was seven and would only do it wrong.

She has had eight years to work out that this was an entirely reasonable thing for an eleven-year-old to say and that there is no version of any of it in which showing him a wrist turn would have altered one single thing that happened afterward.

It has made no difference whatsoever. She still has the evening. She expects to have it at eighty.


It came on him the way fevers come on in a house by the river in winter, which is to say without warning and then all at once: a boy over-warm at supper, cross without cause, refusing his rice, and by the second night burning to the touch and talking to people who were not in the room. Su, eleven, remembers being sent to bed and not sleeping, listening through the wall to her mother's feet crossing and recrossing the floor above the shop, the particular rhythm of a woman keeping a vigil she does not yet have a name for because naming it would be agreeing to it.

Her father went out himself, at ten at night, through sleet that had turned the Causeway to black glass, to Dr. Cray's rooms two streets off.

He knocked, and was heard, and was answered by the housekeeper through a door that did not open past its chain: the doctor was at his supper, and would call in the morning.

Wei Zhang did not raise his voice. Su was not there to see it — she has this from her mother, told once, years afterward, in the flat tone Sau-Ling uses for the things that cost her the most to say — but she has no trouble picturing it, because she has stood beside her father at a shop door often enough to know exactly how he stands when a thing has been refused him and he has decided, in the same breath, that raising his voice would only give the refusal something to push against. He said thank you. He walked home through the same black sleet with nothing to show for the walk but the walk itself, and sat up the rest of that night with a wet cloth and his hand on his son's chest, doing the only thing a father who is not a physician can do, which is stay.

Cray called in the morning. Unhurried, as he is unhurried in everything; the potboy's fever mended for nothing, and the docker's shoulder set on the flagstones with a level nothing on his face that Su, at twelve, would file without a name for it and learn the name for at nineteen — but that is a different winter, a different watching, and Su had not yet begun it. This is only the morning after. Cray looked at Lee for about the length of a pot of tea. Said what doctors say when they have already decided that the rest of the visit is only waiting dressed up as medicine. Took his fee from Sau-Ling's hand.

Counted it.

That is the part her mother has never once, in eight years, been able to let go of — not the visit's lateness, not the housekeeper's chained door, not even the boy's death itself, which Sau-Ling has folded, the way she folds everything, into the ledger her God is permitted to run at a loss. It is the counting. A mild woman who has wished no living soul any harm, who calls weeping a Tuesday and gets on with the pressing, and who still, eight winters on, cannot describe that morning without her hands going still over whatever they are doing, because a man had just stood in a room with a dying child in it and counted his coin before he left it.

He wrote fever in his register. It was true, as far as it went. A fever had, in point of fact, killed the boy. The word cost Cray nothing and committed him to nothing and closed the account exactly as far as any account needed closing, in a parish that had buried children of fevers all winter and would bury more before the spring, and there was, in the strictest reading of it, no lie anywhere on that page.

Lee died on the third night.

Su was in the room for that part. She does not describe it, even to herself, in the years that follow — there is a wall in her own memory precisely where that hour should be, built, she suspects, by the same part of her that decides which knots are worth naming aloud and which are better left alone — but she remembers, with a clarity that has never once dimmed, the specific silence that came after, which was not the silence of a house at rest. It was the silence of a house that has just discovered how much noise a small boy's ordinary breathing had been making in it the whole time, without anyone once noticing, until it stopped.

They buried him in the mission churchyard on a Thursday, in ground the parish gave at the pauper rate and which their mother has paid something into every year since, quietly, out of the shop, in amounts nobody has ever discussed.

There were eleven people. The minister, the three of them, the woman from the eel shop, four men off the reach who had done business with Wei for years and turned up without being asked, and two Chinese from Pennyfields whom Su had never seen before and never saw again and who stood at the back and left before the end.

Sau-Ling did not weep at the graveside, and has never once wept at any graveside, and has explained herself on the subject exactly once in Su's hearing, years later, at the copper, apropos of a different death entirely: that she weeps the way stones weep, which is to say slowly, from the inside, in a manner not available for anybody else's inspection, and that a woman who has already put two sisters in the water on the far side of the world has some right to choose her own arrangements.

Wei did not stand at the back or at the front. He stood beside his wife with his hands loose and empty at his sides in the exact position Su will recognise, eight years later and for the rest of her life, as the one he takes in a doorway when a street is deciding what it thinks of him — and it will take her until she is nearly thirty to work out that this was not composure, and was never composure, and that she has spent a whole childhood mistaking a shape for the feeling it was built to contain.

The yard reopened the following morning.

That is not a figure of speech and it is not, Su has come to believe, hardness. It is simply that on the morning after they buried Lee her father went out at half past four as he had every morning of his adult life, and stood in the horse, and was still standing in it at a quarter past five when his daughter came out in her coat over her nightdress and stood beside him without being asked, and neither of them said anything at all, and they did the form.

Her elbows were wrong that morning. He corrected them.

## II. What Neither Fact Would Cancel

It was not murder. Su has turned this over more times than she could count and always arrives at the same honest place: it was not murder, and it was not even neglect as the parish would reckon neglect, because a doctor did come, and the fee was the fee charged to any house on that street, and the word in the register was the true word. What it was, was smaller than a crime and colder for being smaller: a man deciding, somewhere behind his own eyes, at his own street door, through his own housekeeper, what a chandler's boy off the Causeway was worth getting cold for on a bad night. That decision cost nothing in the ledger any court could read. It cost everything in the one Su's mother keeps.

And the maddening, unclosable thing — the thing Su will still be turning over on the night, four months from this one, when she finally has cause to turn over everything she knows about Dr. Reginald Cray at once — is that both facts are equally, exactly true, and neither one has ever agreed, in eight years, to cancel the other. The same man who would not hurry through sleet for Lee climbed two flights, twice, for the potboy in the spring, and charged him nothing. The same hands that counted Sau-Ling's coin over her dying son set a grown docker's shoulder with a gentleness the whole bar still talks about. He is not two men wearing one face. He is one man, entirely himself both times, and Su has never once been able to make that add up to anything simpler than what it is: that a person can be a mercy to one household and a closed door to another, in the same season, out of the same unhurried temperament, and call neither of them a contradiction, because to him they very likely are not one.

Her father said nothing then, and has said nothing since. He took the family's own liniment advice from the same doctor afterward, and takes it still, because it is good advice and a poor shop cannot afford a feud with the only stethoscope in three streets, and there is a kind of survival in that arithmetic that Su understood long before she had the words for it: that the parish teaches its poor, early and thoroughly, which griefs they are permitted to act on and which they must simply learn to carry past, the way you carry a basket past a man you would rather not greet, because greeting him costs you nothing you can afford to keep and not greeting him costs you everything you have left.

That whole winter, her father taught her forms in the cold yard with a stillness Su mistook, at eleven, for calm.

She was steady that winter herself, in a way nobody in that house had any attention to spare for noticing. She was eleven years old and her brother was dead and she fetched water twice a day from the standpipe at the top of the lane, two cans, forty pounds of it, down a lane iced over for six weeks — and did not once spill either can, and did not once slip, and would not have been able to tell you how she managed either, because it did not occur to her that there was anything to manage. Her mother, who spilt water three times that winter and fell once, did not remark on it. Nobody remarked on it. There was a boy in the churchyard and a shop to open and the whole of the world's attention that January was pointed somewhere else entirely, which is, in the end, how the largest facts about a person generally get missed. She understands it now for what it was: not calm, but the practice doing, for her father, the one thing it is actually built to do — not answering a wrong, only containing one, holding a grief inside a shape precise enough that the grief cannot spill past the edges of it and reach the boy still living who needs him steady. Never in anger, the rule says. Su watched her father keep that rule through the worst morning of his life and did not understand, until years later, what it had cost him to keep it, or that keeping it, that particular winter, may have been the single hardest thing the art ever asked of him.

Lee is the entry Su scratches out first, every time she takes stock of what she owes the world and what the world owes her. His debt is real. It is owed. And it is exactly, precisely, the kind the four rules forbid her to collect — because collecting it would mean deciding, on her own authority, in her own anger, that a man's whole life could be balanced against a boy's, and her father raised her to know the difference between a ledger that is allowed to run at a loss and a ledger a person takes it upon themselves to close by force.

What complicates it past any hope of resolution is that Dr. Cray went on being good to that family for eight years afterward.

Not conspicuously. Not in a way anybody could point to as amends, and Su has never once believed it was meant as amends, because a man who thought he had something to make up for would have behaved differently and this man behaved exactly as he had behaved before.

He asked after Sau-Ling's rheumatism, by name, correctly, every time, for eight years.

He looked at Su's own hand in the spring of 1884 when a mangle roller caught two fingers, and did it in the shop doorway, and did it well, and charged the shop nothing, and said — this is the part Su has never been able to get out of the drawer — that the fingers would be perfectly serviceable and that she should keep using them, which was the correct advice and was not the advice a lazier man would have given.

He recommended the Zhangs' laundry, more than once, to people who took the recommendation.

And he never sent them so much as a collar. Fifteen years, two streets away, the best drying loft on the reach, and not one item of his own washing across that counter until the week after the clasp came loose in front of her — which is a fact that means nothing whatever on its own, and which Su did not even notice until she was nineteen years old and had a reason to go looking for it.

Sau-Ling has never let him into the house again. That is the whole of her position and she has never once stated it. He is greeted at the door, and served at the counter, and asked after, and thanked; and in eight years he has not been past the shop, and neither he nor anybody else has ever appeared to notice the arrangement except the two women who made it without discussing it.

Wei has no position at all. Su asked him once, at fifteen, straight out, whether he blamed the doctor.

Her father thought about it for a long time and then said: he came in the morning.

That is not an answer, Su said.

No, her father agreed, and did not give her one, and went back to what he was doing — and Su understood, years afterward and not that evening, that she had asked a man who had spent his whole adult life on the wrong end of other people's arithmetic whether he blamed one person for behaving exactly as the arithmetic said he would, and that her father had declined the question because the honest answer would have obliged him to blame a great deal more than one doctor, and he had a shop to open.

She carries Lee the way she carries the basket — distributed, low, close in, arranged across the whole of her so evenly that a stranger on the street would never once guess there was a weight involved at all, which is not a figure of speech and is the only way this family has ever carried anything. She will carry Dr. Cray's kindness and Dr. Cray's coldness the same way, side by side, unreconciled, for the rest of her life — and in four months' time, in a fog on the wharf, she will be given, at last, the one thing eight years of carrying it quietly never gave her: a reason to stop merely carrying the weight, and start, for one single night, to act on it.

She does not know this yet. It is still summer. The newspaper on the counter still only frets about the drains, and the shop still keeps its day hours, and up on the high shelf the white bowl gathers its dust in peace, and two streets away, on his usual stool, a kind old doctor is finishing his second brandy of the evening, unhurried, as he is unhurried in everything, and asking Flo, with what sounds exactly like real interest, whether the potboy's fever ever fully cleared.
