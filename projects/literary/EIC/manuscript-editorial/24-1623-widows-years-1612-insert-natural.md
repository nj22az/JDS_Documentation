## IV. The Night After

In 1612 Maggie comes to Shadwell after closing with the last quarter of a pie under a cloth.

Anne opens the door and looks at her face.

Who ate?

Tom first. The boy most of what remained.

Which boy?

Maggie tells her.

Jack Mercer. Sixteen. Father injured at Carter's yard. Four pounds owing. Tom has found him a Company berth and put two shillings of his own wage against the debt.

Anne sets the pie near the hearth.

And?

I put Tom out.

Anne looks at her.

For paying the debt?

No.

For finding the berth?

No.

Then we are going to need the longer version.

The girls are nearly grown and know better than to hover visibly. Joan puts out plates. Her sister finds cups. Anne warms the pie while Maggie gives the evening in pieces.

The questions to Jack.

The articles already written.

Tom putting them on the Pelican bar and asking for a pen.

Maggie making him take the paper off.

The sentence.

*He will sign with or without me.*

Anne cuts the pie.

Did Jack want to go?

Yes.

Did Tom ask him?

Yes. Not as early as he should have.

Was the berth better with Tom on it?

Probably.

Was Tom lying about that?

No.

Anne puts a piece on Maggie's plate.

Eat.

I brought it for you.

Then watch me eat yours after you finish it.

Maggie eats.

Only then does Anne ask, What did you want him to say?

Maggie looks at the crust.

That there was a choice.

There was.

He kept saying the boy would go anyway.

Maybe he would.

That was not the point.

I know.

Maggie looks up.

Anne has known her too long to offer agreement as comfort.

Then why are you looking at me like that?

Because there being a choice does not mean every choice is clean.

That is what I told him.

And did you leave room for his choice to be useful anyway?

Maggie says nothing.

Anne waits.

He will keep the boy safer than some boatswain who sees only another back to haul on a line, Maggie says at last.

Probably.

He is paying part of the debt himself.

Yes.

He asked Jack what he wanted.

Eventually.

Maggie pushes the plate away.

You are making his case.

No. You already know his case. That is why you are here instead of sleeping.

The younger Bell girl carries the empty cups away.

Anne folds her hands.

Did you tell him to leave because you thought he was wrong?

Maggie looks toward the door.

I told him to leave because I could not bear him standing there asking me to bless it.

Did he ask for blessing?

Not in words.

Anne nods.

Words are usually where we hide the cheaper version.

Maggie gives her a tired look.

You have become very wise since Matthew died.

No. I have become very tired of men who arrive with papers and women who arrive pretending they have no papers in their heads.

That gets a breath of laughter from Maggie.

It does not last.

I put the thimble on the bar, she says.

Anne stops moving.

What did he do?

Nothing. Nearly took it. Did not.

What did you say?

That I understood him better when he left it the first time.

Anne closes her eyes briefly.

That was cruel.

Yes.

True?

Some of it.

Those are often the cruelest ones.

Maggie looks at the hearth.

I told him go.

For tonight?

I did not say.

Did he ask?

Yes.

And?

I said go again.

Anne takes the remaining pie and cuts it in half.

Maggie says, You think I should fetch him.

No.

Write?

No.

Then what?

Sleep.

Maggie looks offended.

Anne gives her half the pie.

Tomorrow you can decide whether you were exact. Tonight you are angry and he is angry and Jack still has four pounds against him. None of those improves because you walk the river in the dark.

Maggie eats the pie because Anne remains standing until she does.

At the door Maggie says, I may have been too hard.

Anne shrugs.

You may have been exactly hard enough and wrong about one part. People manage both.

Maggie looks back.

That is an irritating answer.

I learned from you.

Maggie leaves.

Anne bolts the door.

She does not know that the two of them will remain apart for years.

Neither does Maggie.

That is tomorrow's work, and tomorrow has not asked for it yet.
