## X. The Letters Not Sent

By 1625 there are six letters in Tom's chest and one letter gone into a stove.

None has crossed the sea.

The first was written at Plymouth in 1616, after Virginia, while the smell of England was still stronger to him than the sight of it. He gave Maggie fog on the Banks, a shark with Josiah Grimes's disposition and trees so wide that four men could not have joined hands around them. He asked whether the Pelican roof still took water at the north corner.

He did not write about the fort.

He did not write about tobacco growing where streets ought to have been, or the two names on one passenger line, or the man who tried to count the English with a stick until the stick ran out.

He folded the page and kept it.

The second lies beneath it in Surat.

It begins with a bale that was never missing.

Tom has written the landing count on one side of the page and the warehouse count on the other, because that is the quickest way to explain how a single narrow stroke nearly became a thief. The porter appears in the letter only as Yusuf, the name Tom forced himself to keep after the man's face had already begun turning into the story of the error.

Halfway down the page Tom stops describing the two tallies.

He has made the thing sound like a lesson brought home for marking.

He folds that one too.

The third is shorter.

Twelve boys arrived hungry. I made them eat before the factor examined them.

Beneath it he wrote, You would have—

The words are struck through so hard the nib tore the paper.

Tom keeps the torn page.

He does not know why. Perhaps because a clean copy would lie about the sentence having occurred to him.

The fourth is mostly a boot.

He writes about the cobbler on his day ashore, the awning line tied badly above the shop and his own inability to sit beneath it without correcting the knot. He writes that the cobbler made him surrender both boots when Tom offered only the broken one. He writes about hot bread, lentils and a coin returned for the awning repair.

He does not write that the day frightened him more than a storm.

He does not have a useful sentence for being a man who can cross an ocean without hesitation and cannot spend an afternoon with nowhere he is required to stand.

That letter is three pages long.

It contains Maggie's name only at the top.

The fifth burns.

Tom writes it in 1622 after midnight.

Maggie —

You were right.

He sits with the three words for a long time.

Then he ruins them.

He explains Jack. He explains the Company papers. He explains the difference between keeping one boy alive and making the taking of boys good. He explains what he now understands about saying *with or without me* as if the worse man waiting outside the door could wash Tom's own hands for him.

The letter becomes excellent.

That is the problem.

By the second page Tom can hear the bargain hidden under every admission.

I know now.

Therefore let me come back.

He puts the sheets into the little iron stove in the factory office and watches the edges curl inward.

The words *You were right* blacken last.

He does not write them again.

Amboyna produces the sixth letter.

It contains very little about Tom.

A man has been retained, he writes. English factor. Name not yet certain. Sent to Batavia after the others were condemned.

There are ten English names on Tom's private page by then and ten blank lines beneath them for Japanese men the English account has counted but not named. The retained man sits below both groups as one unfinished line.

Tom writes that he means to find out why.

He reads the sentence back.

There is no defence of himself inside it that he can find.

For that reason he distrusts it less.

The page travels east inside his chest while Tom does.

It does not travel west.

The last letter is written in Batavia.

Daniel is asleep aboard by then, if what he does for twenty minutes at a time can be called sleep. The chair in his partition faces the opening. His cup is covered. Nobody swings water near him now.

Tom writes by a lantern whose catch has been repaired with leather so it cannot strike the bracket when the ship rolls.

Alive, he writes.

Damaged.

Can travel.

Food before questions.

He stops.

There is a great deal he could put after that.

He could write Maria Mori's name. He could describe the teak desk and the Dutch release and the way Maria had obtained ten Japanese names without telling him how. He could explain that the Company in Batavia had found a living witness inconvenient to the cleanest English account, and that Dutch custody had found him inconvenient to release, and that two inconveniences together can hold a man for years without either side needing to hate him.

He could write that he has finally done something Maggie might approve of.

The thought makes him put the pen down.

Daniel moves behind the screen.

Tom waits until the movement stops.

Then he writes one final sentence.

I remembered before I knew what remembering required.

He reads it twice.

Does not sign.

The letter folds once and goes inside his coat rather than the sea chest.

Six pages or bundles remain when the ship turns west. Plymouth. Yusuf. The hungry boys. The cobbler. Amboyna. Batavia.

The burned one leaves no paper between them.

Tom has not written a correspondence. Maggie has received nothing and promised nothing. He has simply carried a door around the world and, from time to time, put words in front of it without knocking.

When England appears, the letters remain in his chest.

The unsigned one stays in his coat.

Daniel goes ashore beside him.

Tom takes no paper out.
