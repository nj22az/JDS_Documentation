# 1626: The Man Who Came Back Wrong

> **1626**
>
> *They wanted what happened. They did not want it in my order.*
>
> — Daniel Vale

## I. Two Men at the Door

The first word reaches Wapping before the ship has finished warping in.

Amboyna.

Men carry it along the river without knowing what they mean by it. A Dutch fort. English merchants. Torture. Heads taken off. A quarrel over cloves big enough that London has printed versions of it and small enough that half the men repeating the story still call the island Banda.

By afternoon the Pelican has heard three accounts.

None includes Daniel Vale.

Then the door opens and Tom Fletcher brings him in.

Maggie sees Tom first.

Forty-five now. Scar through one eyebrow. Beard cut close. A sea-bag over one shoulder and no whistle at his neck.

She has not seen him in fourteen years.

Tom has one of Daniel's arms across his shoulders.

That decides what happens before either of them speaks.

Food first, Maggie says.

Tom nods.

They put Daniel on the stool nearest the hearth.

He sits with both hands flat on his knees as though somebody has instructed him not to move them. His coat hangs from him. His face is too thin around the eyes. When the door closes behind another customer, Daniel flinches so hard the cup Maggie sets down rings against the wood.

Drink.

He looks at the ale.

Water, Tom says.

Maggie changes the cup without asking why.

Daniel drinks water.

Half of it goes too quickly. He coughs, stops, stares into the cup.

Maggie puts bread beside him.

He does not touch it.

Tom remains standing.

Maggie looks at him.

Sit down.

He sits at the next stool.

Nothing is said about 1612.

There is a man between them who has had enough said around him already.

* * *

## II. Three Days

Daniel does not tell the story on the first evening.

He barely tells his name.

Daniel Vale.

Rochester.

Factor.

Amboyna.

Batavia.

Surat.

Home.

The words are not a story. They are ports on a chart somebody has torn in half.

Maggie gives him broth. He eats four spoons and stops.

On the second day she gives him broth again.

He manages six.

Tom spends most of the daylight at the river, settling pay and carrying his own sea chest from the ship. He returns before dark and sits near Daniel without asking questions.

Maggie puts a meat pie in front of Tom.

Mutton and mace.

No ale.

Tom looks at it.

Then at her.

Eat, Maggie says.

He eats.

On the third day Daniel sleeps for four hours in the chair beside the fire. The room learns to move around him. Men lower voices near the chair without deciding to. Joan Bell, grown now and working the bar most evenings, puts his cup on the left because his right hand shakes worse when reaching across his body.

Tom notices.

So does Maggie.

Neither remarks on it.

Late in the afternoon Maggie asks Tom one question.

Jack?

Alive.

Where?

Deptford. Rigging. Married.

Children?

Girl. Another expected when I last heard.

Maggie wipes the counter.

Sea again?

One voyage.

Only one?

Jack said he wanted work where he could see both ends of the rope.

Maggie's mouth alters at one corner.

And Carter's four pounds?

Cleared.

How?

Half wage. Two shillings of mine each month. Some arguments at the pay table.

Maggie looks at him.

Tom adds, Jack made most of the useful ones.

She nods.

That is all.

On the fourth morning Daniel asks for paper.

Not to write.

To put under the cup so the table does not rock.

Arthur would have admired the choice, Tom says.

Maggie looks at him.

Who?

Tom realises.

Arthur.

Maggie's face gives nothing away.

Still alive?

Last I knew.

Still Company?

Yes.

Maggie adjusts the cup herself.

Daniel looks from one of them to the other.

You two married?

No, they say together.

Daniel's mouth moves for the first time.

Not quite a smile.

* * *

## III. Water

Carter comes in after noon.

He is broader in the body and better in the coat than he was when Tom last saw him. A man who began by selling rope now sells whatever rope has become attached to by contract: canvas, blocks, pitch, spars, fittings, credit.

He sees Tom.

Fletcher.

Carter.

Fourteen years becomes one nod.

Then Carter sees Daniel.

His voice changes.

Amboyna?

Daniel looks at him.

Carter sits without waiting to be invited.

I heard you were there.

Daniel says nothing.

Carter waits longer than his nature prefers.

At last Daniel says, We were taken at night.

The room quiets by degrees.

Not because anyone announces it.

Because one man stops arguing about cordage, then another stops talking over him.

Daniel looks at the table.

They said there was a plot to take the fort.

Was there? Carter asks.

No.

Daniel says it immediately.

The certainty belongs to him. The narrator does not need to borrow it.

They questioned the Japanese soldiers first. Then the English. Towerson. Others from the factories.

How?

Daniel's fingers tighten on the cup.

Tom stands.

Not threatening Carter.

Simply moving where Daniel can see him.

Daniel takes one breath.

Cloth over the face, he says. Water poured until the body stops understanding that it is in a room.

Nobody moves.

Then they stop. Ask again. Begin again.

He touches two fingers to the table.

I signed.

What? Carter asks.

Whatever they put in front of me by the end.

His voice is flat.

That is the part that wakes me. Not the water. The ink after.

Carter looks down.

Daniel continues because stopping has become harder than continuing.

Twenty-one were executed. Ten English. Ten Japanese. One Portuguese. Towerson among them.

Beheaded?

Yes.

And you?

Daniel looks at his own hands.

Not executed.

Why?

I don't know.

There were men released, men pardoned, men sent elsewhere. I was sent to Batavia. I have spent three years trying to discover what I did right enough to live.

Tom says, Nothing.

Daniel looks at him.

Tom's face is calm.

You do not owe a reason for surviving.

Daniel looks away first.

Carter gets up.

On the shelf behind the bar is the squat stone bottle of genever Hendricks brought into the Pelican for years.

Carter takes it.

Maggie says, William.

He uncorks it anyway and pours the spirit onto the flagstones.

Juniper fills the room.

No one cheers.

Hendricks is at the far end of the bar.

Old now. Grey. River work long since handed mostly to Ned.

He watches the bottle empty.

Carter sets it down.

Hendricks says, That was mine.

Carter's anger leaves his face almost at once.

I know.

Do you?

Carter opens his mouth.

Nothing useful arrives.

Hendricks takes his coat from the peg.

Daniel says, The men who did it were Dutch.

Hendricks turns.

Yes.

I am not saying they were not.

No.

Hendricks looks at the wet stones.

Just making sure everybody knows where Holland ends.

He leaves.

The door closes quietly.

Carter remains standing with the empty bottle in his hand.

Maggie gives him a cloth.

Clean it.

Carter looks at her.

You poured it.

He kneels.

Nobody helps.

* * *

## IV. A Useful Story

London wants Daniel quickly.

That surprises him.

He expected the Company to prefer distance from a damaged factor who cannot get through a meal without stopping twice.

Instead a clerk comes to the Pelican with a formal request for Daniel to attend Leadenhall Street.

His testimony, the clerk says, may be of considerable importance.

Daniel looks at Tom.

Do I have to?

Tom says, No.

The clerk says, The Company would be grateful.

Daniel flinches at the word grateful more than he did at the door.

He goes anyway.

Tom walks with him as far as the building and waits outside.

Inside, Daniel is treated well.

That is the difficulty.

A private room. Good chair. Sweet wine he does not drink. Two directors, one legal man and a secretary whose paper is already headed before Daniel sits down.

They know Towerson's name.

They know the English dead.

They know the water torture.

They know more details from other survivors than Daniel does.

One director says, We intend that England understand what was done to our people.

Daniel looks at the prepared page.

Our people?

The English factors.

There were Japanese men.

Yes.

Ten.

The secretary's pen waits.

And a Portuguese man.

Of course.

They are not in this.

The legal man says, This statement concerns the injury done to the English Company.

Daniel looks at him.

The water felt the same on everybody.

The room changes slightly.

Not hostile.

Impatient.

One director leans forward.

Mr Vale, we do not ask you to say anything untrue. Quite the opposite. We require the clearest possible account of Dutch conduct at Amboyna.

Daniel reads the opening line.

It calls the English men innocent martyrs.

Maybe they were, Daniel says.

The director blinks.

Maybe?

I know there was no plot I joined. I know Towerson denied one. I know what the water made men sign. I do not know every meeting every man attended before I arrived in the room.

The legal man says, Dutch evidence is already being used to imply English guilt.

Then answer their evidence.

We are.

With me.

The legal man does not deny it.

That is when Daniel understands what has changed.

At Amboyna men wanted his confession because it completed a Dutch story.

In London men want his suffering because it completes an English one.

Nobody in this room intends to hurt him.

Nobody needs to.

The director slides the page closer.

Correct anything that is not true and sign at the bottom.

Daniel reads.

He crosses out *barbarous nation*.

The secretary objects.

Daniel crosses out *without cause known to any man* and writes *without cause known to me*.

He adds the Japanese.

Adds the Portuguese.

Changes *all were tortured* to *many were tortured; I was tortured with water*.

The legal man says this weakens the statement.

Daniel looks at him.

Yes.

He changes *murdered* to *executed after the trial*.

The director says, You believe the trial lawful?

No.

Then why avoid the word?

Because I was there and I am tired of other men choosing the strongest word they can carry.

Nobody speaks for a while.

Daniel reaches the signature line.

He does not sign.

I will write my own.

The secretary says, We can take dictation.

No.

Daniel folds the prepared sheet once and returns it.

I have had enough men write while I speak.

He stands.

One director says, Mr Vale, the Dutch will publish their account whether you help us or not.

Daniel pauses at the door.

Then publish yours.

Without you?

Without owning me.

He leaves.

Tom is outside against the wall, eating an apple.

How long? Tom asks.

Daniel looks at the apple.

You waited?

Yes.

Why?

Food first.

Daniel laughs.

It comes out badly.

It is still a laugh.

* * *

## V. The True Copy

Daniel writes at the Pelican because he cannot write at home yet.

Home has a wife in it he has not seen for three years and a table where he once believed ordinary letters could be written.

The Pelican has noise.

Noise helps.

Arthur sends paper from Leadenhall without a note.

Tom recognises the hand on the parcel and says nothing.

Daniel writes slowly.

He begins with names.

Towerson.

The English men he knew.

The Japanese names Tom has brought west in a separate list, recovered in Batavia through Maria Mori without explanation Daniel asks for and Tom does not give.

The Portuguese man.

Daniel stops at each name long enough to remember whether he has anything true to put beside it.

Some get a sentence.

Some get only occupation or berth.

Some get nothing beyond the name.

Then Daniel writes what happened to him.

No nation described as naturally cruel.

No English innocence he cannot personally prove.

No Dutch innocence supplied out of fairness.

A cloth.

Water.

Questions.

A confession signed.

Towerson through the wall.

Transfer to Batavia.

His inability to explain why he lived.

At the end he writes:

*This is the part I saw and the part done to me. Where I do not know, I have written that I do not know.*

He signs.

Daniel Vale.

Then makes another copy.

Tom watches from the hearth.

Maria taught you that?

Daniel looks up.

The copies?

Yes.

No. Maria taught me to make three.

Tom almost smiles.

Daniel gives one to the Company.

One goes to his wife, if she will take it.

The third remains on Maggie's counter.

Daniel pushes it toward her.

I was told this house keeps things dry.

Maggie looks at Tom.

Maria Mori?

Tom nods.

Same woman?

Yes.

Maggie takes Daniel's page.

She does not read it at the bar.

She puts it into the fault beneath the old oak beside Matthew Bell's folded account.

That is all.

Daniel watches her hand return empty.

Good, he says.

* * *

## VI. Nell

Nell finds him two days later.

She has walked from the coaching inn because the driver wanted another penny for the river road and she had already paid him once.

She comes through the Pelican door with dust at the hem and stops when she sees Daniel.

He stands too quickly.

The stool falls.

Neither of them moves to pick it up.

Nell.

Daniel.

They look at one another.

Three years of letters not written stand between them more solidly than the table.

Daniel says, I did not know what to tell you.

Nell takes off her gloves.

You could have begun with alive.

I wasn't sure what that meant.

It meant I was waiting.

Daniel looks down.

I'm not the man who left.

No.

The answer reaches him harder than reassurance would have.

Nell steps closer.

Neither am I.

He looks at her.

You do not get to decide from Batavia that I would rather have the old one.

Daniel's hands begin shaking again.

Nell sees.

She does not take them.

Not yet.

Did they hurt you?

Yes.

Can you tell me?

Not all at once.

Then do not.

She sits.

Daniel remains standing.

Nell looks at the fallen stool.

Are you going to make me pick that up?

He bends for it.

When he straightens she has put one hand on the table between them.

Palm down.

Available.

Not demanded.

Daniel puts his hand beside hers.

Not on it.

Beside.

Maggie brings bread and cheese.

Nell looks up.

We did not order.

Food first, Maggie says.

Nell accepts this immediately.

Daniel's hand stops shaking before the meal is finished.

* * *

## VII. The Pint

Tom stays another week.

Not in the Pelican.

A lodging near the stairs.

He comes in each morning after Daniel and Nell have eaten and leaves when the room becomes crowded.

He does not ask Maggie about the thimble.

He does not tell her about the seven letters in his sea chest.

He does not make Daniel into proof of anything.

On the last evening Daniel and Nell have gone to Rochester.

Carter is at home.

Hendricks has not returned since the genever.

Joan is counting the till.

Tom sits at the short end of the bar with no cup in front of him.

Maggie comes over.

When do you sail?

Morning tide.

East?

For now.

Maggie looks at his hands.

Still reading everything before you sign?

Most things.

Most?

Tom looks at her.

Some men lie faster than I read.

She almost smiles.

Jack told me you hit a paymaster.

Held him against a wall.

Difference?

One leaves fewer witnesses impressed.

Maggie reaches beneath the bar.

Tom hears the scrape.

The brass thimble appears in her palm.

She puts it on the counter.

Tom does not touch it at first.

Till I'm back, he says.

Maggie looks at him.

You were back in 1612.

Tom's eyes lower.

Yes.

She turns the thimble once with one finger.

Then?

Tom considers the question.

Then I was angry you could see the part I was hiding from myself.

Maggie says, I was angry enough to see parts that were not there.

Tom looks up.

That is as close as either of them comes.

Maggie pushes the thimble toward him.

Tom puts one finger through it.

It no longer reaches the first joint.

He takes his hand away.

Leave it, he says.

Maggie returns it beneath the bar.

Then she takes a clean pint cup.

Fills it.

Sets it in front of him.

Tom looks at the ale.

No pie?

You had pie six days running.

I was making up fourteen years.

You were eating my stock.

Tom lifts the pint.

Maggie picks up her rag.

He drinks.

Nothing is settled completely.

Nothing needs to be said about that.
