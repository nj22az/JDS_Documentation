# The Terror (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"Nobody says the word much — Ripper — as though naming it summons it, but it sits in the room anyway."*
>
> *"A monster's a monster in every language, and I never once met a language that had the monopoly on making one."*
>
> — a Prospect regular, Mutiny veteran, unnamed

## I. The Watch Committees

The boots pick her up at the corner of Glamis Road, and for a hundred yards Su Zhang does the arithmetic every woman in the East End has learned to do by the middle of that autumn: two of them, heavy, keeping pace, not closing — yet.

The basket is fourteen pounds of clean linen, still warm from the pressing, breathing soap and hot iron into the cold, and her arms know to the ounce what they can do with it. The dock road is empty where it is never empty. She shifts the basket to her hip, frees her right hand, and does not walk faster, because running tells a thing it is prey.

She has done this arithmetic before this autumn, in smaller ways, her whole life — a girl alone on a dock road learns early which of the world's noises are weather and which are attention — but the arithmetic has changed its character since the end of August, the way weather changes character before a storm without any single cloud you could point to and blame. It used to be background, something her body did without troubling her mind for a report. Now it is foreground. Now she is aware, with a clarity that has nothing pleasant in it, of exactly what she is deciding as she walks, and exactly how quickly she could stop deciding and start acting if the two sets of boots behind her closed the gap they are, for the moment, choosing not to close.

Then a rattle goes off behind her like a wooden thunderclap, and a lantern swings up, and a voice that has been shouting at foreigners all autumn asks her — not unkindly, which is somehow worse — whose girl she is and where she is bound with that basket.

Watch committee. Tradesmen with cudgels, out in twos, doing after dark the work Scotland Yard is failing, in the opinion of every public house and every newspaper column on the reach, to do fast enough for anyone's comfort. They see her home to the end of her own street, proud of themselves, two men guarding a laundry girl from the thing the whole city is currently dreaming about whether it wants to or not, and Su thanks them in her politest English — the careful English, the one that says thank you and does not say anything else — and does not tell them that for a hundred yards of the dock road she had been deciding, quite calmly, in the animal part of her mind that does such deciding without consulting the rest of her, which of the two of them would need putting down first if the arithmetic ever came out wrong.

That is what the autumn has done. To everyone.

By the middle of September the East End has learned a new gait, and wears it everywhere: nobody walks home alone after dark who can help it, and whoever must, walks fast, in the middle of the street where the gas lamps can still reach, one hand near a pocket and whatever the pocket holds. There have been two deaths since the end of August, each of them worse in the telling than the one before — the newspapers gesture at what was done and are careful, even now, not to be indecent about the gesturing, which somehow makes the gesturing worse than plain speech would have been, because a reader's own imagination is always crueller than any single fact a paper is willing to print. Whitechapel is the better part of a mile off from the Causeway. Fear does not respect a parish boundary any more than the river does, and every woman working the dock roads between here and there has started carrying something sharp of her own, tucked where a hand can find it without a stranger seeing the hand go there.

The Prospect keeps its lamps burning a little longer than the season used to require, and the room smells of wet wool and gin and too many men who are, for one reason or another, in no hurry to go home to an empty house. They drink faster than they did in July. They leave in groups that did not used to need to be groups. Nobody says the word much — Ripper, the papers have decided to call it, though the name will not properly stick to the season for another few weeks yet — as though naming the thing outright would be an invitation, a summons, a door left ajar on purpose. But it sits in the room all the same, in the way every man's eye goes to the door each time it opens, and in the fractional pause before anyone answers a stranger's question, and in the fact that Flo has taken, without comment, to locking the till the moment the second lamp is lit rather than waiting for closing as she always used to.

In the corner, the chair with the too-short back leg sits empty tonight, as it usually does — propped level with its two-hundred-year playbill, left for last the way it has always been left for last, nobody left alive who could tell you why, the regulars simply knowing to leave it. It has sat empty through worse than this, presumably, in three centuries of a house standing where the Wall meets the water. Su finds herself glancing at it anyway, this autumn, the way you glance at an old scar on a day when the weather has turned — not because it hurts, but because the weather has reminded the body it is there.

Su settles her chit with Flo and takes up the empty basket, and Dr. Cray, on his stool at the end of the bar, asks after her mother's chest with the same unhurried courtesy he has asked after it every autumn for fifteen years, and she gives him the same answer she always gives, and there is nothing in the exchange, tonight, that a stranger walking in off the street would find remarkable in the smallest degree.

That is the particular cruelty of an ordinary autumn learning to be a frightened one: it does not announce which of its familiar rooms has changed underneath its familiar furniture. It only waits, and goes on pouring ale, and lets a girl walk home past two well-meaning men with cudgels who have no idea, any more than she does yet, how close any of them are standing to the thing the whole city has started, without quite saying so aloud, to dream about.

## II. Leather Apron

The first one comes at the very end of August, and for the first week it is only a name in a stop-press column, a woman found in Bucks Row with her throat opened, and the East End reads it the way it reads any bad thing that has happened to somebody it does not personally know: with the particular blend of horror and distance that lets a person finish their tea before finishing the paragraph.

It does not stay distant.

By the second week of September there is a second woman, found off Hanbury Street this time, and this one the papers cannot manage with distance at all, because whatever was done to her was done with a thoroughness that makes the first killing read, in hindsight, almost gentle by comparison — and the papers, who have discovered by now that horror sells better dressed in restraint than horror dressed in plain description, begin printing the sort of sentence that says everything by refusing to say anything: *injuries of a character it is unnecessary to describe.* Su reads that sentence over a carman's shoulder in the Prospect and understands, the way you understand a wrongly tied knot before you can say which strand is at fault, that the restraint is not mercy. It is appetite wearing mercy's coat, because a reader's own imagination, given that much room to work in, will always supply something worse than the plain fact could have managed on its own, and the papers know it, and print the gap on purpose.

A name starts moving through the streets before the second week is out: Leather Apron. Nobody Su asks can say for certain where it began or whether there is a real man behind it — a local character, a bootmaker, somebody's half-remembered lodger — but the name has the particular stickiness of a thing that answers a fear people would otherwise have to carry shapeless, and once a fear has a name, however invented, it is very much harder to talk anyone out of hunting the name down.

The shop feels it before the Prospect does, because the shop is further from the money and closer to the fear.

A woman who has brought her washing to the Zhangs for six years stops on the step one Tuesday, parcel still under her arm, and asks Su's mother, not unkindly, whether it mightn't be safer for a body to take her custom somewhere else for a while, until things have settled. Sau-Ling takes the question the way she takes everything, without heat, and says only that the shop's water boils the same for everyone's linen regardless of who is doing the murdering three streets off, and the woman, embarrassed now that it has been said back to her so plainly, leaves her washing after all, and comes the following Tuesday too, and the one after that, because embarrassment, in the end, is a weaker tide than habit.

Su hears the second killing discussed at the Prospect the same evening it becomes known, in the particular tone a room takes on when everyone in it wants to talk about a thing and is frightened, underneath the wanting, of what talking about it might summon.

A stevedore says he heard the woman was cut nearly in two. Another corrects him — no, that's the next one, wait and see, this is only the second — with the terrible matter-of-fact certainty of a man already numbering an event that has not yet happened, as though the numbering itself were a kind of grim comfort, a way of getting ahead of a thing too large to meet unprepared. A third says his sister-in-law has stopped taking in washing after dark on principle now, whatever the water costs to heat twice. Dr. Cray, on his stool, asks no questions and offers no theories, which Su notices only because it is unlike him — he is a man who likes a good puzzle, has said as much more than once over a chess problem some regular or other brings in — and yet on this one subject, this autumn, he sits with his brandy and lets the room construct its terrors around him without once adding a brick of his own.

She thinks nothing of it. There is nothing yet to think.

By the middle of September the shape of the fear has settled into a shape the whole reach recognises without anyone needing to draw it: two women dead, both of a kind the papers call unfortunates without quite explaining to a genteel reader what the word means, both killed at night, both in streets no better lit than any other street in Whitechapel, which is to say not lit at all past the second lamp from any corner. The watch committees have begun in earnest. Men who have never in their lives carried anything heavier than a ledger are carrying cudgels now, out in twos, patrolling roads they would ordinarily have crossed the river to avoid at that hour, because fear, it turns out, makes brave men of the merely frightened just as readily as it makes cowards of them, and nobody yet knows which kind of man the autumn is going to need.

Su walks home each of those evenings with the basket on her hip and the arithmetic running underneath everything else she is doing, and twice already the watch has stopped her, kindly, and seen her the last stretch of the way, and both times she has thanked them in her careful English and said nothing about what she had already decided to do with either of them had the kindness turned out, at the last second, to be something else.

Elisabeth still comes on her Mondays. Catherine still sings her fronts of songs and promises the backs of them from Kent. Neither of them has yet been touched by any of it, not directly, not by name — they are, to Su, still only the two customers she is fondest of, moving through a city that has grown strange and watchful around them the way weather grows strange before a storm nobody has yet agreed to call a storm.

The thirtieth of September is two weeks off, and nothing in the shop, or the yard, or the long easy evenings at the Prospect, gives any sign at all of what that particular night is going to take.

## III. The Hands in the Doorway

The word Leather Apron does something to the East End that the word Ripper, when it finally arrives, will only make worse: it gives the fear a face, and having no actual face to put to it, the fear goes looking for one among the faces the street was already a little afraid of before any of this began.

It is not the Zhangs' street first. It is a Polish Jew's, two neighbourhoods over, a bootmaker with a temper and a habit of shouting at his wife loudly enough that the whole court knows the sound of it — a man, in other words, whom a frightened street can point to and say, there, that one, that is the kind of man who does a thing like this, without needing to trouble itself over whether he has ever in his life laid a hand on anyone but his own household. He is walked into a police station bleeding above one eye within the week, for the crime, as far as Su can make out from what reaches the Prospect, of resembling somebody's idea of somebody.

It reaches the Causeway inside a fortnight.

Nothing so organised as a mob, not yet, not at their end of the docks — but a change in the weather that Su can read the way she reads a tide, from small signs long before the water itself arrives. A costermonger who has sold vegetables at the corner of the Causeway for as long as Su can remember stops nodding to her father in the mornings. A group of dockworkers, waiting on a tide, spend an idle half-hour outside a Chinese lodging-house loudly discussing, to nobody in particular and everybody in earshot, the sort of men who come from foreign parts and keep to themselves and who's to say what such men get up to behind their own shut doors. Windows along Pennyfields begin coming down at dusk instead of after full dark, shutters going up early on houses that have never in Su's lifetime troubled to shutter before midnight.

Her father starts standing in the shop doorway at closing.

He does not announce that he is doing it, and if Su asked him outright why, he would very likely answer with something about the evening air, or the state of the step, or some other plain fact that happened also to be true and also to be entirely beside the point. But she watches him do it three evenings running before she understands what she is watching: Wei Zhang, at the hour the shop would ordinarily be bolted and dark, standing instead in his own open doorway with the lamp behind him and both hands folded together in front of him, visible, still, unmistakably a man doing nothing with his hands but holding them where the street can see them doing nothing.

It is, Su realises, the practice turned to a use she has never once seen her father put it to. Not a stance. Not a form. A pure, deliberate absence of threat, offered to a street that has started looking at foreign faces and deciding, on no evidence beyond the foreignness, that violence might live behind them. Her father has spent his whole life carrying, in his own body, the one thing this particular fear is hunting for, and he has answered it the only way the rules permit: not by hiding what he is, which he could not do even if he wished to, and not by explaining himself, which nobody hunting a monster is in the mood to listen to — but by standing in the light, hands empty and folded, for as long as the street cares to look, until looking at him has become so boring, so plainly unrewarding, that the street's attention moves on to find its monster somewhere else.

It costs him something to do it. Su can see that too, though he would never say so. A man who has spent forty years being underestimated by larger men and turning the underestimating to his own account is not built to stand still and be looked at; every year in the yard has trained the opposite instinct, has trained him to be the one thing in the room a careless eye passes over. Standing in his own doorway, deliberately visible, deliberately unthreatening, asks him to hold himself directly against the grain of everything the practice ever taught him, and he does it anyway, every evening, because the rules that stop a fight in an alley are, it turns out, the same rules that can be made to stand quietly in a doorway and let a frightened street satisfy itself there is nothing here worth its fear.

Never first, Su thinks, watching him from behind the counter. Never for show. She understands, watching, that her father has found a fourth use for a rule built for three, and has not asked anyone's permission to do it.

Sau-Ling does her own version of the same work in a different register entirely. She has always gone to the mission church on a Sunday and always will, but this September she goes early, and stays late, and makes a point, in her furred, twenty-years-borrowed English, of being seen and heard thanking the minister loudly for his sermon, of asking after the health of every English neighbour in the pew, of being, in short, so entirely and visibly a Christian woman of the parish that a stranger would have to work very hard indeed to file her anywhere else. Su understands this for what it is too: her mother's own doorway, held the same way her father holds his, in a different coin.

## IV. A Monster in Every Language

The dockworker who fought in the Mutiny has been drinking at the Prospect since before Carter had a shop of his own, and is not, by his own account, a man given to talking about the thing that put the grey in his beard early. But he comes in one evening in the middle of that September, in the thick of the foreigner-hunting, and says, to nobody and everybody, that he doesn't hold with any of it. Never has.

Man's a monster or he isn't, he says. Don't much matter what god he prays to or what his mother called him. A monster's a monster in every language, and I never once met a language that had the monopoly on making one.

Flo pours him an extra half without being asked, and it is the nearest thing to a public defence the Zhangs receive that autumn, and neither the dockworker nor Flo ever mentions it again, and Su never thanks either of them for it directly, because there is no way to thank a person for a kindness without naming, out loud, the thing the kindness was defending against, and naming it would cost more than the thanks could ever repay.

But the dockworker, once started, does not entirely stop, and Su — settling a chit at the far end of the bar, in no hurry to leave — hears the rest of it come out of him sideways, the way the truest things in that room always come out: not offered, only allowed to happen, because somebody has finally asked the one plain question nobody else that autumn has thought to ask him.

A young waterman, new enough to the Prospect not to know better, asks him straight out whether he killed men himself, out there, in the Mutiny.

Some, the dockworker says. Not so many as the men who wrote home about it would have you think. And I watched a great many more men die of the doctoring than the fighting, which is a thing nobody puts in the songs.

He was nineteen, he says, a private soldier at Cawnpore and after, and he learned there, in a summer he does not describe in any detail the room can afterward repeat, that an army carries two kinds of men in it who both call themselves surgeons. There are the ones the fighting breaks — he has seen those, seen a good doctor's hands start shaking at the wrist after a bad month and never properly stop, seen a steady man go to drink because steadiness had turned out, in the end, to be a debt collected from somewhere he could not afford to keep paying it. And there are the other kind. The kind the fighting does not touch at all. He watched a regimental surgeon — an educated man, a gentleman, kind enough by every account the mess ever gave of him — sign the same three words onto paper after paper, morning after morning, for men flogged near to death on an officer's temper and men who had simply worn out under the heat and the marching, and the three words were always the same three words, and the surgeon's hand, writing them, never once shook.

Died of fever, the dockworker says. That's what went in the book. Every time. A man beaten half to death on the triangle and dead by evening, and the book says fever, and the surgeon signs it clean, because fever is a word nobody upstairs ever troubles to argue with, and a word nobody argues with is the only word a busy man needs.

I asked him once, the dockworker says — the one time in that whole summer I was fool enough to ask an officer anything — how a flogging comes to read as a fever in a doctor's own book. And he looked at me the way you'd look at a man asking why water is wet. He said, in effect, that the fever was real enough, that a body under that much strain will find a fever from somewhere to die of, and that the flogging was merely the occasion and not, properly speaking, the cause. Very reasonable, the way he said it. Very calm. I have met calmer men only twice since, and I did not trust either of them either.

The young waterman asks what became of the surgeon.

Retired on his pension same as any of them, the dockworker says. Wrote a paper, I heard, on the diseases of hot climates. Kept his hands steady his whole life, I'd wager, because a hand only shakes when the thing it's doing costs the man something, and it never once cost him anything at all.

He finishes his half and does not say Cray's name, and does not need to, and does not know — because nobody in the Prospect that autumn knows, least of all Su, not yet, not for four more months — that he has just handed the one fact across the bar that will matter more than any other fact in the whole of this story: that a man may be entirely and reliably kind, may set a shoulder gently and climb two flights for nothing and remember every barmaid's name, and be, underneath all of it, precisely the kind of calm the dockworker has just described — the calm that costs the calm man nothing, because the calm man has simply never once let the thing in front of him become his own.

Su files it the way she files everything, without yet knowing the drawer. She will find the drawer in November, in a fog, and when she does, this exact evening — the extra half, the young waterman's plain question, the three words that never made a surgeon's hand shake — will be the first thing to come back to her, whole, and she will understand that the dockworker gave her the truth about Dr. Cray a full two months before she had any reason to go looking for it, simply by telling the truth about a different calm man, in a different country, in a war that had nothing at all to do with Wapping except that it had, in the end, everything to do with it.

Her father is in the doorway again the following evening, hands folded, lamp behind him, waiting for the street to grow bored of looking. It does, eventually, most nights. Su has begun to understand, watching him, that this is what the rules are actually for, underneath the alley and the wrist-locks and the fan raised against a lazy elbow: not the ability to end a fight quickly, which is only ever the last and smallest part of it, but the much harder, much longer discipline of living every ordinary day in a body the world has decided, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything you have actually done, to be a little afraid of — and doing it, every single evening, without once giving the fear anything real to feed on.
