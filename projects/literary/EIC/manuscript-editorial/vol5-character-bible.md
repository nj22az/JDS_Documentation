# The Watchman's Daughter — a character ledger (1888)

This ledger tells Su Zhang's book out of school. It keeps the names the
Prospect remembered from one terrible autumn, and the customers the laundry
knew before the newspapers made them a list, then follows the people out of the
room and down the years. Most of what follows will be on the page; what is not
yet written, this ledger knows anyway. Read it the way you read the last page of
a mystery, or the way the river reads a drowned man: afterwards, and without
being asked.

Volume Five is the cycle's deliberate break. The East India Company is thirty
years dead; it does not act here. It only *echoes* — in the surgeon its wars
taught to be calm, and in the girl its opium war washed out of Canton two
generations back. The book follows Su, not the tavern's ability to receive
testimony, and so is free to leave the Prospect for the Causeway, Whitechapel
and the fog. The Prospect remains her home world. It is not her whole world.

***

## The two engines

The spine of Volume Five is two lines that never quite meet, and the girl who
stands where they cross.

**The question that never closes.** Dr. Reginald Cray is either the season's
monster or only a monster. Su drives him into the winter river on the last third
of a November night, and takes her hand off the clasp of his case rather than
learn which he was, and sinks the one object that could have answered her,
unopened, weighted with the shop's chain. The book keeps her choice: *it fits,
and it does not prove*, forever. The reader is never told, because Su is never
told, because the river is never asked a question it will answer. This is the
volume's held breath, and nothing — no later chapter, no confession, no entry
into Cray's own head — is ever permitted to let it out.

**The lineage that becomes hers.** Grandfather to Wei to Su: a family art carried
out of a burning empire and kept alive in a wash-yard at the far end of the
world. Su begins the book her father's student and ends it a master in her own
right — the art tested in earnest, the fan passed down, the form taught at last
to the next pair of small hands. The watchman's daughter becomes the watchman.
The killing in the fog is the hinge: it is the day the art becomes hers, and the
day she learns to the inch the difference between stopping a thing and avenging
it, which is the width of a knife, and which she spends the rest of her life
standing on the right side of.

## The Zhang household

**Su Zhang**, nineteen. Born in Limehouse ~1869; the watchman's daughter and the
book's whole eye. Trained since seven, at dawn, in the family's Southern kung fu
— **Hung Kuen** for the hard work, **Wing Chun** for the close — and in its four
rules. She reads people the way she reads a wrongly tied knot: the face lies, the
shoulders confess. She keeps two languages in two rooms and does not let them
run together; she keeps an interior tally, in a script only she can read, made
of her mother's open ledger and her father's closing one at once. She has used
the art in earnest twice before the book begins — at fourteen on three dock boys
who thought a Chinese girl with a basket was sport, at seventeen on a man who
would not take his hand off her at closing — and both times her father's answer
was no praise and no comfort, only the rules recited in both languages and half
an hour's stance-work in the cold, which was how a watchman said *I am proud of
you and it frightens me*. In 1888 she loses two customers to the list, drives
their possible killer into the Thames, tells no one, ever — not Flo, not her
mother, and above all not her father, *because he would believe her* — and comes
out the far side of the autumn carrying a story that holds her up and can never
be told: her own *Princess Alice*. She marries Erik Johansson ~1892 and inherits
the yard. She never learns whether Cray was the one. She learns to live at a loss
anyway.

**Wei Zhang**, her father; a small man forty years underestimated by larger ones
and never once correcting them. Born in Canton, raised in Hong Kong behind a
ship-chandler's, where he learned two trades at once — the art before dawn, rope
and tar and lamp-oil the rest of the day. Crossed to London in steerage with one
bundle after his own father died and the colony took the yard. A **dit da** man:
the hitting and the mending kept in the same two hands, master and bonesetter
both. He keeps no God and every rule, and closes his books to the farthing, and
loves his heretic wife in the way of two people who have found the one other
person whose wrongness they can live inside. This autumn his hands begin to fail
him — the cold finding the old breaks his own father set — and Su reads it out of
his grip a half-beat before he will admit it, and grieves it in a language that
needs neither of hers. He tells the story of his crossing perhaps twice a year;
it is the only story in which he is a fool from start to finish, and he tells it
with relish, because it holds rule three. He never asks Su what she carried out
of the fog. On the first morning after, in the yard, he reads her through two
crossed wrists, and all he says is: *your weight is back on your heels — fix it.*
And she fixes it. That is how the two of them carry a thing up a long flight of
years — one landing at a time, without once naming what it weighs.

**Sau-Ling Zhang**, her mother; sent for across the whole earth the year the shop
could feed two, and delivered like freight to a dock and a small serious man she
had met twice. Poor in two countries; buried two sisters and a son. Dipped at the
mission church by the docks, she took the English God practically, at a fair
rate, with strong private opinions about how His work ought to be done — and
keeps beneath the borrowed faith a heresy of her own, spoken aloud exactly once
and true as a plumb line: that a God worth the walk to church runs His books at a
loss, on purpose, forever, the way a decent laundress takes in the drowned man's
shirt and the never-paid woman's Sunday bodice. *Grief in Shadwell is a luxury
taken in instalments*; she calls weeping a Tuesday and finishes the pressing.
Her single largest act in the book is wordless: on the morning after the fog she
is already up at the copper, and takes the bloodied sleeve out of her daughter's
arms without meeting her eyes and boils it *past testimony*, because blood is
only one more stain to a woman who has lifted out every kind there is — and
enters the largest sum of both their lives in a book Su was never taught to read,
and leaves the column open, at a loss, on purpose, because that is the only kind
of book she believes a God worth having would keep.

**Lee Zhang**, the brother; born when Su was four, dead at seven, one winter of
his own dawn mornings behind him and his stance still all elbows. He took a fever;
Wei went himself through the sleet at ten at night and was told, through the
housekeeper, that the doctor was at his supper and would call in the morning. Cray
called in the morning, unhurried, looked at the boy the length of a pot of tea,
took his fee from Sau-Ling's hand — *counted it*; that is the part she cannot
forgive, eight winters on — wrote *fever* in his register, which was true as far
as it went, and left. Lee died on the third night. It was not murder, nor even
neglect as the parish counts it. It was a man deciding, behind his eyes, at a
street door, what a chandler's boy off the Causeway was worth getting cold for.
The same man climbed two flights twice for the potboy and charged nothing. Both
facts are him; in eight years neither has ever cancelled the other. Lee is the
entry Su scratches out first, every time, because his debt is real and owed and
exactly the kind the rules forbid her to collect.

**The grandfather** (unnamed in life, present in the telling); a master in Canton
— not a rich man, a man other men came to, which on his street was better than
rich. He kept his school until the English gunships came up the Pearl River and
the war made the city no place for teaching boys; then moved the family to the
raw new colony at Hong Kong and began again behind a chandler's on the
waterfront. He was the lane's master and the lane's bonesetter both; the plain
wooden **fan** is his, and splinted the very wrists it broke. He died on the
hillside above the harbour and was buried there. His words, worn smooth by two
crossings of the world, are the ones Wei recites into the dark and Su gives back
in English.

## The Prospect and the river

**Dr. Reginald Cray**, about sixty-one; a fixture at the Prospect for some fifteen
years, since he retired from the East India Company's medical service and took
rooms nearby on a Leadenhall Street pension for decades of service nobody asks
too closely about. A surgeon in Bengal in the fifties, before the Mutiny, before
the Crown took what the Company built. Two brandies, same stool, no wasted motion;
good with his hands the way old surgeons are — he sets a docker's shoulder on the
bar floor with a level *nothing* on his face that twelve-year-old Su files without
yet knowing the drawer. He carries a good leather case that never leaves his side,
and once, when its clasp comes loose, it holds no bandages and no vials — something
long, wrapped in oilcloth, the case's full length. He is also, and this is the
part that will not simplify, **kind** — or something that photographs identically
to kindness. Whether he is the season's monster or only November's, Su never
learns and the reader never learns; he goes into the winter ebb with a question
unfinished on his face, *as though a calculation had come out wrong and he wanted,
professionally, to know where*. Reported missing by his housekeeper within the
week. Nobody looks very hard. He and Su are the Company's two long echoes met in
one room — the surgeon its wars taught to be calm, the girl its opium war washed
out of Canton — and the machine has been dead thirty years, and its consequences
are only now reaching the bar.

**Elisabeth** (to the streets and the papers, **Long Liz** — the real Elizabeth
Stride; the book keeps that frame in its Author's Note and character-map, but Su
never does). To Su she is only **Elisabeth**: a tall Swede with a soft accent she
can put on or take off like a bonnet, a laundry customer on Mondays when she
comes. Su knows the day side of her and no other — never the street name, never
what she did after dark to earn the farthings, never that she walked the very
dark the whole city feared. She learns it only from the murder column, too late. She crossed
her own ocean as Wei crossed his and remade herself in English, and knows it; once
she and Su spent a quarter of an hour at the counter comparing the words they
still could not feel anything in — *grief in English is only a noise; in Swedish it
has a floor and walls* — and were, briefly and exactly, understood, which did not
happen to Su often in either language. Liz tells everyone she lost her husband and
two children when the *Princess Alice* went down; Su worked out years ago the story
isn't true, or isn't hers, and never said so, because some stories are
load-bearing. She becomes the third name on the list on the thirtieth of September,
found near Berner Street with her throat cut and nothing else done, as though
something interrupted.

**Catherine** (to the streets and the papers, **Kate** — the real Catherine
Eddowes; frame kept in the apparatus, never in Su's mouth). To Su she is
**Catherine**: small, quick, Midlands-voiced, cheerful the way a kettle is
cheerful, loudest when things are hottest. She calls Su *duchess* for no
reason anyone remembers and sings while she waits for her mending — halves of
songs, whatever is already in the room, and one slow one about a girl waiting on a
quay that she only ever has the front of. She promises the hop gardens will give
her the whole of it, and it will all come out at last. She comes home from Kent
early, the hopping gone wrong, broke as ever; becomes the fourth name the same
night, three-quarters of a mile on, in Mitre Square, where nothing interrupted
anything. Her bodice, taken in at the seams, wrapped in paper, paid for and never
collected, stays in the shop's back room for years, because neither Su nor her
mother can bring themselves to unpick the seams and use the cloth. Some accounts
are kept open on purpose.

**Flo**, a Prospect barmaid; the one who, that winter, presses on Su a rooted
cutting of the house's crimson flower — the dancer that has hung at the Prospect's
window since before either of them was born, split by the bar's keepers for a
hundred years, *roots easy, mind it in frost*. A gift with no questions in it,
which is that winter the only kind Su can take. It goes in the chandlery window
between the lamps and blooms the next summer like a small unreasonable argument for
the world.

**The dockworker** (a Prospect regular, unnamed); a Mutiny veteran who doesn't love
being asked, and who tells Su the one thing she needs: that the Company's surgeons
saw men flogged to death for looking at an officer wrong and were expected to
certify the cause as *fever*, and did, and were paid on time for it — and that he
wouldn't trust a Company surgeon's calm as far as he could throw him, *not the calm
ones especially*, because the ones who came back rattled at least still had
something in them that flinched. He hands her Cray's shadow without knowing he has
done it.

**Erik Johansson**; a big, unhurried man off the timber ships, come up the shop
steps four years after the autumn with a sea-bag of salt-stiff shirts and a name
with soft corners in it. His father was Swedish, out of a fishing village on the
coast outside Stockholm; his mother was born on a Trinidad sugar estate to a family
shipped out of Calcutta under indenture in the years after the Company died — the
machine had changed its name by then, but the ships had not changed their business.
He asks Su almost nothing, ever, which is not incuriosity but seamanship: a man
long enough on the water knows everyone comes off it with cargo stowed below the
line. Su reads him at a glance — the trim of a man carrying something lashed down
and riding quiet — and marries into a language with floors and walls, and finds, on
slack-tide Sundays out on the river, an hour with nothing owing in it either
direction. Through him the volume ties, quietly, into the cycle's blood: the same
machine that moved the Zhangs moved the Johanssons, and never once entered either
house in its books except as freight.

*(Minor, and load-bearing: the **potboy** Cray climbed two flights for, twice, and
charged nothing; the **boarding-house keeper's wife** on the Causeway; the
**tea-stall woman** in Canton who filled the white bowl and said* bring it back
yourself *; the **boy who said Run** on the Hong Kong waterfront, thirteen, a
ledger under one arm, who read Wei boots-to-bundle and settled his passage in a
column Wei could not see.)*

## The object-language

Volume Five, like Book One, keeps its weight in things, not speeches.

- **The fan** — plain folded wood, three generations smooth; correction, weapon
  and bonesetter's fulcrum in one. In the alley it is applied the one way the
  grandfather never applied it. By the coda it is Su's to carry and Su's to pass
  on: the lineage token.
- **The white bowl** — a working rice bowl on the high shelf, never used, never
  sold, dusted by no one but Wei: the debt he was *not allowed to pay* (bring it
  back, not send it), kept open on purpose, forever. The oldest rule, the one the
  four come out of.
- **Kate's bodice** — paid for, never collected, kept in its paper for years and
  carried to Su's own house when the shop changes hands: an account kept open on
  purpose; the only entry both women can read and neither will say aloud.
- **The crimson flower** — the Prospect's hundred-year window plant, cut for Su by
  Flo; the cycle's living thread, blooming in a Chinese chandler's window the
  summer after the worst winter.
- **Cray's case** — the oilcloth shape; the one object that could answer the
  question; given to the Thames unopened, *the only language the river speaks,
  which has no word for yes and no word for no.*
- **The river** — took six hundred off Woolwich on the *Princess Alice* and did
  not pause; takes Cray and keeps his ripple; moved both the Zhangs and the
  Johanssons across the world as freight. It keeps everything fed to it and has
  never once been asked what it holds, and has never once answered.

***

## The four rules

Recited in Cantonese, given back in English, because a rule known in only one
language is only half known:

> **Never first. Never for show. Never for payment. Never in anger.**

*The practice stops things. It does not answer them.* The book is the story of a
girl keeping all four through an autumn built to break every one of them — and
keeping them, at the last, by a hair, in the fog, with her hand coming off a
clasp.

***

## Reader's note on the history

Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes were real women, murdered on 30 September
1888. This volume treats them as people — customers, a song, a bodice never
collected — and keeps the murders themselves at the edge of report, refusing what
the newspapers did. **A point of view, held strictly:** the reader-facing
apparatus (Author's Note, character-map, illustration captions) keeps the names
history knows them by, *Long Liz* and *Kate*; but the narrative is Su's, and Su
knew only the day side of them, under their given names — **Elisabeth** and
**Catherine** — and never knew they were prostitutes. She learns *what* they were
only from the murder column, in the same flat print that tells her they are dead.
That gap between what the city knew and what Su knew is the chapter's grief, and
it is never closed. The resolution (Cray, the fog, the river) is **invented**, and
the book knows it: no real person is accused, and the true case is left exactly as
history left it — open. That openness is the point. It is Sau-Ling's kind of
ledger, and the river's, and Su's.
