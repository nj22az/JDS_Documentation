## III. The Voice

There are kinder men aboard than Tom.

The cook gives Jack the burnt bottom of the porridge pot. The old quartermaster teaches him to judge speed from the bubbles passing the hull. The sailmaker lets him sleep in the loft one wet afternoon and pretends not to see him.

None of them keeps him alive as often.

Tom's voice stops Jack before a block drops where his head is going. It sends him across the deck before green water comes over the waist. It tells him when to flatten, when to climb and when the familiar line in his hand has become the wrong line because darkness has altered everything around it.

It is not a voice kept for Jack. It works the whole ship.

Men hate it, obey it and repeat it in smaller voices down the watches. Tom sees a bad knot from twenty feet. He can wake beneath a coat and know from the pitch of canvas that a sheet has begun to run.

He can also make a grown man feel six years old for coiling against the lay.

When Pritchard drops a marline spike from aloft and misses Jack's head by the width of one hand, Tom has him at the mast before the spike stops rolling. He does not strike him. He makes Pritchard stand Jack's next two watches and carry Jack's work as well as his own.

Pritchard takes the punishment out on Jack in words.

Boatswain's little debt, he calls him. Two shillings buys a loud father.

Jack has not told anybody about the allowance.

He finds out how Pritchard knows at the next ration issue. The purser's book lies open beside the cask. Beside Thomas Fletcher is the monthly two-shilling deduction. Beside Jack Mercer is the assigned half wage.

A ship has little privacy from the men who keep its books.

The assistant calls ten mouths for Jack's mess.

Jack counts eleven.

Again, Tom says.

Eleven.

Ten on the mess, the assistant says.

Tom turns the wooden tally over and puts one black thumbnail against the final cut.

That is eleven.

That is the boy. Half wage.

The wage book pays Carter, Tom says. The victual book feeds Jack. Unless the Company has found a way for half a boy to do a whole watch, weigh eleven.

The assistant looks toward the purser, who is occupied with a cheese that has become liquid inside its rind. He adds the biscuit.

Jack carries the share away.

Does half wage mean half everything?

It means half your wage.

And the two shillings?

Mine.

The food?

The ship's expense. It must keep alive what it intends to use.

Jack likes the answer less than kindness and trusts it more.

The beer is brown and sour enough to carry threads. Pritchard calls it rope with ambitions. The water is worse: wet-cellar smell, black flecks and the occasional soft thing nobody examines.

Jack softens biscuit in broth and watches three beetles rise to the surface.

Pritchard leans over his cup.

You have more meat than the rest of us.

Jack lifts one beetle with the spoon and drops it into Pritchard's drink.

Half wages, he says. Full meat.

The men nearest them laugh.

Pritchard studies the beetle, then drinks without removing it.

Waste nothing.

This time Jack laughs with the others instead of after them.

Tom makes him count the water before he eats.

Ten measures come full. The eleventh ends with the dipper knocking wetly against the bottom of the cask.

Put the shortage in the book, Tom says.

The cask is empty, the assistant says.

That is the shortage.

It issued full when opened.

It does not issue full now.

Evaporation. Leakage. Bad cooperage.

Tom waits.

Waiting is the quiet form of his voice.

The assistant writes the shortage.

Jack watches the pen.

What happens if he doesn't?

At the next reckoning the book has more water than the hold.

And then?

The book wins until men become too thirsty to agree.

Tom tips half his own cup into Jack's.

I have mine, Jack says.

You counted yours. Drink.

After rations comes the ordinary work that makes one day indistinguishable from the next until something breaks.

Pump. Scrub. Coil. Tar. Haul. Eat. Sleep if the ship allows it.

Pritchard ties a twist of oakum to the back of Jack's breeches while they scrub the deck. Jack discovers the tail by its shadow and leaves it there until he can transfer it to Pritchard without being seen. The old quartermaster asks whether London boys grow that way or only shipboard ones.

Only the handsome ones, Jack says.

Two minutes later he asks Pritchard the same question.

Pritchard finds the oakum at his own belt and bows.

Improved by sea air.

Even Tom's mouth moves at one corner.

The joke does not erase the marline spike or the debt. It only means Jack has become a person the mess answers back.

Later Tom sends him into the weather shrouds with spun yarn and a serving mallet. A short length has begun chafing where rope works against rope.

Replace it? Jack asks.

With what?

New rope.

And when the store is empty?

Jack looks east, where the sea has no warehouse in it.

Tom puts the yarn in his hand.

Preserve first. Replace when preservation fails.

Jack begins badly, then less badly. On the fourth attempt the turns sit hard against one another.

Find the join, Tom says.

Jack runs one tarred finger along the serving.

There.

Better.

It is the smallest word Tom owns that can mean praise.

Near noon the heat thickens. The deck seams soften under bare feet. The bell goes. Men crowd round the last thin barley broth before the next order can claim their hands.

One sailor does not eat.

He sits in the full sun with both arms folded over his chest. His biscuit remains beside the cup, broth soaking halfway through it.

Jack points.

If you don't want that, Pritchard will eat it and the beetles.

The man looks at him without understanding.

His face is grey beneath the heat.

Cold, he says. God help me, boy. I am cold.

Jack looks at the sun.

Tom looks at the untouched biscuit.
