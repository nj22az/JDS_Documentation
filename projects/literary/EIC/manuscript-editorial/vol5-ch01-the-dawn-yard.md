# The Dawn Yard (1888)

> **1888**
>
> *"A weapon that walks about all day must have better manners than a knife that stays in a drawer."*
>
> — Wei Zhang, the yard behind the chandlery, Limehouse

***

The yard behind the chandlery is six paces by four, and Su Zhang has known every stone of it in the dark since she was seven years old.

It is a summer dawn, and the dark is only just going grey over the wall, and the air already carries the whole inventory of the river: tar and rope-oil from the shop at her back, mud and low water from the Causeway beyond the wall, coal smoke starting up from a thousand grates as Limehouse wakes to boil its first kettles. The wash-copper is not yet lit. That is the one certain mark of the hour — before the copper, the yard is the yard; after it, the yard is the laundry, and the day has the family and not the family the day.

She stands in the horse.

*Sei ping ma*, her grandfather called it, in a city she has never seen; the four-level stance, the one you sink into until the thighs burn and then keep sinking, until the burn is a fact you live beside rather than a thing that is happening to you. Her father set her in it the first morning, both his hands pressing her knees out over her feet, and told her she would stand in it every day of her life and that most days it would teach her nothing, and that she was to stand in it anyway, because the days it taught her nothing were the rent she paid on the day it would teach her everything. She was seven. She did not understand a word of it and did the thing exactly, which he told her, much later, was itself the first lesson and the hardest, and the only one he had ever worried she might not have in her.

She has it in her. She stands in the horse while the light comes up, weight down through the centre of each foot, spine stacked, breath going low into the belly the way he taught her before she could have said what a belly was for, and she does not shake. The shaking stopped years ago. What is left, on a good morning, is a stillness with a great deal of readiness folded up inside it, like a spring lying flat.

Her father comes out of the shop with two cups of tea and the fan.

Wei Zhang is a small man who has spent forty years being underestimated by larger ones, which he considers a professional advantage and has never once corrected. He sets one cup on the step for her, for after, and keeps the other, and stands sipping it and watching her hold the stance the way another father might watch a daughter at her letters — with attention, and without praise, because praise, in the yard, is a thing you spend once and cannot get back, and he is saving his.

The fan he keeps in his hand. It is a plain folded thing of dark wood, worn to the colour and the smoothness of an old knuckle, older than her father and older than the grandfather who carried it out of Canton in a bundle with a white bowl. It is not a weapon and it is not a toy. It is the yard's one instrument of correction and, when the yard has done a man harm, its one instrument of repair, because on their street in Canton the man who taught the art was also the man who set the bones — *dit da*, her father says, the hitting and the mending kept in the same two hands, because a person who has learned to break a wrist has learned the one thing needful to put it back, and ought to be made to do both, so that he never forgets what the first thing costs.

Bridge, her father says.

Su brings her arms up out of the stance into *kiu sau*, the bridge-hands, forearms forward and rooted, and her father steps in and lays his own forearm across hers, and they begin.

This is the part the sailors' stories never get, because the sailors only ever see the end of it, in a lane, in the half-second when it stops looking like anything at all and starts looking like a man sitting down against a wall who did not decide to sit. What the stories never see is this: two people standing almost still in a grey yard, arms crossed and touching, rolling slowly, each reading the other through the skin of the forearm. *Chi sau*, in her grandfather's tongue; the sticking hands. Her father translates it, when he translates it at all, as *listening*, and makes her give it back to him in English, because a thing you can only say in one language is a thing you only half know, and he did not carry this across the world so that his daughter would half know it.

She listens.

Through two crossed wrists in the dark she can feel where his weight is before he has finished deciding to put it there. She can feel the small forward lean that means a push is coming and the smaller settling-back that is the push's twin and its lie, the feint that lives inside every true thing. Wing Chun keeps to the centre and gives nothing away from it; Hung Ga plants like a tree and dares the weather; her family, out at the poor thin end of both lineages, kept what a laundry family could use of each, and what it could use turned out to be everything that costs nothing but years. The stances came from the one. The listening came from the other. The rope and the sheets, though it took her the longest time to understand this, came from the shop, and did as much as either.

Her father tests her with a push she should not be able to feel the front of, and she feels it, and is not there for it, folding with it and stepping into the place his balance has just left, and the point of her elbow arrives, gently, exactly under his floating rib, where a hand that meant it could stop a larger man's argument before he had finished forming it.

Good, her father says.

He does not say it often. He says it now, and Su files it the way she files everything, in the interior tally that has no name in either of her languages, the running book she has kept since before she can remember, in a script only she can read. Her mother's God keeps books; her father's art keeps rules; and somewhere in the narrow yard between the two of them they made a third thing in their daughter, an account that must balance whatever the balancing costs, and this morning it has taken in one word of her father's, worth more than its weight, and entered it, and gone on.

The fan raps her trailing elbow a half-inch higher without a word being spent.

She lifts it. He nods. They roll again.

While they roll he recites, because the art comes with its rules the way the tide comes with the river, and a form done without the rules is only exercise, which is to say only vanity with sweat on it. He says them in Cantonese and waits, and Su gives them back in English into the grey air, and this is as close as the two of them come, most mornings, to conversation.

Never first.

Never for show.

Never for payment.

Never in anger.

The practice stops things, he says — *the practice*, in the same flat voice he uses for the shop's accounts, and it took her years to understand that the flat voice was not modesty and was not concealment; it was accuracy. *Gong fu* is only skill and the time you spend to get it. A man who has spent thirty years learning to cook rice has *gong fu*. What she has is *gong fu*, and it happens to be the kind that can put a man on the ground, and her father will not dress that up in a bigger word, because a bigger word would be the first step onto the road that ends with rule two. The practice stops things, he says. It does not answer them. You are not learning to win a fight. You are learning to end one that has already started, in the fewest moves that will end it, and then — and this is the part he leans on, every time, the part he considers the whole of it — to live correctly, afterward, with having ended it.

Whoever holds the art, he says, carries a weapon everywhere, all her life, in her own body. His father's words, worn smooth by two crossings of the world. And a weapon that walks about all day must have better manners than a knife that stays in a drawer.

The light is up over the wall now. Somewhere down the Causeway a door goes, and a bucket rings on a coping, and the first cart of the morning starts its iron complaint over the cobbles toward the dock. The yard has perhaps a quarter of an hour left of being only the yard.

Her father steps back and lowers his arms, and the roll is over, and he picks up his tea again as though it has been waiting, which it has.

Su comes up out of the stance at last. The blood goes back into her legs like a tide turning, that particular ache she has had every morning of her life and would not know how to live without. She takes up her own cup from the step. The tea has gone to the temperature the whole ritual is secretly built around, hot enough to comfort, cool enough to drink straight down, and she drinks it straight down, standing, the way her father does, the way his father did on a hillside above a harbour she will never see.

On the high shelf inside the shop window, wrapped in its cloth, stands the white bowl.

She can just see the shape of it from the yard through the open shop door: a plain rice bowl, not fine, not Chinese-fine the way the missionaries' cups are English-fine, only a working bowl of the sort a tea-stall keeps a dozen of. It has stood on that shelf her whole life. It is never used and never sold and never washed by anyone but him, and when she was small she asked why he did not simply send it back to Canton — there are ships for Hong Kong every week from the dock at the end of their own street — and her father gave the question the same grave consideration he gives a column of figures, and said, *Because she asked me to bring it. Not to send it.* And that was the whole answer, and Su was most of the way grown before she understood that the bowl was a debt he was not permitted to pay, kept on a shelf where he would have to see it every day and go on not paying it, forever, on purpose; and that this, too, was a rule, older than the four, the one they all come out of: that some accounts are to be carried at a loss, kept open, never closed, because closing them would be the theft and not the keeping.

Her mother would call that mercy. Her mother, who was dipped in the river's cold water at the mission church and keeps a God who runs His books at a loss on principle, would say the bowl is the nearest thing her husband owns to a prayer, and her husband, who keeps no God and every rule, would say nothing at all, and go on dusting it.

Su is nineteen years old this summer. She lives in the yard between the two of them and is made of pieces of each, and she has not yet had the morning that will ask her which of them she is — the mother who keeps the loss open, or the father who keeps the rule that stops things — because it is summer, and the river is only the river, and the worst thing in the newspaper on the shop counter is a debate about the drains.

She rinses the two cups at the yard butt and goes in to light the copper.

Her mother is already at the counter, sleeves back, hair pinned, doing the thing she does every morning of the world, which is to turn a pile of other people's dirt into a pile of other people's whiteness and take four farthings for it, warm from being held the whole walk over. Sau-Ling Zhang was sent for across the world the year the shop could feed two, and has been poor in two countries, and holds, on the strength of it, opinions about who deserves clean linen that come down, when you boil them, to *everyone*. She does not look up. She says, in Cantonese, that the sailors' shirts from the boarding house have to go back today whatever else does not, because the man pays on the last Monday and only then, and a shop that lets the last Monday slide once will find the man has taught himself it slides always.

Su says, in Cantonese, that she will take them.

The copper catches, the kindling first and then the coal, and the cold water above it begins its long climb toward the boil, and the smell of the day starts up — lye and blue-bag and hot iron, the smell that gets into the family's own clothes and stays there, so that Su has never once in her life smelt of anything but the work, and has stopped, years ago, being able to smell it on herself at all.

Out on the Causeway the carts thicken. The river goes about its enormous business a wall away, moving the freight of an empire past every door on the street as it has done since before the shop or the family or the wall, carrying cargo it will never trouble to name and, folded invisibly in among it, the consequences of things done and decided half a world and two generations off, riding quiet under the manifest, coming home.

The bowl stands on the shelf.

The fan goes back in the drawer.

And Su Zhang, who can stand in a dark yard with her eyes shut and tell you through two crossed wrists which foot a man's weight is on, ties an apron over the strength nobody who buys their laundry will ever guess is there, and opens the shop, on the last ordinary summer the century will let her have.
